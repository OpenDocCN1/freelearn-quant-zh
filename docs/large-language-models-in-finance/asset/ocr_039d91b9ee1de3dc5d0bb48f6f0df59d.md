Realizing this vision in practice, however, requires solving several hard problems. Computational demands remain substantial, particularly when combining test-time reasoning (e.g., Tree of Thoughts), large multimodal encoders, and high-capacity time series models in a single workflow. While architecture and inference optimizations continue to reduce cost and latency, most institutions will need to adopt a layered approach: reserving the most expensive capabilities for a small set of high-impact use cases, and relying on lighter models elsewhere.

Data quality and coverage also vary dramatically across markets, asset classes, and institutions. Deep time series models and multimodal systems are only as reliable as the data they see. Emerging markets, private assets, and niche products often exhibit sparse, noisy, or shifting data regimes, limiting the benefits of sophisticated architectures. For many applications, careful feature engineering and robust baselines remain competitive with, or superior to, the most advanced models.

Regulatory and supervisory expectations are no longer a blank slate, but they remain layered, use-case dependent, and jurisdiction specific. The European Union’s AI Act applies a risk-based structure whose obligations depend on the system’s classification and role in the value chain [European Parliament and Council of the European Union, 2024].

In the United States, SR 26-2 superseded SR 11-7 and SR 21-8, but its relevance to this chapter is deliberately limited. The guidance is non-binding, is expected to be most relevant to banking organizations with more than $30 billion in total assets, narrows the model definition by excluding simple-arithmetic tools and deterministic rule-based software, and in footnote 3 expressly excludes generative-AI and agentic-AI models. It continues to apply to traditional statistical and quantitative models and to non-generative, non-agentic AI models that meet the revised definition [Board of Governors of the Federal Reserve System et al., 2026]. Advanced reasoning LLMs, multimodal generators, and agentic systems should therefore be governed through the institution’s own proportionate controls and the laws and supervisory frameworks that actually apply, not described as SR 26-2-compliant. Where an out-of-scope LLM feeds an in-scope model, the interface remains a material validation dependency that should be inventoried and tested.

The NIST AI Risk Management Framework and its Generative AI Profile offer voluntary lifecycle practices organized around governing, mapping, measuring, and managing risk [National Institute of Standards and Technology, 2023, 2024]. For capital markets, IOSCO’s 2026 supervisory toolkit adds practical questions concerning use-case design, model and data risk, testing, monitoring, and third-party dependencies [International Organization of Securities Commissions, 2026].

Financial institutions should therefore map each application to the laws and supervisory guidance that apply to its jurisdiction, function, materiality, and affected clients. Advanced reasoning, multimodality, and time-series LLMs should remain governed extensions to the modeling stack, with documented intended use, formal scope analysis, access controls, performance thresholds, incident procedures, human oversight, dependency testing, and a tested fallback process.
