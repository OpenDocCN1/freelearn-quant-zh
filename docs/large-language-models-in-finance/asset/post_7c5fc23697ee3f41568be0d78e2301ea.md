### 1.9.3 Custom fine-tuned models

Most institutions do not train models like BloombergGPT from scratch. A prevalent approach is to adapt a capable general-purpose model, but the available adaptation route depends on what the provider actually exposes, and the four are not interchangeable. **Open-weight adaptation** (Llama, Mistral, Qwen) permits full fine-tuning, LoRA, and quantized training on weights the institution holds. **Provider-managed fine-tuning** is offered by some closed APIs for those models, on the provider’s infrastructure and subject to their terms. **Provider-managed fine-tuning via a cloud partner** is a distinct route that is easy to miss: a model may have no self-service fine-tuning on its own public API while managed fine-tuning is nonetheless available through a cloud arrangement — Anthropic’s direct API does not currently expose ordinary self-service fine-tuning, but managed fine-tuning has been offered through platforms such as Amazon Bedrock. **Prompt, retrieval, and tool adaptation** is the route that always exists, and it is more capable than its reputation suggests. **Private enterprise arrangements** exist but are bespoke and cannot be assumed. Naming a model family is therefore not the same as naming an adaptation strategy, and a design that assumes fine-tuning availability without checking it will fail at procurement, not at training.

Custom fine-tuned models occupy a pragmatic middle ground. They offer significantly better performance on in-house financial tasks than off-the-shelf models, while avoiding the data requirements and operational complexity of training a fully domain-specific model. For banks, asset managers, and fintech firms, this approach provides a practical path to regulatory alignment.

In practice, custom fine-tuning allows organizations to encode proprietary knowledge, internal terminology, and task-specific reasoning patterns directly into the model, making it more effective for several financial use cases such as risk assessment, compliance automation, research summarization, and decision support.

Adapting general LLMs for finance typically involves two main strategies:

- **Fine-tuning**: Training a pre-trained LLM further on a specialized dataset (e.g., financial news, earnings transcripts, proprietary research). If $D_{finance}$ is the domain-specific corpus, the fine-tuning objective minimizes a task-specific loss:

$$\mathcal{L}(\theta) = \frac{1}{|D_{finance}|} \sum_{(x,y) \in D_{finance}} \ell(f_\theta(x), y)$$ (1.19)

where $\ell$ is the loss function (e.g., cross-entropy for classification/generation) and $f_\theta$ represents the model parameterized by $\theta$.

- **Continued pre-training**: Further pre-training a general LLM on a large corpus of domain-specific text before fine-tuning. This helps the model better adapt its vocabulary and internal representations to the nuances of financial language.

Domain-specific tokenization and pre-processing techniques can also significantly enhance performance [Devlin et al., 2019], [Raffel et al., 2020].

So far, we've examined how LLMs evolved and how they're deployed in financial applications. But understanding the architecture alone isn't enough. What truly matters is how these models *think*. Financial reasoning pushes LLMs beyond linguistic fluency into the realm of logic, mathematics, and decision-making. In this next section, we'll look at how modern models handle numerical data and interpret structured finance documents.

## 1.10 Financial reasoning in LLMs

Financial reasoning is where language models are most tested. In finance, it's not enough for a model to generate fluent text; it must make sense of what the numbers are saying, how they connect to the narrative, and whether those statements hold up under accounting and regulatory logic. Unlike general NLP tasks, financial work depends on precision and internal consistency: balance sheets must balance, and pricing models must respect the rules of no-arbitrage.

This section looks at how today's LLMs manage that kind of disciplined reasoning. We'll examine their ability to work with numerical data, interpret structured documents such as filings and tables, support decisions under uncertainty, and remain reliable in the face of bias and model drift.

Before we can understand how LLMs reason about financial documents, we need to look at their relationship with numbers themselves. Numerical precision is where language models first show their limits, and where many of the challenges in financial reasoning begin.

### 1.10.1 Handling numerical data

Numerical reasoning remains a structural weakness of large language models. Standard LLMs process numbers primarily as sequences of tokens rather than as quantities endowed with algebraic structure. As a result, values such as “1000” or “3.5%” are not internally represented as scalars subject to arithmetic constraints, but as symbolic strings whose quantitative meaning is implicit rather than explicit. While this abstraction is largely benign in general language tasks, it becomes critical in finance, where precision, consistency, and reconciliation are non-negotiable.

Recent research has proposed several strategies to mitigate this limitation. Three directions have emerged as particularly influential. First, **numeracy-aware representations and tokenization schemes** aim to reduce fragmentation of numerical values and improve magnitude awareness during training. Approaches such as arithmetic-focused pretraining objectives and numeral-aware embeddings have been shown to improve basic numerical competence without altering model architecture [Petrak et al., 2022, Jin et al., 2021].

Second, **external tool augmentation** reframes the LLM as a controller rather than a calculator. In this paradigm, the model learns to invoke symbolic environments—such as Python interpreters or spreadsheet engines—to perform exact computations, while retaining responsibility for task decomposition and interpretation. Methods such as Toolformer, Program-Aided Language Models (PAL), and Program-of-Thoughts prompting demonstrate substantial gains on numerical reasoning benchmarks by separating linguistic reasoning from arithmetic execution [Schick et al., 2023, Gao et al., 2023b, Chen et al., 2023].

Third, **neuro-symbolic and program-supervised approaches** explicitly integrate symbolic constraints into financial reasoning tasks. Datasets such as FinQA, ConvFinQA, and TAT-QA frame financial question answering as a sequence of executable operations over extracted quantities, enabling deterministic validation of intermediate results [Chen et al., 2021, 2022b, Zhu et al., 2021]. These approaches are particularly well aligned with finance, where balance identities and accounting relations impose hard structural constraints.

Despite this progress, robust numerical reasoning remains difficult. Models can perform strongly on curated financial text-classification benchmarks while remaining materially weaker on quantitative question-answering tasks such as FinQA and TAT-QA, particularly when answers require several linked operations [Chen et al., 2021, Zhu et al., 2021]. The broader lesson is that linguistic fluency does not guarantee numerical coherence. Reliable systems therefore separate language understanding from exact computation and numerical validation.

A simple financial example illustrates the issue. Consider quarterly earnings with reported revenue \(R_t\) and expenses \(C_t\). A reliable financial reasoning system should be able to:

- 1. Extract values for \(R_t\) and \(C_t\) from tables or narrative disclosures using domain-adapted OCR and NLP.

- 2. Compute net income via \(N_t = R_t - C_t\). (1.20)

- 3. Validate reported earnings per share (EPS) by reconciling \(N_t\) with the number of shares outstanding.
