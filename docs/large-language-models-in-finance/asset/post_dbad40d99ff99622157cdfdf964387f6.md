
## 11.10 Selected standards, regulatory sources, and research resources

The following annotated reference map covers the principal resources used in this chapter and gives practitioners a structured starting point for implementation. It complements the book's full bibliography.

- **U.S. financial model-risk management (scope-limited).** SR 26-2, Revised Guidance on Model Risk Management, supersedes SR 11-7 and SR 21-8. It is non-binding, is expected to be most relevant above $30 billion in total assets, and expressly excludes generative-AI and agentic-AI models while continuing to apply to traditional quantitative and non-generative, non-agentic AI models that meet its definition. It is therefore not a generative-AI compliance standard; use it directly only after formal scope analysis [Board of Governors of the Federal Reserve System et al., 2026].

- **Cross-sector AI risk management.** NIST's AI RMF organizes work around Govern, Map, Measure, and Manage, while the Generative AI Profile adds risks and actions specific to generative systems [National Institute of Standards and Technology, 2023, 2024].

- **Financial-stability perspective.** The FSB's 2024 report analyzes AI-related vulnerabilities, and its 2026 consultation report proposes sound practices for organization-wide governance and the AI lifecycle in financial institutions [Financial Stability Board, 2024, 2026].

- **European Union.** Regulation (EU) 2024/1689 provides the AI Act's risk-based obligations, including the specific Annex III financial and insurance uses that may be classified as high-risk [European Parliament and Council of the European Union, 2024].

- **United Kingdom financial services.** The FCA's current approach remains outcomes-focused, principles-based, and technology-neutral, applying existing regulatory frameworks to AI-enabled financial services [Financial Conduct Authority, 2025].

- **AI-washing and truthful claims.** The SEC's 2024 Delphia and Global Predictions actions provide a documented financial-services example of enforcement involving false or misleading AI-related representations [U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission, 2024].

- **Experimental financial markets with LLM agents.** Henning et al., Lopez-Lira, Ouyang and Sui, and Anand et al. provide complementary controlled evidence on behavioral divergence, prompt-conditioned strategies, market bubbles, and system-design effects [Henning et al., 2025, Lopez-Lira, 2025, Ouyang and Sui, 2026, Anand et al., 2026].

- **Algorithmic interaction and coordination.** Calvano et al. provide a foundational controlled study of tacitly collusive behavior among autonomous pricing algorithms; the result is not LLM-specific but is relevant to common-mode and interaction risk [Calvano et al., 2020].

- **Privacy-preserving learning.** Differential privacy, federated learning, secure aggregation, and secure multi-party computation are covered by Dwork and Roth, Kairouz et al., Bonawitz et al., and Evans et al. [Dwork and Roth, 2014, Kairouz et al., 2021, Bonawitz et al., 2017, Evans et al., 2018].

- **Adversarial machine learning.** NIST's adversarial machine-learning taxonomy provides terminology for evasion, poisoning, privacy, and misuse risks that can be incorporated into security testing [Vassilev et al., 2025].

## 12 Mathematical Innovations and Empirical Evidence

This chapter connects the architectural and methodological tools developed in previous chapters with concrete applications and empirical findings from the emerging literature on large language models (LLMs) in finance. The previous chapters introduced core LLM technology, fine-tuning, retrieval-augmented generation, agentic workflows, and reinforcement-learning concepts. Here we ask how those ingredients are actually combined in financial systems: which models are trained on financial text, how memory and multi-agent structures are used in trading and research workflows, and what evidence is currently available for risk, fraud, credit, and benchmark tasks.

The chapter therefore has a different emphasis from a pure model-development or benchmarking chapter. Its purpose is not to rank every available model, nor to present new proprietary experiments. Instead, it offers a structured review of reported evidence and translates recent systems into a common mathematical language. This is essential in finance because model performance is rarely a single-number property. The same architecture may behave differently across asset classes, regimes, document types, regulatory constraints, and operational workflows. A useful financial LLM must therefore be understood simultaneously as a statistical model, a software component, and a governed decision-support system.

We focus on four main strands:

- Domain-specialized foundational models for financial text;
- Memory-augmented and multi-agent trading systems;
- Applications in risk assessment, fraud detection, and credit modeling; and
- Evaluation frameworks and emerging application domains.

Rather than presenting new proprietary experiments, we synthesize and formalize patterns reported in recent work, and discuss how they instantiate the design principles of Chapters 2, 3, 4, 5, and 9.

**Reader note.** The chapter uses the acronym LLM throughout to keep headings and the table of contents compact. Equations are written as stylized abstractions: they are intended to clarify the statistical or decision-theoretic structure of the systems, not to reproduce any one implementation line by line. Code blocks are included only as implementation sketches; in a production manuscript or companion repository they should be referenced by the surrounding section or by the corresponding repository file, rather than treated as numbered figures or tables.

**Disclaimer.** Unless explicitly attributed to a specific published study, numerical results in this chapter are illustrative and designed to convey _qualitative_ patterns reported in the literature. They should not be interpreted as audited backtests or investment recommendations. Any real-world deployment must follow the governance and validation principles of Chapters 11 and 9.

### 12.1 Foundational financial language models

We begin with models whose architecture and training corpora are explicitly tailored to financial text. These models embody the domain-specialization strategy advocated in Chapter 3: starting from generic architectures and progressively aligning them with financial data. The section reviews four representative patterns. BloombergGPT illustrates large-scale financial pre-training; FinBERT illustrates domain adaptation of an encoder model; FinEAS illustrates supervised sentence-embedding adaptation for financial sentiment; and FinGPT illustrates a systems-oriented, parameter-efficient approach built around data pipelines and adapters. Table 12.1 at the end of the section summarizes these differences.

#### 12.1.1 BloombergGPT: Industry-scale financial specialization

At a basic level, BloombergGPT is an industry-scale financial LLM: a roughly 50-billion-parameter, decoder-only transformer trained on a large mixture of general text and financial data [Wu et al., 2023b]. Its purpose is not only to answer general-language prompts, but also to handle financial language such as filings, market news, entity names, earnings-call language, and research-style questions. It therefore provides a useful first example of how financial domain specialization changes both the data distribution and the training objective.

Let $\mathcal{D}_{\text{gen}}$ and $\mathcal{D}_{\text{fin}}$ denote general and financial corpora with token counts $N_{\text{gen}}$ and $N_{\text{fin}}$, respectively. A token is a discrete unit of text used by the model, and a sequence $x_{1:T} = (x_1, \dots, x_T)$ contains $T$ such tokens. For a GPT-style autoregressive model, causal language modeling (CLM) means training the model to predict the next token from the previous tokens. Training can therefore be represented by the objective

$$
\mathcal{L}_{\text{CLM}}(\theta) = -\mathbb{E}_{(x_{1:T}, d) \sim \mathcal{D}} \sum_{t=1}^T \log p_\theta(x_t \mid x_{<t}, d), \quad (12.1)
$$

where $\theta$ denotes model parameters, $x_{<t} = (x_1, \dots, x_{t-1})$ is the prefix available at prediction time, and $d \in \{\text{gen}, \text{fin}\}$ indicates whether a sample comes from the general or financial
