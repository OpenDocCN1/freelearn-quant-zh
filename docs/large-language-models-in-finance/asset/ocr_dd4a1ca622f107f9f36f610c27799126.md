with a parameter budget far below the base model: for an adapted \(d_{out} \times d_{in}\) matrix, \(r(d_{in} + d_{out}) \ll d_{in}d_{out}\). The saving is one of _rank_, not sparsity: the factors themselves are dense. When \(\Delta\) is parameterized as \(\Delta W = BA\) with \(B \in \mathbb{R}^{d_{out} \times r}\), \(A \in \mathbb{R}^{r \times d_{in}}\) and \(r \ll \min(d_{in}, d_{out})\), the product has shape \(d_{out} \times d_{in}\) — the shape of _W_ itself, as it must — and we obtain a low-rank LoRA-style adapter.

A second layer of efficiency comes from quantization (QLoRA): the base parameters \(\phi\) are stored in low precision (e.g. 4-bit) while the adapters remain in higher precision. The combination allows full forward precision in critical paths at significantly reduced memory footprint.

Empirical reports highlight that:

+   - Substantial performance gains on financial tasks are possible with only millions of trainable parameters;
+   - Training and inference costs remain compatible with on-premise infrastructure; and
+   - Performance is heavily contingent on data quality and curation, underscoring the importance of the data layer, covered in Chapter 2.

The four model families illustrate a common design choice. One can spend capacity by training a financial model from scratch, adapt a general encoder to financial language, specialize sentence embeddings for financial sentiment, or use parameter-efficient adapters on top of a flexible base model.
