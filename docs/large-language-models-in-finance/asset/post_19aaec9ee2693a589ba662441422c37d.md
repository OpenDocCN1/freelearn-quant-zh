
## Ethics, Governance, and Compliance: A Comprehensive Framework for Financial LLMs

Financial large language models can widen access to analysis, accelerate research and compliance work, improve customer service, and support more consistent decision processes. These benefits are economically meaningful only when the systems are reliable, contestable, secure, and governed in proportion to the consequences of their use. In finance, a plausible but incorrect answer can become a recommendation, an order, a credit decision, a disclosure, or a control failure; governance therefore determines whether technical capability becomes durable value or unmanaged risk.

This chapter develops an integrated risk framework for the ethics, governance, and compliance of financial LLMs and agentic systems. It combines scope-limited U.S. model-risk guidance, cross-sector AI risk management, financial-stability concerns, and emerging evidence from controlled market experiments [Board of Governors of the Federal Reserve System et al., 2026, National Institute of Standards and Technology, 2023, 2024, Financial Stability Board, 2024, 2026]. The framework is organized around three substantive pillars and a four-layer implementation architecture. Documented regulatory events are clearly separated from controlled experiments, hypothetical stress tests, and composite implementation vignettes so that illustrative material is not mistaken for historical fact.

In this chapter, we will cover the following topics:

- Why comprehensive governance is essential for beneficial financial AI
- Theoretical foundations and a three-pillar governance model
- What controlled market experiments do—and do not—establish
- A four-layer implementation architecture from infrastructure to accountability
- Documented, hypothetical, and composite governance cases
- Decision-linked metrics and continuous improvement
- Future challenges, open questions, and practical reference resources

### 11.1 The imperative for comprehensive governance

The central purpose of governance is not to prevent the use of LLMs. It is to make valuable uses repeatable while keeping failure within tolerable bounds. Financial institutions already operate under demanding obligations concerning safety and soundness, market integrity, consumer protection, privacy, operational resilience, and senior-management accountability. LLMs do not displace those obligations; they introduce new pathways through which the obligations can be breached, including unsupported generation, prompt-induced behavior, untraceable data use, tool misuse, common-mode responses, and excessive reliance on third-party models.

LLMs also differ from many conventional financial models. Their outputs are generated from learned statistical representations rather than a compact set of explicit equations, and their behavior can depend materially on prompts, retrieval context, tools, memory, decoding settings, and model version. A production “model” is therefore better understood as a socio-technical system that includes the base model, data and retrieval pipeline, prompts, tools, interfaces, users, controls, and organizational incentives. Validation of the base model alone is not validation of the deployed system.

The governance challenge is consequently broader than accuracy. A system can be accurate on average and still be unacceptable because its rare errors are severe, its explanations are misleading, its behavior changes after an update, its data lineage is incomplete, or its actions become correlated with other systems during stress. Conversely, an LLM need not be perfect to be useful when it is restricted to an appropriate role, surrounded by verification, and prevented from taking irreversible actions without additional controls.

Existing frameworks provide important foundations but must be combined with careful scope analysis. In the United States, SR 26-2 superseded SR 11-7 and SR 21-8. It is non-binding supervisory guidance, is expected to be most relevant to banking organizations with more than $30 billion in total assets, and narrows the model definition by excluding simple-arithmetic tools and deterministic rule-based software. Most importantly for this chapter, footnote 3 explicitly excludes generative-AI and agentic-AI models from its scope while preserving its application to traditional statistical and quantitative models and to non-generative, non-agentic AI models [Board of Governors of the Federal Reserve System et al., 2026]. The chapter therefore invokes SR 26-2 directly only for systems that fall within its formal perimeter and uses selected principles by analogy for LLM governance only when that analogy is stated. An institution’s own governance and risk-management practices, together with applicable sectoral law and supervisory requirements, remain the control basis for generative and agentic systems outside the guidance.

A mixed architecture can still create a validation dependency: when an out-of-scope LLM supplies data, features, narratives, or decisions to an in-scope quantitative model, the downstream review should examine that interface and record it in the system inventory. This is an architectural implication, not an expansion of SR 26-2’s formal scope. The NIST AI Risk Management Framework and its Generative AI Profile add lifecycle-oriented treatment of validity, reliability, security, transparency, privacy, and harmful-content risks [National Institute of Standards and Technology, 2023, 2024]. The Financial Stability Board identifies third-party concentration, market correlations, cyber risk, and weaknesses in model risk, data quality, and governance as potential channels of financial vulnerability [Financial Stability Board, 2024, 2026]. The FAIR framework contributes a finance-specific structure for integrating these concerns [Noguer i Alonso and Mendell, 2025a,b].

The resulting objective is proportionate governance: controls should intensify with autonomy, scale, speed, irreversibility, customer impact, and systemic relevance. A research assistant that drafts a memo does not require the same controls as an agent that can submit orders, alter a portfolio, approve credit, or communicate directly with customers. The framework below therefore begins with materiality and intended use rather than with the model label alone.

### 11.2 Theoretical foundations: Building a multi-dimensional framework

A coherent governance framework must connect technical properties to financial consequences and then to accountable decisions. This section first explains why several professional disciplines are required and then organizes their contributions into three mutually dependent pillars. The purpose is not to create another abstract checklist, but to provide a translation mechanism from model behavior to operational controls, risk limits, and legal responsibilities.

#### 11.2.1 The convergence of disciplines

Effective governance of financial LLMs requires structured interdisciplinary collaboration. Computer scientists and machine-learning engineers contribute knowledge of architectures, training dynamics, evaluation, security, and technical limitations. Quantitative researchers and financial engineers contribute market microstructure, pricing, execution, portfolio, liquidity, and systemic-risk expertise. Financial analysts and portfolio managers provide domain judgment about information quality and economic relevance. Behavioral economists examine decision biases and interaction effects. Legal, compliance, privacy, and ethics specialists interpret obligations concerning fairness, disclosure, accountability, customer outcomes, and redress.

The challenge is not merely to place these specialists in the same committee. Institutions need a shared language that allows a technical observation to become a risk decision. For example, sensitivity to a prompt template must be translated into a measurable exposure; the exposure must be assigned an owner and threshold; and the threshold must trigger a defined action such as routing to human review, reducing a limit, reverting a model version, or suspending a tool.

We refer to this translation process as cognitive bridging. It consists of documented interfaces between disciplines: model cards and system inventories for technical-to-risk translation; risk taxonomies and scenario libraries for risk-to-business translation; and control mappings, accountability matrices, and evidence packs for business-to-regulatory translation. The value of cognitive bridging is practical: it prevents a vulnerability from remaining a technical observation that never reaches the people authorized to change production behavior.
