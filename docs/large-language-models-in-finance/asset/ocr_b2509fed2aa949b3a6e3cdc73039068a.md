tail exposure. In a derivatives application, rollouts simulate the payoff distribution under volatility, jump, and liquidity scenarios; backpropagation then updates both mean utility and downside-risk estimates. A path is promising when its upper-confidence score is high, not merely when its historical average payoff is high. This exploration–exploitation mechanism prevents the reasoning process from becoming a random walk and directs compute toward branches that are valuable, uncertain, and still feasible [Kocsis and Szepesvári, 2006, Browne et al., 2012].

Having explored methods for enhancing reasoning quality, we now turn to the role of inference-time computation in improving model performance.

# ## 13.1.4 Scaling test-time compute for financial intelligence

The strategic allocation of computational resources during inference emerges as a critical consideration for financial AI systems [Snell et al., 2024]. Rather than uniformly applying the same analytical depth to all problems, adaptive systems allocate resources based on decision importance, complexity, and time constraints. This principle challenges the conventional focus on model size, suggesting that smaller models with sophisticated inference procedures can outperform larger models using simple forward passes.

Financial institutions face a strategic choice between investing in larger foundation models and developing inference architectures that extract more value from smaller models. Neither option dominates universally. On some tasks, structured search, self-refinement, or additional test-time computation can allow a smaller model to outperform a larger model using a single forward pass; on other tasks, the extra latency and complexity provide little benefit. The trade-off should be established through task-level evaluation.

| Task type | Complexity | Compute scaling benefit |
|---|---|---|
| Daily risk reports | Low | Minimal |
| Credit decisions | Medium | Moderate |
| M&A valuations | High | Significant |
| Fraud detection | High | Significant |
| Portfolio optimization | Very high | Substantial |

Table 13.1: Illustrative cost-benefit considerations for test-time compute scaling

The return on test-time compute is likely to vary across financial applications. Table 13.1 should therefore be read as an engineering hypothesis rather than a universal empirical ranking: routine report generation may need little deliberation, whereas complex valuation or structuring tasks may justify deeper search. Each tier requires a measured quality-latency-cost curve and a clear escalation policy.

As financial decision-making relies on diverse forms of information, the next frontier lies in enabling models to reason across multiple modalities.
