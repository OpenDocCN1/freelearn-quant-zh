## 1.9 Understanding model types

We must now take a closer look at how these models are categorized in practice. Not all LLMs serve the same purpose as some are broad, general-purpose systems built for flexibility, while others are carefully adapted or trained from scratch for finance. Understanding these distinctions helps practitioners decide which type of model to adopt or customize for a given financial use case.

To guide model selection and provide a decision flow, therefore, we categorize LLMs in the following types:

- **Domain-specific models**: Adapted to financial texts to improve handling of industry terminology and task conventions.
- **General-purpose models**: Broad corpora, best for open-domain tasks.
- **Custom fine-tuned models**: Tailored to proprietary datasets, balancing performance and control.

Understanding different model types is crucial for financial practitioners because the choice between general-purpose and domain-specific models significantly impacts both performance and cost. General-purpose models offer broad capabilities and easy deployment but may lack the specialized financial vocabulary and reasoning patterns needed for complex tasks. Domain-specific models can improve performance on matched financial tasks, but they require additional data, validation, and maintenance. This section will help you navigate these trade-offs and make informed decisions about which approach best suits your specific use case, budget, and performance requirements.

### 1.9.1 Domain-specific models (BloombergGPT, FinBERT and FinEAS)

Domain-specific models are designed to close the gap between general language understanding and the highly specialized nature of financial discourse. Finance introduces challenges that general-purpose models often struggle with: dense jargon, domain-specific abbreviations, subtle sentiment cues, forward-looking statements, and a heavy reliance on context drawn from regulations, filings, and market conventions.

Models such as BloombergGPT, FinBERT, and FinEAS represent different points along the spectrum of financial specialization. Some are trained at massive scale on proprietary financial corpora, while others focus narrowly on excelling at specific tasks like sentiment analysis or semantic similarity. Together, they illustrate how targeted domain adaptation can significantly improve accuracy and interpretability in financial NLP tasks.

- **BloombergGPT**: Remains a significant example of a large-scale, finance-specific model, leveraging Bloomberg's extensive proprietary data [Wu et al., 2023b]. Its influence lies in demonstrating the potential of highly specialized pre-training.

- **FinBERT and similar models**: Models like FinBERT [Yang et al., 2020] and subsequent fine-tuned variants of newer architectures (e.g., fine-tuned Llama or Mistral for finance) continue to be valuable for specific tasks like sentiment analysis, named entity recognition, and classification within the financial domain.

- **FinEAS** (Financial Embedding Analysis of Sentiment): A specialized sentence-embedding architecture for financial sentiment analysis [Gutiérrez-Fandiño et al., 2021]. We explore this model in detail in the following subsection.

FinEAS focuses specifically on optimizing semantic representations for sentiment-sensitive tasks. This narrower but more deliberate design makes it a useful case study for understanding how architectural choices affect performance in financial NLP. Let's examine it with some more detail.

### FinEAS: Advanced financial sentiment analysis

FinEAS is a sentence-level financial sentiment model designed to capture the subtle evaluative cues characteristic of financial discourse, such as forward-looking optimism, hedging language, and risk framing. Rather than adapting a general-purpose language model post hoc, FinEAS is explicitly optimized for semantic representation in financial sentiment tasks.

Architecturally, FinEAS builds on Sentence-BERT [Reimers and Gurevych, 2019], which fine-tunes BERT so that its sentence-level embeddings—rather than its token-level ones—carry the useful signal. The contribution is deliberately architectural rather than exotic: the claim is that financial sentiment is a sentence-level task, that vanilla BERT's sentence embeddings are weak, and that starting from a model already tuned for sentence representation therefore beats domain-specific token-level pre-training.

The objective is correspondingly simple. A sentence $s$ is encoded to a 768-dimensional embedding, passed through a linear layer and a tanh activation—because the target is a continuous score bounded in $[-1, 1]$—and trained by least squares:

```
\(\hat{y}(s) = \tanh(w^\top e_\theta(s) + b) \in (-1, 1), \quad (1.17)\)

\(\mathcal{L}_{\text{FinEAS}}(\theta, w, b) = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^N (y_i - \hat{y}(s_i))^2, \quad (1.18)\)
```

where \(e_\theta\) is the Sentence-BERT encoder and \(y_i\) is the RavenPack EVENT_SENTIMENT_SCORE for headline \(s_i\), a continuous variable on \([-1, +1]\).

Two features of this are worth dwelling on, because both cut against the instinct of most practitioners. First, there is no contrastive loss, no triplet loss, and no temperature parameter.

It is ordinary supervised regression, and the model works because of the encoder it starts from, not the objective it optimizes. Second, modelling sentiment as a *continuous* quantity rather than a three-way class is itself the departure from FinBERT, and it is the more faithful representation of what a trading desk actually consumes: not the label *negative*, but a number that can be aggregated, decayed, and sized.

The reported evaluation uses a large RavenPack US news sample—not FiQA or the Financial PhraseBank—and reports mean squared error rather than accuracy or F1. With the backbone frozen, FinEAS attains MSE 0.0556 against 0.2124 for BERT and 0.2108 for a bidirectional LSTM; with full fine-tuning it edges FinBERT (0.0044 versus 0.0050 on the six-month sample) [Gutiérrez-Fandiño et al., 2021]. We return to this model in Chapter 13, and not to praise it: its evaluation protocol contains exactly the defect this book devotes a chapter to warning against, and the cost of that defect is measurable in the paper's own numbers.

### 1.9.2 General-purpose architectures powering financial AI

While domain-specific models like FinBERT, FinEAS, and BloombergGPT are tailored to financial data, most real-world applications in finance still rely on powerful general-purpose LLMs as their foundation. These models are developed by leading research labs and open-source communities and provide flexible architectures on which specialized financial systems are built.

Several major model families form the backbone of this ecosystem:

- **OpenAI's GPT series**: Successors to GPT-4 continue to be highly influential, offering strong performance across a wide range of text generation and understanding tasks. They are frequently used as base models for fine-tuning in financial contexts [Brown et al., 2020], [OpenAI, 2022].

- **Google's Gemini family**: The Gemini models, known for their native multimodality and reasoning capabilities, are increasingly explored for financial applications that require integrating text with charts, tables, and other data formats [Gemini Team, Google, 2023].

- **Meta's Llama series**: Llama 3 is a representative open-weight foundation-model family used as a base for specialized financial tools and research [AI @ Meta, 2024].

- **Anthropic's Claude series**: Models like Claude 3 are recognized for their large context windows, strong reasoning abilities, and focus on safety and reliability, making them suitable for analyzing lengthy financial reports and complex queries [Anthropic, 2024].

- **Mistral AI models**: Mistral continues to release powerful open models known for their efficiency and performance, often competing closely with proprietary counterparts. These are widely adopted and adapted within the financial sector [Mistral AI, 2023].

- **DeepSeek models**: Models from DeepSeek AI, especially those excelling in code generation, are valuable for quantitative finance, algorithmic trading strategy development, and financial data analysis automation.
