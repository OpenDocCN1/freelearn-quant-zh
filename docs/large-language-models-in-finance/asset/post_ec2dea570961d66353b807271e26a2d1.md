Reported backtests on small equity portfolios indicate that FinCon can achieve higher cumulative return and Sharpe ratio than Markowitz and deep RL baselines on the tested portfolios, while maintaining equal or lower drawdowns.

## FinAgent: Multimodal tool-augmented agent

FinAgent integrates multiple modalities, including:

- A price encoder \(\phi_{price}\) mapping historical price sequences to latent features;
- A news encoder \(\phi_{news}\) (often an LLM or FinBERT) producing text embeddings;
- A chart or image encoder \(\phi_{chart}\) capturing technical patterns.

A simple fusion architecture is:

```
z_{price} = \phi_{price}(p_{t-L:t}), \qquad (12.15)
z_{news} = \phi_{news}(\mathcal{N}_t), \qquad (12.16)
z_{chart} = \phi_{chart}(C_t), \qquad (12.17)
z_{fusion} = \psi(z_{price}, z_{news}, z_{chart}), \qquad (12.18)
```

where \(\psi\) is a learned fusion network (e.g. concatenation followed by MLPs and attention). The policy is then formulated:

```
a_t \sim \pi_\theta(a \mid z_{fusion}, tools_t),
```

with tool outputs (indicators, risk calculations) as additional inputs. Training is typically done via RL or imitation learning from expert demonstrations.

In reported experiments on selected equities and crypto-assets, FinAgent achieves higher annualized returns and Sharpe ratios than baseline models for the tested periods, with drawdowns that remain within risk-tolerant boundaries. Gains are largest where text and chart patterns interact strongly with price dynamics.

## 12.2.4 Backtesting caveats

Across FinMem, TradingAgents, FinCon, FinAgent and similar systems, a set of methodological caveats recur:

- **Limited scope:** Many studies focus on a handful of assets over one or two market regimes.
- **Simplified costs:** Transaction costs and market impact are often modeled crudely or omitted.
- **Short horizons:** Some reported Sharpe ratios are computed over short windows, making them sensitive to a few favourable periods.
- Data leakage risk: Careful separation of training, validation and test periods is essential but not always extensively documented.

These caveats reinforce the message of Chapter 9: multi-agent LLM systems are powerful tools for research and hypothesis generation, but their path to live deployment must pass through robust simulation, conservative risk controls, and human oversight.

## 12.3 Risk assessment, fraud detection, and credit modeling

We now turn to applications where the primary objective is not trading, but detection, classification, monitoring, and decision support in risk and compliance. These settings differ from the trading systems reviewed above in two ways. First, the output is often an alert, a risk score, a credit decision, or a case-management recommendation rather than a portfolio action.

Second, the cost of an error is not only financial: false positives burden operational teams, false negatives can create fraud or anti-money-laundering exposure, and opaque model rationales can create audit and fairness problems.

The section therefore separates three layers that are sometimes conflated in informal discussions. A supervised detector or credit model produces the primary risk signal; an LLM may enrich the input features or summarize unstructured material; and a separate LLM-assisted workflow may help analysts investigate, explain, or escalate a case. Keeping these layers distinct makes the architecture more auditable: the real-time detector can remain calibrated and independently validated, while the LLM layer is evaluated for evidence quality, rationale faithfulness, and analyst usability.

## 12.3.1 Graph-based fraud and AML detection

Graph neural networks (GNNs) and transformer-style architectures over transaction graphs have become strong baseline architectures for fraud and anti-money-laundering (AML) detection. The basic data object is a graph: nodes represent accounts, customers, merchants, wallets, or counterparties, while edges represent transactions, transfers, shared identifiers, or other relationships.

Before introducing the equations, Figure 12.2 shows the intended separation of responsibilities. The graph model produces the primary risk score or alert; the LLM layer supports feature enrichment, rationale drafting, and analyst interaction, but it should not be confused with the calibrated real-time detector.

Raw transaction graph plus KYC, e-mail, ticket, and case metadata
↓
Feature construction: node features $x_v$ and edge features $e_{uv}$
↓
Supervised GNN or graph-transformer detector
↓
Risk score, alert priority, or AML case flag
↓
LLM-assisted triage: evidence summary, rationale draft, and analyst questions

Figure 12.2: Schematic role separation in a graph-based fraud architecture.

The supervised graph model produces the primary alert or risk score, while the LLM layer supports feature enrichment, explanation, and analyst triage.

Let $G = (V, E)$ denote a transaction graph with node features $x_v$ and edge features $e_{uv}$. A generic graph transformer updates node representations via

$h_v^{(l+1)} = \text{GT}\left(h_v^{(l)}, \{h_u^{(l)}, e_{uv}\}_{u \in \mathcal{N}(v)}\right),$ (12.19)

where GT is a multi-head attention mechanism over neighbours:

$\alpha_{uv} = \text{softmax}_u\left( \frac{(W_Q h_v^{(l)})^\top (W_K h_u^{(l)} + W_E e_{uv})}{\sqrt{d_k}} \right),$ (12.20)

\(\tilde{h}_v^{(l+1)} = \sum_{u \in \mathcal{N}(v)} \alpha_{uv} W_V h_u^{(l)},\) (12.21)

\(h'_v = \text{LN}(h_v^{(l)} + \tilde{h}_v^{(l+1)}),\) (12.22)

\(h_v^{(l+1)} = \text{LN}(h'_v + \text{FFN}(h'_v)),\) (12.23)

with $W_Q, W_K, W_V, W_E$ learnable matrices.

LLMs can extend this architecture, but in three distinct roles:

- **Feature extraction.** An LLM can convert unstructured material such as e-mails, KYC documents, support tickets, analyst notes, or case narratives into structured attributes that enrich $x_v$ or $e_{uv}$.
- **Rationale generation.** After a supervised detector has produced an alert, an LLM can draft a natural-language explanation that cites the graph paths, transaction bursts, entities, and documents supporting the flag.
- **Analyst tooling.** An LLM interface can allow investigators to ask questions about a case, retrieve linked evidence, compare similar historical alerts, or prepare an escalation memo.

This separation is operationally important. The detector used for real-time screening should remain a validated supervised model with monitored thresholds, calibration, and false-positive rates. The LLM-assisted rationale or triage layer is an auxiliary workflow component: it may improve analyst productivity and explainability, but it should be evaluated for faithfulness and should not be allowed to silently change the underlying alert label.

The overall detector-training objective typically combines a supervised loss for fraud labels (e.g. cross-entropy) with regularization terms that control class imbalance, calibration, or fairness metrics.

## 12.3.2 Narrative-augmented credit risk

Narrative credit risk models combine traditional numerical features with embeddings of borrower narratives. These embeddings may come from a FinBERT- or FinEAS-style encoder, depending on whether the task emphasizes classification or sentence-level similarity. Let \(\mathbf{f}_\text{num} \in \mathbb{R}^{d_n}\) denote numerical features and \(\mathbf{f}_\text{narr} \in \mathbb{R}^{d_t}\) narrative embeddings from a financial sentence encoder. The following formula gives a stylized risk score:

\(\text{RiskScore} = \sigma(\mathbf{w}_\text{num}^\top \mathbf{f}_\text{num} + \mathbf{w}_\text{narr}^\top \mathbf{f}_\text{narr} + b),\)

where \(\sigma\) is a squashing function (e.g. logistic). The model can be trained with:

\(\min_{\mathbf{w}_\text{num}, \mathbf{w}_\text{narr}, b} \mathbb{E}[\ell(y, \text{RiskScore}) + \lambda_\text{fair} \Phi(\mathbf{f}_\text{narr})],\)

where \(\ell\) is a classification loss (e.g. cross-entropy), and \(\Phi\) encodes fairness or regularization penalties (e.g. discouraging correlation between narrative features and protected attributes).

Reported models often show:

- Higher discrimination on default vs non-default events;
- Particular gains for thin-file or non-traditional borrowers; and
- New challenges around fairness, explainability and data governance.

These challenges tie directly into the governance frameworks of Chapter 11.

## 12.4 Evaluation frameworks and benchmarks

Robust, multi-dimensional evaluation is essential to avoid overinterpreting isolated successes.
