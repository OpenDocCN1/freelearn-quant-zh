
For this document class, the extraction schema should explicitly represent:

- tranche seniority, balances, coupons, and legal final maturities;
- each waterfall step and the conditions under which it applies;
- coverage-test definitions, numerators, denominators, thresholds, and cure mechanics;
- reinvestment-period rules and asset-eligibility criteria;
- concentration limits, collateral-quality tests, and manager fees; and
- links between extracted clauses and any cash-flow or stress-testing model that consumes them.

A production workflow should test the resulting structured representation against known examples and reconcile extracted balances, percentages, and waterfall priorities before the data reach a risk engine. Once validated, the structured terms can support covenant monitoring, exception management, and portfolio-level analysis of contractual exposures [Chen et al., 2024b].

## 8.1.2 Automated compliance checks for SEC and FINRA materials

Public companies and regulated intermediaries operate under detailed filing, disclosure, communication, approval, and recordkeeping requirements. LLMs can help identify potentially missing disclosures, inconsistent narratives, unsupported claims, or risky language, but they should not issue final legal or compliance determinations. The appropriate output is a documented finding for qualified human review.

### A layered verification design

Compliance review is strongest when tasks are allocated to the component best suited to them:

- **Structural checks** verify required sections, signatures, dates, exhibits, and document versions using deterministic rules;
- **Quantitative checks** reconcile tables, totals, periods, percentages, and XBRL facts with ordinary code;
- **Semantic checks** use classifiers or LLMs to flag disclosure gaps, inconsistent statements, boilerplate risk factors, and unsupported claims;
- **Evidence checks** require every semantic finding to cite the relevant source passage and the policy or rule used by the checker; and
- **Human disposition** records whether a reviewer accepts, rejects, or remediates each finding.

The compact workflow below replaces a long placeholder class with the control logic that matters operationally.

### Listing 8.2: Layered filing review with evidence and human disposition

```python
def review_filing(document, filing_type, policy_engine, semantic_model):
    structural = check_required_sections(document, filing_type)
    numerical = reconcile_tables_and_xbrl(document)

    semantic_findings = semantic_model.flag_disclosures(
        document=document,
        filing_type=filing_type,
    )

    grounded_findings = []
    for finding in semantic_findings:
        evidence = locate_exact_evidence(document, finding)
        rule = policy_engine.lookup(finding.policy_id)
        if evidence is not None and rule is not None:
            grounded_findings.append({
                "finding": finding,
                "evidence": evidence,
                "policy_version": rule.version,
                "status": "awaiting_human_review",
            })

    return {
        "structural": structural,
        "numerical": numerical,
        "semantic": grounded_findings,
        "final_determination": None,
    }
```

The explicit final_determination = None is intentional: the automated system assembles evidence and prioritizes work, while an authorized reviewer owns the compliance decision.

### FINRA communications review

FINRA Rule 2210 establishes standards for broker-dealer communications with the public, including content, supervision, approval, and recordkeeping requirements. A communication-review system may screen for exaggerated or promissory language, one-sided presentation, unsupported performance claims, missing risk disclosures, or outdated approvals. Other rule sets may apply to research reports, recommendations, products, jurisdictions, and communication channels, so the active policy library must be versioned rather than embedded permanently in a prompt [Financial Industry Regulatory Authority, 2026].

A useful finding record contains the suspected issue, exact text span, active rule or internal policy, severity, rationale, and recommended reviewer action. Severity should be derived from explicit factors rather than from an opaque model-generated label. For example, an institution can combine the materiality of the claim, customer reach, evidence quality, disclosure gap, and rule criticality into a configurable triage score. That score prioritizes review; it does not declare the communication compliant or non-compliant.

Multi-label classification can support this process because one passage may raise several distinct concerns at once. However, probability outputs must be calibrated on representative, time-stamped validation data and monitored as rules, products, and communication styles change [Guo et al., 2017]. Automated edits should remain proposed redlines until approved by an authorized compliance professional.

## 8.2 Advanced document intelligence applications

The natural extension of single-document extraction is reasoning across a set of related documents. Financial analysis often requires comparing annual and quarterly filings, earnings releases, footnotes, policies, contracts, and market commentary across time. This section therefore completes the document-intelligence discussion before the chapter turns to client-facing systems.

### 8.2.1 Multi-document reasoning and synthesis

A multi-document system must solve four separate problems: selecting the relevant documents, aligning their entities and reporting periods, detecting inconsistencies or changes, and synthesizing an answer with traceable citations. A document graph is useful when relationships themselves matter—for example, when an amended agreement supersedes an earlier clause, a footnote explains a line item, or a risk factor changes across reporting periods.

### Long context, RAG, and document graphs

*Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG)* retrieves a query-specific set of source passages before asking the model to generate an answer [Levis et al., 2020]. The emergence of million-token-class context windows, including configurations in the one-to-two-million-token range, changes but does not eliminate the architectural choice. A large context window makes it possible to provide many complete filings in one request, but nominal capacity is not the same as reliable use of every token. Cost, latency, access control, position effects, duplicated evidence, and citation discipline still matter [Liu et al., 2024a].

Table 8.1 summarizes the practical trade-off.

| Architecture | Best suited to | Main advantages | Main controls and limitations |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Long-context prompting | A bounded, known set of documents that fits comfortably within the effective context budget | Preserves full-document order and avoids retrieval misses | Higher token cost and latency; irrelevant text can dilute evidence; permissions must be enforced before prompt construction |
| RAG | Large or frequently changing repositories and focused factual questions | Lower context cost; metadata filtering; source-level access control; easier corpus updates | Retrieval can miss decisive passages; requires evaluation of recall, reranking, chunking, and citation faithfulness |
| Document graph | Cross-references, amendments, entity relationships, temporal dependencies, and contradiction analysis | Makes document relationships explicit and supports structured consistency checks | More ingestion and maintenance effort; graph construction errors can propagate into synthesis |
| Hybrid | High-stakes analysis over a large corpus | Uses retrieval or graphs to select evidence, then supplies complete relevant sections or documents to a long-context model | Most robust but operationally complex; requires end-to-end testing, provenance, and cost controls |

Table 8.1: When to use long context, RAG, a document graph, or a hybrid design

Long-context prompting is appropriate when the evidence set is small, stable, permission-compatible, and comfortably below the effective context limit. RAG is preferable when the corpus is large, dynamic, or access-controlled, or when queries are narrow and repeated. A document graph becomes valuable when the answer depends on amendment chains, entity resolution, period alignment, or cross-document consistency rather than simple passage retrieval. For many financial applications, the most defensible design is hybrid: retrieve and rerank candidate materials, expand to complete sections, preserve explicit relationships, and then synthesize within a controlled long-context request.

### Listing 8.3: Hybrid multi-document orchestration (architecture sketch)

```python
def answer_across_filings(question, corpus, model, token_budget):
    permitted = corpus.filter_by_user_entitlements()
    candidates = hybrid_retrieve(question, permitted, top_k=40)
    ranked = rerank(question, candidates)[:12]

    if estimated_tokens(expanded) <= token_budget:
        evidence = expanded
        mode = "retrieval_plus_long_context"
```
