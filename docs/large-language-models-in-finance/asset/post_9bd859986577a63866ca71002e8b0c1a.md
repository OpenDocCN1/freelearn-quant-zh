
## Fine-tuning for financial NER

NER is formulated as a token classification problem: the model assigns a label to every token in the input sequence. The standard labeling scheme is BIO tagging, where B- marks the beginning of an entity span, I- marks continuation tokens within the same span, and O marks tokens that belong to no entity. This scheme allows the model to handle multi-token entities (such as “Goldman Sachs” spanning two tokens, or “$81.8 billion” spanning three) without any special architecture changes.

For financial NER it is important to use a cased pre-trained model rather than an uncased one, because capitalization carries strong signal for proper nouns—company names, executive names, and country codes are far more reliably capitalized in financial text than in casual language. The snippet below shows the model setup and a single labeled training example:

```python
from transformers import AutoModelForTokenClassification

# Load pre-trained model
model = AutoModelForTokenClassification.from_pretrained(
    "bert-base-cased", # Cased for proper nouns
    num_labels=len(label_list),
    id2label=id2label,
    label2id=label2id,
)

# Example training data (BIO tagging)
train_example = {
    'tokens': ['Apple', 'reported', 'Q3', 'revenue', 'of', '$',
               '81.8', 'billion'],
    'labels': ['B-ORG', 'O', 'B-DATE', 'O', 'O', 'B-MONEY',
               'I-MONEY', 'I-MONEY']
}

# Fine-tune on financial NER dataset
trainer = Trainer(
    model=model,
    args=training_args,
    train_dataset=financial_ner_train,
    eval_dataset=financial_ner_val,
    tokenizer=tokenizer,
    data_collator=data_collator,
    compute_metrics=compute_metrics,
)

trainer.train()
```

### Evaluation metrics

NER evaluation uses F1 as its primary metric, but there are two distinct levels at which it can be computed, and the choice matters for financial applications. Entity-level F1 requires the model to correctly identify both the complete span of an entity and its type—a partial match (e.g., predicting B-MONEY on “$81.8” but missing “billion”) counts as a failure. Token-level F1 gives partial credit for partially correct spans. For downstream financial pipelines where entity spans feed into calculators or databases, entity-level F1 is the right metric: a partially extracted monetary amount is not useful and may introduce errors.

- Entity-level F1: Correct if entire entity span and type match
- Token-level F1: Correct per-token predictions
- Per-class metrics: Separate F1 for each entity type

Per-class metrics are worth tracking separately in financial NER because performance varies substantially across entity types: ORG and MONEY tend to be well-learned (they appear frequently and have clear textual signals), while rarer types like INSTRUMENT or METRIC may require larger or more specialized training sets to reach the same level of accuracy.

While NER focuses on identifying and labeling entities, many financial applications require going further, combining extracted information with reasoning and calculation. This leads us to question answering.

## 3.6.3 Financial question answering

Question answering over financial documents is qualitatively harder than NER or sentiment classification because the model must reason and calculate rather than merely recognize or label. It must locate relevant information scattered across mixed text and tables, determine what calculation is needed, carry out multi-step arithmetic, and express the result in the correct format and units. A model that can accurately parse a balance sheet but applies the wrong formula—computing year-over-year revenue growth when asked for gross margin change—provides no value and may actively mislead analysts.

### FinQA: Numerical reasoning dataset

FinQA [Chen et al., 2021] is the standard benchmark for this capability. It consists of questions paired with annual report tables and text passages, where answering correctly requires a multi-step reasoning chain that integrates numerical extraction with arithmetic. The example below illustrates the challenge: the question asks about gross profit margin change across two years, which requires computing two ratios and taking their difference—none of which is stated directly in the source:

Table: Apple Inc. Income Statement (millions)

| Year | Revenue | COGS | Gross Profit |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2022 | 394,328 | 223,546 | 170,782 |
| 2023 | 383,285 | 214,137 | 169,148 |

Question: "What was the year-over-year change in gross profit margin from 2022 to 2023?"

Reasoning Steps:
- 1. Calculate 2022 gross margin: 170,782 / 394,328 = 43.31%
- 2. Calculate 2023 gross margin: 169,148 / 383,285 = 44.13%
- 3. Calculate change: 44.13% - 43.31% = +0.82 percentage points

Note that the gross margin actually *improved* slightly from 2022 to 2023 despite lower absolute revenue and profit—a nuance that only emerges from carrying out the full calculation rather than reading the direction of revenue change alone. This is exactly the kind of multi-step inference that general-purpose models frequently miss without task-specific fine-tuning.

### Fine-tuning for financial QA

The four capabilities below describe what a model must internalize to answer FinQA-style questions reliably. Each represents a distinct learning challenge, and poor performance at any one stage propagates errors through the rest of the reasoning chain:

- 1. Extract relevant numbers from tables/text
- 2. Determine required calculations
- 3. Perform multi-step numerical reasoning
- 4. Generate final answer with proper formatting

T5 (Text-to-Text Transfer Transformer) is a natural fit for this task because its encoder-decoder architecture handles both the reading comprehension component (the encoder attends to table and text jointly) and the answer generation component (the decoder produces the reasoning chain and final value). Crucially, by training the decoder to produce the explicit reasoning program alongside the final answer—encoded as a string following a separator—the model learns to show its work. This improves both accuracy, by rewarding structurally correct reasoning chains, and interpretability, since analysts can verify the steps rather than accepting a bare number:

```python
# Fine-tune T5 for Financial QA
from transformers import T5ForConditionalGeneration

model = T5ForConditionalGeneration.from_pretrained("t5-base")

# Format as text-to-text
def format_finqa_example(example):
    input_text = f"""
    Answer the financial question based on the table and text.

    Table: {example['table']}
    Text: {example['text']}
    Question: {example['question']}
    """

    target_text = f"{example['answer']} | {example['reasoning']}"
    return {'input': input_text, 'target': target_text}


# Train
trainer = Trainer(
    model=model,
    args=training_args,
    train_dataset=finqa_train.map(format_finqa_example),
    eval_dataset=finqa_val.map(format_finqa_example),
)
trainer.train()
```

## 3.6.4 Financial text summarization

Summarization is among the most immediately useful financial NLP applications. Analysts and portfolio managers routinely face hundreds of pages of earnings call transcripts, 10-K filings, and research reports per week. A model that can reliably compress these into concise, factually accurate summaries without hallucinating figures or omitting material disclosures can dramatically improve the efficiency of investment research workflows.

### Earnings call summarization

Earnings calls present a particularly demanding version of the summarization problem. A typical transcript runs to 10,000–20,000 words spread across a prepared management presentation and an extended analyst Q&A session, which together exceed the context window of most standard transformer models. The desired output is a 200–300 word summary that preserves the four elements analysts care about most:

- Financial performance highlights (revenue, earnings, margins vs. expectations)
- Key business developments (product launches, acquisitions, restructuring)
- Forward guidance (updated revenue or earnings outlook)
- Management outlook and qualitative tone

To make this concrete, we now look at how summarization models are fine-tuned for this setting.

### Fine-tuning for summarization

Because earnings call transcripts routinely exceed the 512-token limit of standard BERT-style encoders, the architecture must be capable of processing long documents. The Longformer Encoder-Decoder (LED) achieves this through sparse attention—each token attends locally to its neighbors and globally to a small set of designated tokens, rather than attending to all tokens quadratically.
