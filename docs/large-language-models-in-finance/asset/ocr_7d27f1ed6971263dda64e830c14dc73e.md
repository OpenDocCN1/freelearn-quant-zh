The FAIR framework contributes a finance-specific structure for integrating these concerns [Noguer i Alonso and Mendell, 2025a,b].

The resulting objective is proportionate governance: controls should intensify with autonomy, scale, speed, irreversibility, customer impact, and systemic relevance. A research assistant that drafts a memo does not require the same controls as an agent that can submit orders, alter a portfolio, approve credit, or communicate directly with customers. The framework below therefore begins with materiality and intended use rather than with the model label alone.

# 11.2 Theoretical foundations: Building a multi-dimensional framework

A coherent governance framework must connect technical properties to financial consequences and then to accountable decisions. This section first explains why several professional disciplines are required and then organizes their contributions into three mutually dependent pillars. The purpose is not to create another abstract checklist, but to provide a translation mechanism from model behavior to operational controls, risk limits, and legal responsibilities.

# 11.2.1 The convergence of disciplines

Effective governance of financial LLMs requires structured interdisciplinary collaboration. Computer scientists and machine-learning engineers contribute knowledge of architectures, training dynamics, evaluation, security, and technical limitations. Quantitative researchers and financial engineers contribute market microstructure, pricing, execution, portfolio, liquidity, and systemic-risk expertise. Financial analysts and portfolio managers provide domain judgment about information quality and economic relevance. Behavioral economists examine decision biases and interaction effects. Legal, compliance, privacy, and ethics specialists interpret obligations concerning fairness, disclosure, accountability, customer outcomes, and redress.

The challenge is not merely to place these specialists in the same committee. Institutions need a shared language that allows a technical observation to become a risk decision. For example, sensitivity to a prompt template must be translated into a measurable exposure; the exposure must be assigned an owner and threshold; and the threshold must trigger a defined action such as routing to human review, reducing a limit, reverting a model version, or suspending a tool.

We refer to this translation process as cognitive bridging. It consists of documented interfaces between disciplines: model cards and system inventories for technical-to-risk translation; risk taxonomies and scenario libraries for risk-to-business translation; and control mappings, accountability matrices, and evidence packs for business-to-regulatory translation. The value of cognitive bridging is practical: it prevents a vulnerability from remaining a technical observation that never reaches the people authorized to change production behavior.
