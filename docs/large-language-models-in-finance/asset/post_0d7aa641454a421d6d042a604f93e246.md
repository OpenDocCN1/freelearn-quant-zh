## Evaluation metrics

Summarization is harder to evaluate automatically than classification or span extraction, because a good summary can use entirely different wording from the reference while conveying the same information. The three metrics below capture complementary aspects of summary quality:

- **ROUGE**: Overlap-based metrics (ROUGE-1, ROUGE-2, ROUGE-L)
- **BERTScore**: Semantic similarity using embeddings
- **Human Evaluation**: Factual accuracy, completeness, readability

ROUGE measures n-gram overlap between the model's summary and a gold reference. It is fast to compute and provides a reproducible benchmark for comparing model versions, but it penalizes valid paraphrases and rewards superficially similar but potentially wrong text. BERTScore addresses the paraphrase problem by comparing contextual embeddings of predicted and reference tokens—two sentences with the same meaning receive a high score even if they share no words. Human evaluation remains the gold standard in financial summarization because it is the only method that can reliably detect factual errors such as an incorrectly stated revenue figure or an omitted guidance revision. In high-stakes financial settings, automated metrics should always be supplemented by at least a sample-level human audit before a summarization model is deployed in production.

With this foundation in place, we now move beyond supervised signals to settings where explicit labels are not always available, and alignment must be learned from human preferences.

## 3.7 Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF)

The fine-tuning methods discussed so far assume that the desired model behavior can be fully specified through labeled examples: a sentence paired with a sentiment label, a question paired with a correct answer, a transcript paired with a gold summary. For many financial applications this assumption holds. But a growing class of tasks—generating investment commentary, drafting client-facing explanations of portfolio decisions, writing nuanced risk narratives—resists clean labeling because quality depends on subtle, subjective attributes: Is the tone appropriately confident without being overconfident? Does the response include the right regulatory disclaimers? Is the reasoning transparent enough for a compliance officer to follow?

RLHF addresses this by replacing the fixed label with a learned reward signal derived from human judgments. Rather than telling the model exactly what to output, it tells the model which of several candidate outputs humans prefer, then uses reinforcement learning to optimize the policy toward higher-scoring responses. This makes RLHF the natural complement to supervised fine-tuning: SFT teaches the model *what* to do, while RLHF teaches it *how well* to do it according to human standards that resist precise formalization.

To understand how RLHF operates in practice, it is useful to first look at the overall pipeline.

### 3.7.1 RLHF pipeline

RLHF proceeds in three stages that build on one another. Starting from a supervised fine-tuned model ensures that the policy being aligned already produces plausible financial outputs, which makes the reinforcement learning phase more stable and data-efficient.

- 1. **Supervised fine-tuning (SFT):** Initial fine-tuning on high-quality demonstrations establishes a competent baseline policy. This step is necessary because reinforcement learning is highly unstable when applied directly to a randomly initialized or purely pre-trained model—the reward signal is too sparse and the policy’s outputs too incoherent to generate useful preference data.

- 2. **Reward model training:** A separate model is trained on human preference rankings to predict which of two outputs a human would prefer. This stage must come after SFT because the preference pairs need to contrast responses that are already plausibly financial in nature; comparing random outputs would produce a reward model that learns nothing useful about financial quality.

- 3. **RL optimization:** The SFT model is further fine-tuned using PPO (or a simpler alternative) to maximize the reward signal, with a KL-divergence penalty anchoring it to the SFT policy. This ordering is essential: the reward model must already be trained before RL can begin, and the KL anchor to the SFT policy prevents the model from collapsing into reward-hacking behaviors.

Among these stages, reward modeling is the key step that translates subjective human judgment into a signal the model can learn from.

### 3.7.2 Reward model training

The reward model is trained on preference pairs: for a given prompt, human annotators are shown two or more model-generated responses and asked to rank them. The example below illustrates the kind of discrimination the reward model must learn. Both responses contain the same numerical facts, but Response B contextualizes those facts—explaining why the Services segment growth matters for overall margins—in a way that reflects genuine analytical judgment rather than recitation. Training the reward model on thousands of such comparisons teaches it to assign higher scores to outputs that demonstrate insight, appropriate hedging, and clear structure.

The example below illustrates a typical preference pair used to train the reward model:

> Prompt: "Analyze Apple's Q3 2024 earnings"
> 
> Response A: "Apple's Q3 FY2024 revenue was $85.8B, up 5% YoY. iPhone revenue was $39.3B, down about 1%. Services revenue reached $24.2B, up 14%. Net income was $21.4B with EPS of $1.40."
> 
> Response B: "In Q3 FY2024, Apple reported revenue of $85.8B (+5% YoY). Growth did not come from the iPhone, which was roughly flat at $39.3B (-1%); it came from the high-margin Services segment, which reached $24.2B (+14%). That mix shift matters more than the headline, because Services carries a materially higher gross margin than hardware. Net income was $21.4B and EPS $1.40."
> 
> Human Ranking: B > A (B identifies the revenue mix shift, not just the totals -- and both responses are factually grounded)

The reward model \(r_\phi(x, y)\) is trained to output a scalar score for any prompt-response pair. The Bradley-Terry loss function operationalizes the preference signal: it maximizes the log-probability that the preferred response \(y_w\) receives a higher score than the dispreferred response \( y_l \), pushing the model to assign consistently higher rewards to the kinds of outputs that human reviewers favor. Formally, the reward model maps each prompt-response pair to a real-valued score:

```
\( r_\phi(x, y) = \text{RewardModel}_\phi(x, y) \in \mathbb{R} \)
```

and is trained by minimizing the Bradley-Terry loss over all collected preference pairs:

```
\( \mathcal{L}_{\text{RM}} = -\mathbb{E}_{(x, y_w, y_l)} [\log \sigma(r_\phi(x, y_w) - r_\phi(x, y_l))] \)
```

where \( y_w \) is the preferred (winner) response and \( y_l \) is the less preferred (loser) response.

Once this learned notion of “quality” is in place, it can be used to directly guide model behavior through reinforcement learning.

### 3.7.3 Policy optimization with Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO)

With a trained reward model in place, the third stage uses reinforcement learning to update the policy—the fine-tuned language model—so that it generates responses that receive higher rewards. The objective below captures the core trade-off: the first term drives the model toward high-reward responses, while the KL divergence penalty prevents it from drifting too far from the supervised fine-tuned reference model \( \pi_{\text{ref}} \). This penalty is essential in financial settings: without it, the model may find ways to “game” the reward signal by producing outputs that superficially match annotator preferences (say, by including more disclaimers and hedges) while losing the accuracy and coherence of the underlying response—a failure mode known as reward hacking.

Use PPO [Schulman et al., 2017] to fine-tune the model to maximize expected reward:

```
\( \max_\theta \mathbb{E}_{x \sim \mathcal{D}, y \sim \pi_\theta(y|x)} [r_\phi(x, y)] - \beta \cdot \text{KL}[\pi_\theta || \pi_{\text{ref}}] \)
```

The KL divergence term prevents the model from deviating too far from the reference policy \( \pi_{\text{ref}} \) (the SFT model), avoiding reward hacking and maintaining general capabilities.

PPO enforces stable policy updates through a clipped surrogate objective. The ratio \( r_t(\theta) \) measures how much the new policy deviates from the old one for a given action; clipping this ratio to the interval \( [1 - \epsilon, 1 + \epsilon] \) prevents any single update from making large, destabilizing changes to the policy. The advantage estimate \( \hat{A}_t \) captures whether the action taken (the token generated) turned out better or worse than the baseline, and scales the gradient accordingly:

The update step in PPO is defined as follows:

```
\( \mathcal{L}_{\text{PPO}}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_t \left[ \min \left( r_t(\theta) \hat{A}_t, \text{clip}(r_t(\theta), 1 - \epsilon, 1 + \epsilon) \hat{A}_t \right) \right] \)
```
