## 12.4.1 Multi-task financial benchmarks

PIXIU and similar benchmarks define suites of tasks that span across:

- Sentiment analysis on financial news and social media;
- Entity and event extraction from filings and reports;
- Question answering over multi-document corpora;
- Numerical reasoning and table interpretation.

Evaluations typically benchmark performance across three categories of models:

- Generic LLMs (e.g. GPT-style, LLaMA-style);
- Domain-adapted models (BloombergGPT, FinBERT, FinEAS, FinGPT variants); and
- Task-specific fine-tuned encoders.

Let \(\mathcal{T} = \{T_1, ..., T_m\}\) denote tasks, each with dataset \(\mathcal{D}_{T_i}\) and metric \(M_{T_i}\). For a model \(f\), we can define a multi-task score as:

```
S(f) = \sum_{i=1}^m \omega_i M_{T_i}(f; \mathcal{D}_{T_i}),
```

with weights \(\omega_i\) reflecting task importance. In practice, researchers often report per-task scores and leave aggregation to readers, as different institutions prioritize tasks differently.

Reported results consistently show that no single model dominates across all tasks: gains on entity- and sentiment-heavy tasks do not automatically translate into strong performance on numerical reasoning, and vice versa. The following table summarizes the schematic structure of a multi-task financial benchmark with respect to various task types and key metrics:

| Task type | Example dataset | Key metric | Typical winner |
|---|---|---|---|
| Sentiment | News / tweets | F1 / accuracy | FinBERT / FinEAS-style encoders |
| Entity/event | Filings | F1 / EM | BloombergGPT / FinLLMs |
| QA over docs | 10-Ks, research | EM / F1 | RAG + LLM |
| Numerical QA | Tables + text | EM / RMSE | Tool-augmented LLMs |

## 12.4.2 FinQA and numerical reasoning benchmarks

FinQA and related datasets stress-test the hybrid reasoning capabilities of LLMs. Systems significantly outperform naive prompting, if they combine:

- Retrieval modules (Chapter 4);
- Structured table understanding; and
- External tools (calculators, code interpreters)

A typical pipeline factorizes the task into:

1. Selecting relevant textual and tabular context;
2. Generating an intermediate program or computation graph \( g_\theta(x) \) via the LLM; and
3. Executing \( g_\theta(x) \) in a trusted numerical environment to produce the final answer.

Here, the combined objective refers to the training loss for the whole retrieval–program–execution pipeline, not to a single standalone metric. In a stylized supervised setting, one may write

\( \mathcal{L}_{\text{FinQA}}(\theta) = \mathcal{L}_{\text{ret}}(c_\theta, c^*) + \lambda_{\text{prog}}\mathcal{L}_{\text{prog}}(g_\theta, g^*) + \lambda_{\text{ans}}\ell(\text{Exec}(g_\theta, c_\theta), y), \)

where \( c_\theta \) is the retrieved textual or tabular context, \( c^* \) is the supporting evidence when it is annotated, \( g_\theta \) is the generated program or computation graph, \( g^* \) is a reference program when available, and \( y \) is the final numeric answer. The retrieval term rewards selecting the right evidence; the program term rewards generating a valid intermediate computation; and the answer term rewards numerical correctness after executing the program. When reference programs are unavailable, the middle term is often replaced by execution checks, consistency losses, or human-verified rationales.

Even the best systems still exhibit error rates that would be unacceptable in high-stakes production settings. This motivates the layered assurance strategies, covered in Chapter 11.

## 12.5 Emerging applications and future directions

The preceding sections focused on the best-developed parts of the literature: financial language models, memory-augmented trading systems, risk workflows, and benchmarks. The same ingredients are now beginning to appear in newer domains where the data are more heterogeneous and the systems are more tightly connected to external infrastructure. This section highlights two directions that are representative rather than exhaustive. The first concerns decentralized finance, where LLMs must combine contract code, on-chain data, governance forums, and adversarial security assumptions. The second concerns hybrid optimization workflows, where an LLM acts less as a predictor and more as an interface between human constraints and classical, quantum-inspired, or quantum-assisted optimization routines.

### 12.5.1 DeFi, on-chain analytics, and protocol governance

DeFi-focused systems such as DeFiScope integrate LLMs with:

- Parsers for smart contracts and protocol documentation;
- Indexers for on-chain data and transaction graphs; and
- RAG pipelines for governance proposals and forum discussions.

A stylized architecture is the one that can:

1. Parse and normalize contract code into an intermediate representation;
2. Embed documentation, proposals and forum posts into a vector store;
3. Use an LLM to answer questions or summarize proposals, grounded in retrieved evidence and contract semantics.

These systems can assist in summarizing complex proposals, flagging suspicious patterns, and explaining protocol changes to non-expert users. They also operate in adversarial environments, where robustness to prompt injection, data poisoning and adversarial contracts is critical.

### 12.5.2 Quantum-enhanced optimization

Quantum-inspired and quantum-assisted optimization methods are being explored for portfolio and risk problems. In such architectures, an LLM may:

- Select the optimization formulation based on natural-language descriptions of constraints and objectives;
- Generate or refine parameterizations for classical or quantum optimizers; and
- Explain the resulting allocations or hedges to human decision makers.

For instance, a quantum approximate optimization algorithm (QAOA) for portfolio selection may seek to minimize a cost function with:

```
C(w) = -\mu^\top w + \frac{\lambda}{2}w^\top\Sigma w,
```

subject to constraints, with \(\mu\) expected returns and \(\Sigma\) covariance. The LLM can map informal risk preferences and constraints into choices of \(\lambda\), feasible sets, or custom penalty terms. At present, hardware and integration constraints limit these approaches to research and small-scale pilots.
