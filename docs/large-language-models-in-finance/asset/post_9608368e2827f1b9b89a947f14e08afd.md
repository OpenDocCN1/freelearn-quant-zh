
## 6.9 Implementation roadmap

A financial MCP deployment should progress in controlled stages rather than moving directly from a prototype to production use. Each stage should add capability only after the required controls, evidence, and operational ownership are in place. The roadmap below gives a practical sequence for introducing MCP-assisted workflows while limiting authorization, data, and execution risk.

- 1. **Inventory and classify.** Identify the proposed MCP servers, tools, data sources, side effects, system owners, users, and regulatory obligations. Each tool should be classified by business purpose, data sensitivity, permitted users, and whether it can create a financial or external side effect.

- 2. **Start read-only.** Begin with a small number of approved resources and deterministic calculations that do not change business state. At this stage, the system should retrieve, summarize, and calculate, but it should not create external communications, submit orders, modify records, or trigger operational actions.

- 3. **Build the control envelope.** Add identity propagation, policy-as-code, schema pinning, point-in-time checks, deterministic validation, and audit records before adding more intelligence or autonomy. The control envelope should be tested independently of the model.

- 4. **Add draft actions.** Permit the model to create proposals, tickets, memos, or reports that remain non-binding. These outputs should be clearly marked as drafts and routed through review workflows before they are used for client communication, regulatory reporting, trading, or risk decisions.

- 5. **Introduce approval-bound execution.** Allow execution only when the action is bound to a reviewed request through canonical hashes, idempotency keys, separation of duties, and reconciliation. Any material change to the request, evidence, policy version, or destination should invalidate the approval.

- 6. **Operate as a model-risk system.** Monitor drift, incidents, server changes, tool selection, refusals, escalation quality, latency, cost, and control failures. Model, prompt, schema, server, policy, and dependency changes should trigger risk-based revalidation before promotion.

This staged approach keeps MCP adoption aligned with financial model-risk management. The system earns additional capability only when the surrounding controls can demonstrate that the workflow remains authorized, auditable, recoverable, and safe to operate.

## 6.10 Limitations and open problems

The architecture described in this chapter reduces the authority of the language model and makes failures more detectable, but it does not eliminate risk. Model error, malicious insiders, supply-chain compromise, flawed deterministic code, bad market data, weak approvals, and poor human judgment can still undermine a financial workflow. The control envelope should therefore be understood as a risk-reduction architecture, not as a proof that MCP-assisted systems are safe in all settings.

Several open problems remain:

- Tool-selection reliability under distribution shift. Institutions need better ways to evaluate whether a model selects the right tool when market conditions, document types, prompts, or user behavior differ from the validation set.

- Non-interference between servers sharing one host context. A host may connect to several MCP servers with different permissions, data classifications, and business purposes. Stronger methods are needed to show that one server cannot influence, observe, or corrupt another server's context or outputs.

- Point-in-time provenance across vendors. Financial workflows often depend on documents, prices, positions, filings, and model outputs from different providers. Standardized provenance, revision linkage, and availability-time metadata remain difficult to enforce across vendors.

- Automation bias in approval interfaces. Human review is meaningful only if reviewers can challenge the system. More work is needed to measure when users over-trust model-generated explanations, accept defaults too quickly, or fail to notice missing evidence.

- Privacy-preserving audit evidence. Audit records must preserve accountability without creating unnecessary exposure of client data, positions, prompts, or confidential business logic. Better methods are needed for balancing reconstruction, privacy, and regulatory access.

- Continuous certification of server and schema changes. MCP servers, schemas, prompts, policies, and dependencies may change between periodic reviews. Institutions need practical methods for continuous revalidation and certification of these changes.

Post-quantum cryptography, confidential computing, and federated architectures may become relevant for particular threat models, especially where long-term confidentiality, shared analytics, or third-party exposure are central concerns. However, they should not distract from immediate controls: least privilege, safe credential handling, schema validation, deterministic computation, point-in-time evidence, idempotency, and complete auditability. These controls are not frontier research problems; they are the baseline for making MCP-assisted financial workflows governable today.

## 6.11 Implementation-quality checklist

Before a financial workflow is allowed to use MCP in production, the institution should be able to answer “yes” to the following checks. These checks do not replace formal model-risk, security, legal, or compliance review, but they define the minimum implementation evidence expected for a material MCP-assisted workflow.

- 1. **Protocol and capability pinning.** The exact MCP revision, transport, declared capabilities, approved servers, and schema hashes are pinned, versioned, and logged.

- 2. **Server ownership and trust classification.** Every server has an accountable owner, trust classification, data-flow review, egress policy, and approved operational purpose.

- 3. **Secret isolation.** The model-visible context contains no access tokens, private keys, passwords, or unnecessary sensitive data.

- 4. **Narrow tool design.** Tools are narrow, typed, allow-listed, and separated by business effect: read, calculate, draft, approve, and execute.

- 5. **Deterministic authorization.** Business authorization is enforced outside the model and does not depend on prompt compliance or model self-assessment.

- 6. **Evidence validity.** All evidence has a recorded source, version, hash, permission status, and availability time.

- 7. **Numerical reconciliation.** Material numbers are recomputed, reconciled, and validated outside the LLM using deterministic code or approved calculation engines.

- 8. **Approval binding.** Approvals bind to the exact canonical request and expire after any material change to parameters, evidence, policy version, destination, or output.

- 9. **Idempotent execution.** Write actions use idempotency keys, reconciliation, execution ledgers, and recoverable state transitions.

- 10. **Adversarial testing.** Prompt injection, malicious tool metadata, confused-deputy behavior, over-broad tool use, and data exfiltration are tested.

- 11. **Operational monitoring.** Latency, error, timeout, retry, queue-depth, and cancellation distributions are monitored against an explicit service-level objective (SLO).

- 12. **Audit reconstruction.** Audit records support reconstruction of the material decision without storing secrets or relying on hidden chain-of-thought.

- 13. **Change-triggered revalidation.** Model, prompt, policy, schema, server, dependency, and infrastructure changes trigger risk-based revalidation before promotion.

If any item fails, the workflow should remain in pilot, read-only, shadow, or human-reviewed mode until the gap is remediated and retested.

## 6.12 Summary

MCP is valuable because it standardizes how LLM applications connect to context and tools. It provides interoperable messages, lifecycle handling, capabilities, resources, prompts, and tools. Its value should not, however, be inflated into a claim that the protocol alone secures a financial institution.

This chapter showed that secure financial integration requires a separate control envelope around MCP-based tool use. That envelope provides identity, least privilege, business authorization, typed tool contracts, point-in-time evidence checks, deterministic numerical validation, approval binding, idempotent execution, and auditability. The model may propose, explain, summarize, or draft, but deterministic systems must decide what may execute.

The chapter also developed a finance-specific threat model, covering prompt injection, confused-deputy behavior, token passthrough, compromised servers, over-broad tools, stale data, duplicate execution, data exfiltration, and approved mismatch. These risks cannot be addressed by prompt wording alone. They require enforceable controls at the host, server, policy, validation, execution, and audit layers.

The central design boundary is therefore:

- MCP provides interoperable messages, lifecycle, capabilities, resources, prompts, and tools;
- the host provides consent, orchestration, and clear user interaction;
- the financial control envelope provides identity, least privilege, business authorization, temporal validity, numerical integrity, approval, idempotency, and audit;
- the model proposes and explains, while deterministic systems decide what may execute.

The practical goal is to turn tool use from an impressive demonstration into a governable financial workflow. When MCP is combined with typed tools, point-in-time evidence, deterministic checks, approval gates, audit records, validation tests, and operational monitoring, it can support financial systems that are not only more capable, but also more controlled and accountable.

The next chapter builds on this foundation by examining how LLM-based systems can be applied across financial workflows while preserving the same principles of validation, supervision, and governance.
