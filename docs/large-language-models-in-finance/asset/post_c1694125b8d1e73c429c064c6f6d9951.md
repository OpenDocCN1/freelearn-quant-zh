
$$\theta^* = \arg \min_{\theta} \mathbb{E}_{(x,y)\sim\mathcal{D}_{finance}}[\mathcal{L}(f_\theta(x), y)] + \lambda\|\theta - \theta_0\|^2$$ (3.3)

where L is the task-specific loss function (e.g., cross-entropy for classification, mean squared error for regression), \(f_\theta\) is the model parameterized by \(\theta\), and the regularization term \(\lambda\|\theta - \theta_0\|^2\) prevents catastrophic forgetting by keeping parameters close to their pre-trained values.

The strength of fine-tuning lies in its ability to specialize general-purpose models without requiring training from scratch. A model pre-trained on a large general corpus already encodes broad linguistic and representational structure. Fine-tuning refines this knowledge for financial contexts, teaching the model that _duration_ refers to interest rate sensitivity, _beta_ measures systemic risk, and _guidance_ in an earnings context means forward-looking statements about future performance.

## 3.3 Foundations of fine-tuning

Understanding the theoretical underpinnings of fine-tuning provides insights into when and why it works, and how to optimize the process for financial applications.

### 3.3.1 Transfer learning theory

Fine-tuning can be understood as a form of transfer learning, in which knowledge acquired in one domain (general text) is adapted to a new domain (finance). The effectiveness of this transfer depends largely on the relationship between the source and target domains.

Transfer learning works best when the two domains have a similar underlying structure. Financial text, for example, retains the same basic linguistic foundations as general language—grammar, syntax, and common expressions—while introducing specialized terminology and domain-specific semantics. Pre-training on large general corpora, therefore, provides a strong linguistic base that fine-tuning can refine for financial contexts.

The similarity of tasks also plays an important role. Models trained on next-token prediction during pre-training learn broad language modeling capabilities that transfer naturally to downstream tasks such as financial text generation, summarization, and question answering. Because these tasks rely on similar underlying abilities (understanding context, predicting plausible continuations, and organizing information) the knowledge acquired during pre-training remains highly useful.

At a representational level, pre-training produces hierarchical internal representations within the model. Lower layers tend to capture general linguistic patterns, such as syntax and frequently occurring words, while higher layers encode more task-specific relationships. During fine-tuning, adaptation often occurs more strongly in the upper layers, allowing the model to incorporate financial concepts while preserving the broader linguistic knowledge learned during pre-training [Yosinski et al., 2014].

### 3.3.2 Catastrophic forgetting and regularization

A key challenge in fine-tuning is *catastrophic forgetting*: the tendency of a model to lose general knowledge when it is aggressively adapted to a narrow task. If fine-tuning proceeds too quickly or relies on a small financial dataset, the model may overfit to the training examples and degrade its broader reasoning capabilities.

One common mitigation strategy is **weight regularization**, which discourages large deviations from the original pre-trained parameters. In practice, this is implemented by penalizing parameter changes during training:

```
\mathcal{L}_{total} = \mathcal{L}_{task} + \lambda \sum_i (\theta_i - \theta_{0,i})^2
```

This penalty — an explicit \(\|\theta-\theta_{pre}\|^2\) term added to the objective, and distinct from AdamW’s decoupled weight decay, which shrinks toward zero — keeps the updated parameters close to their pre-trained values unless the fine-tuning data provides strong evidence that larger adjustments are beneficial.

Another important mechanism is **learning rate scheduling**. Using smaller learning rates than those employed during pre-training helps prevent abrupt parameter shifts that could overwrite previously learned knowledge. In practice, fine-tuning often uses learning rates that are 10–100 times smaller than pre-training rates, with gradual decay over time:

```
\eta_t = \eta_0 \cdot \frac{1}{1 + \alpha t}
```

where \(\eta_0\) is the initial learning rate and \(\alpha\) controls the rate of decay.

A related technique involves **layer-wise learning rates**, where different parts of the network are updated at different speeds:

- Lower layers (general features): Very small learning rate or frozen
- Middle layers (domain adaptation): Moderate learning rate
- Upper layers (task-specific): Standard learning rate

This approach helps preserve the general linguistic knowledge learned during pre-training while still allowing the model to adapt to the specific demands of financial tasks.

### 3.3.3 Sample efficiency and few-shot learning

Financial institutions often have limited labeled data for specific tasks. As a result, fine-tuning must be sample-efficient, achieving strong performance with hundreds or thousands of examples rather than millions.

One reason this is possible is that **pre-training provides an inductive bias**. Pre-trained models begin with strong priors about language structure and semantics, which significantly reduces the amount of task-specific data required during adaptation. Instead of learning language and the downstream task simultaneously, the model can reuse the linguistic knowledge acquired during pre-training and focus on learning the financial task itself.

Another commonly used strategy is **data augmentation**, which expands limited labeled datasets through synthetic data generation:

- Paraphrasing: Generate equivalent expressions ("revenue grew" → "sales increased")
- Back-translation: Translate to another language and back
- Mixup: Interpolate between training examples
- LLM-based generation: Use larger models to generate additional training examples

A related direction involves **few-shot meta-learning**. Techniques such as MAML (Model-Agnostic Meta-Learning) train models to adapt quickly from only a handful of examples. Although meta-learning approaches are less common in financial applications due to their computational cost, they remain promising for institutions that manage many related tasks but have limited labeled data for each individual task.

With these theoretical considerations in mind, we now turn to the practical mechanics of adapting language models to financial tasks, beginning with the most direct approach: full fine-tuning. This method provides the baseline against which more efficient adaptation strategies, such as PEFT, supervised task-specific training pipelines, and alignment techniques like RLHF, can be understood.

## 3.4 Full fine-tuning methodology

Full fine-tuning represents the most direct method for adapting a pre-trained language model to a specific financial task. In this approach, all model parameters are updated using domain-specific training data, allowing the model to fully adjust its internal representations to the linguistic patterns, numerical relationships, and domain conventions found in financial text. Although this method can be computationally demanding, it often provides the strongest performance when accuracy and task specialization are critical.
