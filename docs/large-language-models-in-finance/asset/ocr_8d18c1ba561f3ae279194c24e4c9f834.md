where $f_i$ captures individual dynamics, $g_{ij}$ captures interaction, and $\eta_i(t)$ represents external shocks and model error. The equation is deliberately general: interaction may occur through prices, order books, common funding constraints, shared data, or direct communication.

Let $x^*$ be an equilibrium and let $z = x - x^*$. Linearization gives

$$\dot{z}(t) = Jz(t), \quad J = \frac{\partial F(x)}{\partial x}\bigg|_{x=x^*},$$ (14.28)

where the off-diagonal blocks of $J$ encode cross-agent coupling.

# **Proposition 14.4.1** (Local Spectral Stability). *For the deterministic linearized system* $\dot{z} = Jz$, *the equilibrium is locally exponentially stable when*

$$\alpha(J) := \max_k \text{Re } \lambda_k(J) < 0,$$ (14.29)

*and it is unstable when* $\alpha(J) > 0$.

*Proof.* The solution is $z(t) = e^{Jt}z(0)$. If all eigenvalues have strictly negative real parts, the matrix exponential decays exponentially; if at least one eigenvalue has positive real part, perturbations along the corresponding invariant direction grow.

The proposition does not imply that more agents or more connectivity are always destabilizing. Connectivity can diversify small shocks and propagate large shocks, producing the familiar “robust-yet-fragile” pattern studied in financial networks [Haldane and May, 2011, Acemoglu et al., 2015]. In an agentic market, greater strategic homogeneity or stronger feedback can move the spectral abscissa toward zero, slowing recovery from shocks, or above zero, creating local amplification. This spectral condition replaces an unsupported universal probability law with a testable stability diagnostic.
