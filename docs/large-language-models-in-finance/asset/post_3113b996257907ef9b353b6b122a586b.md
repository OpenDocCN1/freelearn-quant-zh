
```
# 3. Summarizer agent
summary = llm.summarize_company(
    ticker=ticker,
    docs=context_docs,
    pricing=pricing
)

# 4. Risk agent
risk_report = llm.analyze_risks(
    ticker=ticker,
    docs=context_docs,
    risk_data=risk_info
)

# 5. Thesis agent
thesis = llm.write_investment_thesis(
    ticker=ticker,
    question=question,
    summary=summary,
    risk_report=risk_report
)

return {
    "summary": summary,
    "risk_report": risk_report,
    "thesis": thesis
}
```

In a production environment, each function call to the LLM would:

- include clear system and user prompts [Reynolds and McDonnell, 2021],
- be constrained by tool schemas and governance rules,
- log all intermediate artifacts for later review.

> **Final-phase update: Governed agentic trading**
> For trading workflows, a multi-agent workstation should not be read as an LLM that directly emits buy or sell orders. A minimal governed design separates five planes: (i) an **evidence plane** for point-in-time data, documents, timestamps, and provenance; (ii) a **cognition plane** for specialist agents, forecasts, scenarios, and research memory; (iii) a **control plane** for mandates, authority budgets, constraints, approvals, and kill states; (iv) an **execution plane** for intent-to-order compilation, venue adapters, pre-trade gates, fills, and reconciliation; and (v) an **assurance plane** for validation, shadow evaluation, monitoring, incident response, and immutable audit. This is a minimal addition to the chapter's front-office architecture: LLMs are most naturally placed in slow cognition and explanation, while market access remains in deterministic control and execution services [Noguer i Alonso and Pacheco Azmar, 2026].

## 7.2 Middle office: Risk management and surveillance

While front-office applications focus on market opportunities and trading decisions, middle-office applications focus on measuring, monitoring, and controlling risk. Middle-office functions translate trading and banking activities into risk profiles, limits, and regulatory reports. LLMs assist by structuring free-text information, generating scenarios, and prioritizing human attention.

### 7.2.1 Credit risk and early-warning signals

Credit risk analysis relies on:

- financial statements and notes [Altman, 1968, Ohlson, 1980],
- management commentary and guidance [Mayew et al., 2015],
- rating-agency reports [Becker and Milbourn, 2011],
- covenants in loan agreements and indentures [Chava and Roberts, 2008],
- sector and macroeconomic analysis [Duffie et al., 2007].

LLMs help by:

- extracting key ratios and textual signals from credit memos [Mai et al., 2019, Netzer et al., 2019, Berg et al., 2020],
- mapping narrative risk descriptions into standardized tags,
- monitoring news and filings for early-warning indicators (e.g. legal disputes, regulatory investigations, downgrades) [Campbell et al., 2008].

A systematic setup might maintain, for each obligor:

- 1. a structured profile summarizing business model, leverage, liquidity, and collateral;
- 2. a stream of narrative events categorized by risk type;
- 3. early-warning scores derived from both structured and textual factors [Bharath and Shumway, 2008].

### Implementation sketch: Covenant extraction

Loan and bond documents often contain covenants that are crucial for credit risk: leverage caps, coverage ratios, restrictions on asset sales, cross-default clauses, and so on. Extracting these covenants consistently is a natural task for document NLP, but production systems need clause-level evidence, defined calculation rules, and legal review. Public contract benchmarks such as CUAD and ContractNLI provide useful starting points for extraction and document-level inference [Hendrycks et al., 2021, Koreeda and Manning, 2021].

### Listing 7.4: Simple covenant extraction from loan agreements

```
# Illustrative template. Not production code.
from transformers import AutoTokenizer, AutoModelForTokenClassification
import torch
model_name = "approved-legal-ner-model" # replace with the reviewed model identifier
tokenizer = AutoTokenizer.from_pretrained(model_name)
ner_model = AutoModelForTokenClassification.from_pretrained(model_name)
id2label = ner_model.config.id2label
def extract_covenants(text):
    tokens = tokenizer(
        text,
        return_tensors="pt",
        truncation=True
    )
    with torch.no_grad():
        out = ner_model(**tokens)
    logits = out.logits[0]
    preds = torch.argmax(logits, dim=-1).cpu().numpy()
    words = tokenizer.convert_ids_to_tokens(tokens["input_ids"][0])
    covenants = []
    current = []
    for w, p in zip(words, preds):
        label = id2label[p]
        if label.startswith("B-Covenant"):
            if current:
                covenants.append(" ".join(current))
                current = []
            current.append(w)
        elif label.startswith("I-Covenant") and current:
            current.append(w)
        else:
            if current:
                covenants.append(" ".join(current))
                current = []
    if current:
        covenants.append(" ".join(current))
    return covenants
```

In practice, covenant extraction would be combined with:

- core credit models (PD/LGD/EAD) [Basel Committee on Banking Supervision, 2005],
- collateral and exposure data,
- workflow tools for analysts to review and correct extracted information.

### 7.2.2 Market risk and scenario analysis

Market risk is the risk of losses from changes in prices, rates, spreads, currencies, or volatility. Market risk management uses models such as Value-at-Risk (VaR) and Expected Shortfall (ES) alongside stress testing and scenario analysis. VaR estimates a loss threshold at a given confidence level; Expected Shortfall estimates the average loss beyond that threshold. [Jorion, 2007, McNeil et al., 2015]. LLMs contribute by:

- generating narrative descriptions of stress scenarios [Hirtle and Lehnert, 2015],
- mapping narratives to shocks on risk factors (rates, spreads, FX, equity levels and volatilities, commodities),
- explaining why particular positions or portfolios are vulnerable.

A typical scenario pipeline:

- 1. define a qualitative scenario (e.g. “sharp, unexpected tightening by the central bank due to inflation surprises"),
- 2. use an LLM to elaborate the scenario into a set of coherent macro events [Schorfheide et al., 2018],
- 3. map those events into shocks on curves and spreads [Diebold and Li, 2006],
- 4. run the shocks through a pricing/risk engine,
- 5. use the LLM to summarize the impact by desk, product, and risk factor.

### 7.2.3 Real-time market surveillance

Market-surveillance systems monitor trading activity for potential market abuse (manipulation, spoofing, layering, and insider trading). They generate large volumes of alerts based on statistical triggers and rule-based filters [Cumming et al., 2020, Aitken et al., 2015].

LLMs assist by:

- analyzing trader communications (emails, chats) to identify narratives consistent with suspicious behavior,
- summarizing and contextualizing alerts for human investigators,
- clustering similar alerts and highlighting patterns across desks, instruments, or time [Golbeck et al., 2018].

Here, LLMs primarily act as *triage engines*, not as decision-makers. Human investigators remain responsible for final decisions and regulatory filings.

## 7.3 Back office: Banking, fraud, AML, and operations

Back-office applications are less focused on real-time trading decisions and more focused on accurate processing, documentation, investigation, and compliance support. Back-office functions include credit underwriting, transaction monitoring, KYC/AML processes, and a wide variety of operational workflows. These processes generate and consume large volumes of structured data, unstructured text, and documents [Arner et al., 2017].

### 7.3.1 Credit underwriting and review

In retail and small and medium-sized enterprise (SME) banking, credit underwriting typically combines:

- structured data (income, balance, transaction history, bureau scores) [Thomas et al., 2017],
- document-based information (pay slips, tax returns, financial statements),
- narrative information (business plans, explanations of purpose of funds).

LLMs can:

- extract structured features from documents (income, expenses, collateral) [Huang et al., 2022, Kim et al., 2022],
- summarize applicant profiles into standardized credit memos,
- flag inconsistencies or missing information [Netzer et al., 2019].

The goal is to augment, not replace, established credit-scoring models. LLM-derived features feed into existing PD/LGD frameworks and manual credit committees [Berg et al., 2020].
