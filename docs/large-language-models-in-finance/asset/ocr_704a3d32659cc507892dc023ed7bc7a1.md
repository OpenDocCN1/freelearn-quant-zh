+   2. Sample efficiency: Financial data can be expensive, restricted, noisy, or limited in coverage. RL methods therefore need to learn efficiently from fewer examples and avoid overfitting to narrow historical conditions. Offline-RL research emphasizes that apparent value estimates can be unreliable when a learned policy chooses actions poorly represented in the dataset [Levine et al., 2020, Kumar et al., 2020].

+   3. Non-stationarity: Financial markets change over time as regimes, regulations, liquidity, and participant behavior evolve. Models trained on past data may become unreliable if they are not monitored and updated carefully.

+   4. Interpretability: Financial institutions often need to explain decisions, recommendations, and risk assessments. RL-trained systems must therefore provide traceable reasoning, auditability, and evidence for their outputs.

+   5. Safety and robustness: Financial outputs can affect monetary decisions, compliance obligations, and client outcomes. Systems need guardrails, validation, stress testing, fallback behavior, and human oversight. Principle-guided critique and revision are one research direction, but they supplement rather than replace external controls [Bai et al., 2022].

These challenges also point to active areas of research. In financial LLMs, future work is likely to focus on methods that make RL safer, more data-efficient, and easier to govern.

# 9.6.2 Future research directions

Two promising directions are offline RL, which learns from existing historical data rather than live interaction [Levine et al., 2020, Kumar et al., 2020], and constitutional AI, which uses explicit principles to critique and revise model behavior [Bai et al., 2022].

# Offline RL for historical data

Offline RL trains policies from previously collected data rather than through live interaction with an environment. This is especially relevant in finance because experimenting directly in real markets can be costly, risky, or impractical. The central technical problem is distribution shift: a policy may select actions outside the support of the historical behavior policy, where value estimates are extrapolations rather than evidence-backed estimates [Levine et al., 2020]. Conservative Q-learning addresses this problem by lowering values assigned to unsupported actions [Kumar et al., 2020].
