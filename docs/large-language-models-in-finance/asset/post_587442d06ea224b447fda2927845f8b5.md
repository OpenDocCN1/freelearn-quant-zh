

- **Human-in-the-loop behavior**: Measure how human analysts interact with the model: override rates, escalation patterns, and whether the model reduces or creates manual workload.

We recommend maintaining a living “model evaluation card” for each production model that records datasets, metrics, thresholds, and governance sign-offs, aligned with the institution’s broader model risk management framework.

## 3.8.6 Model risk management and regulatory compliance

In regulated financial institutions, deploying a fine-tuned language model is not purely a machine-learning problem; it is also a scoping, governance, and accountability problem. The governing framework depends on the institution, the use case, the system’s function, and the applicable legal and supervisory perimeter.

**U.S. scope and status.** On 17 April 2026, the Federal Reserve, FDIC, and OCC issued SR 26-2, *Revised Guidance on Model Risk Management* superseding SR 11-7 and SR 21-8. The guidance is non-binding: non-compliance with it alone does not constitute a violation of law or a basis for supervisory criticism. It is expected to be most relevant to banking organizations with more than $30 billion in total assets, while smaller organizations apply it in a manner proportionate to their risk profile and the nature and materiality of their model use. The revised definition also excludes tools that perform only simple arithmetic and software whose outputs are generated deterministically from fixed rules [Board of Governors of the Federal Reserve System et al., 2026].

The scope boundary is critical for this book. Footnote 3 of SR 26-2 states that generative-AI and agentic-AI models are novel and rapidly evolving and are *not within the scope of the guidance*. The same footnote makes two qualifications: an institution’s own governance and risk-management practices should still determine controls for systems outside the guidance, and the guidance continues to apply to traditional statistical and quantitative models and to non-generative, non-agentic AI models. A discriminative FinBERT-style classifier, scorer, or tagger may therefore remain in scope when it satisfies the revised model definition and is used in a relevant banking process; a generative LLM or agent does not become an SR 26-2 model merely because it is fine-tuned for finance. Such a system should not be described as “SR 26-2 compliant.”

Composition creates an important dependency without expanding the formal scope. If a generative LLM supplies features, narratives, extracted values, or decisions to an in-scope quantitative model, validation of the downstream model should examine the quality, stability, controls, and failure modes of that upstream input. The dependency belongs in the system inventory and evidence package even though the generative component remains outside SR 26-2’s stated perimeter.

For models that are within scope, the revised guidance is best summarized through four risk-based practices:

- **Determine applicability before borrowing language.** Apply the revised model definition, intended use, materiality, and institutional perimeter before labeling a system or control package.
- **Validate through rigor and effective challenge.** The quality of validation depends on the rigor and effectiveness of the review, not on a prescribed organizational chart. Independence, competence, authority, and documented challenge should be proportionate to risk.
- **Use a materiality-based monitoring cadence.** Validation and monitoring frequency should respond to model change, performance, use, and risk rather than defaulting automatically to annual revalidation.
- **Inventory models and dependencies.** Documentation should identify third-party components, upstream data and AI systems, downstream decisions, limitations, change triggers, and the controls that contain misuse.

European frameworks include the EBA Guidelines on internal governance (EBA/GL/2021/05), the European Central Bank’s guide to internal models for relevant prudential uses, and the EU AI Act’s risk-based requirements. The AI Act does not make every financial use high-risk: classification depends on the system’s intended purpose and the categories specified in the regulation. Its obligations also apply on a phased timetable, so legal and compliance teams must verify the relevant role, use case, and application date rather than rely on a generic “AI system” label [European Parliament and Council of the European Union, 2024].

In practice, a financial LLM deployment should undergo a documented scope determination and a proportionate system review. The evidence package should:

- state whether each component is generative, agentic, discriminative, deterministic, or a traditional quantitative model, and identify the framework that actually applies;
- document training data, fine-tuning method, retrieval and tool dependencies, intended use, prohibited use, known limitations, and human approval boundaries;
- report numerical accuracy, temporal consistency, factual grounding, calibration, robustness, security, and override or escalation outcomes at a level proportionate to materiality;
- define quantitative thresholds, monitoring triggers, incident procedures, and a tested fallback or rollback path; and
- treat changes to the base model, data, prompts, tools, retrieval index, or fine-tuning configuration as governed version changes whose review depth depends on their risk significance.

This governance infrastructure is a condition of controlled deployment rather than an administrative afterthought. The “model evaluation card” recommended in the finance-specific evaluation checklist above is a natural artifact for documenting formal scope, analogous principles, evidence, thresholds, approvals, dependencies, and ongoing monitoring under the applicable framework.

## 3.9 Case studies: How financial LLMs are actually built

The three case studies in this section bring together the methods discussed throughout the chapter and show how they have been applied to financial NLP problems at different scales. Each illustrates a different point on the spectrum from targeted fine-tuning to full domain-specific pre-training. Taken together, they support a more cautious thesis: domain adaptation often improves performance on matched financial tasks, but the size and reliability of the gain depend on the data, base model, objective, and evaluation design.

### 3.9.1 BloombergGPT: Domain-specific pre-training and fine-tuning

BloombergGPT [Wu et al., 2023b] is a prominent publicly documented effort to create a finance-specific LLM from the ground up. Rather than starting from a general-purpose pre-trained model and fine-tuning, Bloomberg pre-trained a 50.6B-parameter model from scratch on a mixed financial and general-domain corpus. The rationale was that while fine-tuning adapts an existing model's representations, domain-specific pre-training allows the model to build financial knowledge into its foundational representations from the first layer up—a potentially more thorough form of specialization, at the cost of enormous computational investment.

The published model and training design can be summarized as follows:

- 50.6 billion parameters in a decoder-only Transformer;
- a corpus of more than 700 billion tokens, combining financial and general-domain sources;
- 569 billion training tokens sampled from that corpus;
- a reported compute budget of approximately 1.3 million A100 GPU-hours on 512 NVIDIA A100 40 GB GPUs; and
- evaluation across financial and general NLP benchmarks, with the strongest gains concentrated in finance-specific tasks [Wu et al., 2023b].
