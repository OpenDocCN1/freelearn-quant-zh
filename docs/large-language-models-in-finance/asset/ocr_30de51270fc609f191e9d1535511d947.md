# 11.10 Selected standards, regulatory sources, and research resources

The following annotated reference map covers the principal resources used in this chapter and gives practitioners a structured starting point for implementation. It complements the book's full bibliography.

**U.S. financial model-risk management (scope-limited).** SR 26-2, Revised Guidance on Model Risk Management, supersedes SR 11-7 and SR 21-8. It is non-binding, is expected to be most relevant above $30 billion in total assets, and expressly excludes generative-AI and agentic-AI models while continuing to apply to traditional quantitative and non-generative, non-agentic AI models that meet its definition. It is therefore not a generative-AI compliance standard; use it directly only after formal scope analysis [Board of Governors of the Federal Reserve System et al., 2026].

**Cross-sector AI risk management.** NIST's AI RMF organizes work around Govern, Map, Measure, and Manage, while the Generative AI Profile adds risks and actions specific to generative systems [National Institute of Standards and Technology, 2023, 2024].

**Financial-stability perspective.** The FSB's 2024 report analyzes AI-related vulnerabilities, and its 2026 consultation report proposes sound practices for organization-wide governance and the AI lifecycle in financial institutions [Financial Stability Board, 2024, 2026].

**European Union.** Regulation (EU) 2024/1689 provides the AI Act's risk-based obligations, including the specific Annex III financial and insurance uses that may be classified as high-risk [European Parliament and Council of the European Union, 2024].

**United Kingdom financial services.** The FCA's current approach remains outcomes-focused, principles-based, and technology-neutral, applying existing regulatory frameworks to AI-enabled financial services [Financial Conduct Authority, 2025].

**AI-washing and truthful claims.** The SEC's 2024 Delphia and Global Predictions actions provide a documented financial-services example of enforcement involving false or misleading AI-related representations [U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission, 2024].

**Experimental financial markets with LLM agents.** Henning et al., LopezLira, Ouyang and Sui, and Anand et al. provide complementary controlled evidence on behavioral divergence, prompt-conditioned strategies, market bubbles, and system-design effects [Henning et al., 2025, Lopez-Lira, 2025, Ouyang and Sui, 2026, Anand et al., 2026].

**Algorithmic interaction and coordination.** Calvano et al. provide a foundational controlled study of tacitly collusive behavior among autonomous pricing algorithms; the result is not LLM-specific but is relevant to common-mode and interaction risk [Calvano et al., 2020].
