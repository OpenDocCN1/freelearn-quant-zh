
practice, production-grade systems mitigate these failures by combining LLM-based extraction with symbolic consistency checks and retrieval-augmented verification against authoritative sources such as original regulatory filings [Gao et al., 2023b, Chen et al., 2023].

The ability to reason about numbers as real quantities, rather than as textual artifacts, defines one of the most active frontiers in financial AI. Models that lack this capability may remain rhetorically impressive, but they will remain fundamentally unreliable for financial analysis.

### 1.10.2 Interpreting financial data

Yet financial reasoning doesn't stop at getting the math right. Numbers only gain meaning when they're linked to the text and rules that define how financial information fits together. That's where interpretation begins. At the core, LLMs convert sequences of tokens into high-dimensional embeddings:

```
$h_i = E_{w_{i,:}} \in \mathbb{R}^d, \quad \text{equivalently} \quad H = XE \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times d}, \quad (1.21)$
```

where $E \in \mathbb{R}^{|V| \times d}$ is the embedding matrix, $w_i \in \{1, \dots, |V|\}$ is a token index, $E_{w_{i,:}}$ is the corresponding row, and $X \in \{0, 1\}^{n \times |V|}$ stacks the one-hot rows of the sequence. The lookup is a row selection, not a matrix product against the token ids themselves — the ids are indices, and multiplying by them would be meaningless. These embeddings capture semantic and syntactic information but not exact numerical meaning.

Financial reasoning demands additional structure:

- Accounting identities: Balance sheets must satisfy

Assets = Liabilities + Equity.

- No-arbitrage conditions: In a frictionless one-period market with no arbitrage, an asset paying cash flow $D_{t+1}$ satisfies

$S_t = \mathbb{E}_t^{\mathbb{Q}} \left[ \frac{S_{t+1} + D_{t+1}}{1 + r_{f,t}} \right]$.

where $\mathbb{Q}$ is a risk-neutral measure, $\mathbb{E}_t^{\mathbb{Q}}$ is conditional on information at $t$, and $r_{f,t}$ is the one-period risk-free rate known at $t$. Three qualifications are routinely dropped and all three matter: the cash flow $D_{t+1}$ cannot be omitted for a dividend-paying asset; the expectation is *conditional*, not unconditional; and the relation presumes frictionless trading. Equivalently, the discounted cum-dividend price is a $\mathbb{Q}$-martingale. This is a consistency condition a financial LLM's output must respect, not a general pricing formula.

- Cross-modal reasoning: Modern multimodal models (e.g., Gemini) can integrate text, tables, and charts, producing more consistent reasoning across data formats.

Thus, financial LLMs are increasingly built as *hybrid systems* rather than standalone models.

Once models can connect structured data with narrative context, the next step is using those insights to inform decisions.

### 1.10.3 Decision-making in financial AI

Financial reasoning ultimately supports decision-making under uncertainty. The objective is not only to summarize but also to recommend actions aligned with risk-return trade-offs. A general framework is:

$$ \max_{\pi \in \Pi} \mathbb{E}[U(W_{t+1}) \mid \pi], \quad (1.24) $$

where $\pi$ denotes an investment policy, $U(\cdot)$ a utility function, and $W_{t+1}$ future wealth.

In practice:

- LLMs perform **information synthesis** from filings, news, and transcripts.
- Extracted features feed into quantitative models (e.g., VaR, credit risk scoring).
- Final decisions remain subject to human or algorithmic validation, ensuring compliance with institutional risk limits.

Hence, LLMs act primarily as reasoning *assistants*, not autonomous traders.

### 1.10.4 Limitations and biases in financial AI

Even as the preceding capabilities of LLMs mature, they remain imperfect. As a final discussion, let’s look at the biases and interpretability gaps that still constrain how confidently financial institutions can rely on LLM-based reasoning systems:

- **Bias and staleness:** Models inherit historical market patterns and may fail in new regimes or “black swan” events.
- **Interpretability:** Regulators demand explanations, but attention weights and embeddings provide limited transparency.
- **Hallucinations:** LLMs sometimes fabricate plausible but false numbers, a severe risk in regulated contexts.
- **Robustness:** Models generalize poorly across different jurisdictions, asset classes, or stress scenarios.

Research directions include mechanistic interpretability, symbolic enforcement of financial constraints, and continuous learning with real-time market data.

These limitations remind us that while LLMs can extend human reasoning, they still depend on human oversight and continual recalibration to remain reliable in the dynamic world of finance.

## 1.11 Case study wrap-up: Earnings call assistant

Throughout this chapter, we have seen each concept instantiated in the earnings call assistant. It is worth pausing to map the full pipeline against what we have covered.

The assistant begins by tokenizing the raw transcript and converting tokens to contextual embeddings via a pre-trained Transformer. Positional encodings ensure that the model preserves the sequential structure of the call—knowing that the CFO speaks after the CEO matters. Multi-head self-attention then resolves long-range dependencies. Precisely which tokens may attend to which depends on the mask: in a bidirectional encoder such as BERT every token attends to every other token, whereas in a decoder-only model a causal mask restricts each position to itself and the positions before it. For an earnings-call assistant this is not a detail — an encoder scoring sentiment may read the whole transcript at once, while a decoder drafting the summary sees only what it has already written. Either way, attention resolves the long-range dependencies that n-gram models could not handle.

The choice of pre-trained base model shapes each output module: a BERT-style encoder scores management sentiment; a T5 or GPT-style decoder generates the free-text summary; and a lightweight classification head, trained with a supervised cross-entropy objective over labelled guidance-revision examples, flags guidance revisions. The distinction matters and is worth stating once here, because it is a common source of confusion: masked language modelling is the pre-training objective that produced the encoder's representations. It is not the objective used to train a downstream head. The head learns from labels, not from masked tokens. RLHF alignment ensures the summaries match analyst preferences rather than generic text quality.

Finally, the assistant's numerical reasoning module, combining LLM extraction with symbolic consistency checks, validates that reported EPS figures in the transcript are internally consistent, mitigating the quantitative hallucination risk identified in Section 1.10.

The result is a system designed to reduce analyst processing time while surfacing the signals most relevant to portfolio decision-making. The actual benefit must be measured against a documented human baseline. Every architectural choice discussed in this chapter has a direct counterpart in this pipeline.

## 1.12 Summary

This chapter traced the evolution of language models from their statistical beginnings to the deep learning architectures that underpin today's financial AI systems. The transition from n-gram models to attention-based Transformers marked a turning point, enabling models to capture long-range context, semantic nuance, and domain-specific dependencies that statistical methods could not. Pre-trained architectures such as GPT, BERT, and T5 established a flexible foundation, introducing training paradigms like autoregressive and masked language modeling that remain central to modern NLP.

Within finance, these general frameworks have been adapted to the distinct demands of the domain. Models like FinBERT, BloombergGPT, and FinEAS illustrate how domain-specific fine-tuning and embedding strategies can extract meaningful sentiment, surface hidden risks, and reconcile structured and unstructured data. At the same time, institutions continue to rely on powerful general-purpose models such as GPT-4, Claude, Gemini, Llama, and Mistral as the architectural backbone for specialized financial applications.
