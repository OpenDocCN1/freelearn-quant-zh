# 3.8 Evaluation and validation

Rigorous evaluation is not a formality that happens after the interesting work is done—it is an integral part of the fine-tuning process that determines whether a model is safe to deploy. This is especially true in finance because errors in production are not just embarrassing; they can affect investment decisions, trigger compliance violations, or introduce systematic bias into credit and risk assessments. A model that achieves strong benchmark scores but fails on out-of-distribution inputs, hallucinates figures under time pressure, or degrades silently as market regimes shift presents serious operational risk.

This section organizes evaluation into three layers that complement one another. Offline metrics measure performance on held-out labeled data before deployment. Financial-specific validation tests properties that standard NLP benchmarks do not cover—numerical precision, temporal consistency, and factual grounding. Robustness and A/B testing extend evaluation into production, where the model faces the full complexity of real-world inputs and user behavior.

We start with the most familiar layer of evaluation: performance on held-out data.

# 3.8.1 Offline evaluation metrics

The right metric depends on the task type. Classification, generation, and numerical reasoning each call for a different primary measure, and using the wrong one can give a misleading picture of model quality. A model with high accuracy on a class-imbalanced sentiment dataset may simply be predicting the majority class most of the time; a model with high ROUGE on a summarization benchmark may be extracting surface phrases without understanding their meaning.

**Classification tasks** are typically evaluated using accuracy, precision, recall, and F1 score, with per-class metrics providing additional visibility in imbalanced datasets. Tools such as confusion matrices and ROC curves with AUC further help diagnose where the model is performing well and where it is systematically making errors.

**Generation tasks** require a different set of metrics that capture both surface-level and semantic quality. Perplexity measures how well the model fits the test data, while BLEU and ROUGE assess n-gram overlap with reference outputs. BERTScore complements these by capturing semantic similarity, and in practice, human evaluation remains essential to judge coherence, factual accuracy, and overall usefulness.

**Numerical tasks**, which are common in finance, demand metrics that reflect both precision and tolerance. Exact match accuracy is useful for strict correctness, while tolerance-based measures (such as ±1% or ±5%) distinguish between minor rounding differences and meaningful errors. Mean absolute error and root mean squared error provide additional insight into the magnitude and distribution of prediction errors.
