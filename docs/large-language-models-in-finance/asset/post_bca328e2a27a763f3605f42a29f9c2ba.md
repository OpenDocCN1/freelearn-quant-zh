
during pre-training, the system first retrieves relevant documents, passages, tables, or filings and then conditions generation on that retrieved evidence [Lewis et al., 2020].

A compact abstraction is:

```
\hat{y} = G_\theta(x, R_k(x; \mathcal{K}_t)), \quad (2.31)
```

where x is the user query, K_t is the time-indexed knowledge base available at time t, R_k retrieves the top-k relevant evidence units, and G_\theta is the generator. The time index matters in finance: a response about a company's leverage, capital adequacy, or risk factors should depend on the filings, market data, and policies that were valid at the relevant date.

A practical financial RAG pipeline has five stages:

- 1. **Ingestion**: collect filings, research reports, policies, market commentary, and structured data feeds with metadata such as publication date, fiscal period, source, permission level, and document type.
- 2. **Chunking and indexing**: split documents into coherent passages or table units, embed them, and store them in a vector, keyword, or hybrid search index.
- 3. **Retrieval and reranking**: retrieve candidate evidence using semantic and lexical search, then rerank by relevance, recency, authority, and access permissions.
- 4. **Grounded generation**: pass the selected evidence to the model and require the answer to cite or summarize only the retrieved material.
- 5. **Validation and logging**: check citations, numerical consistency, access rights, latency, and audit records before the answer enters a regulated workflow.

RAG is especially important for financial systems because it reduces reliance on stale parametric memory. For example, if a user asks what changed between two annual reports, the system should retrieve both the current and prior 10-K, preserve their filing dates, compare the relevant sections, and state which version supports each claim. This design also helps with document-level auditability: the answer can be traced back to a specific filing, paragraph, table, or policy record.

RAG, however, is not a complete solution to hallucination or alignment. It improves factual grounding, but the system can still retrieve the wrong document, misread a table, overstate a conclusion, or ignore a compliance constraint. For this reason, alignment and hallucination control enter the architecture at several layers: retrieval filters, permission checks, prompt constraints, citation requirements, numerical validators, human review queues, and post-generation monitoring. In regulated financial deployments, RAG should therefore be viewed as one component of a broader governed generation stack rather than a standalone guarantee of truth.

With prompting, retrieval, and adaptation in place, we can now turn to a more fundamental question—how such a system is built in the first place.

## 2.6 How to build an LLM from scratch

Building LLMs optimized for financial applications represents the ultimate expression of domain specialization. It allows for the optimization of every aspect of model design, from tokenization strategies to loss functions, specifically for financial use cases [Brown et al., 2020]. The decision to build a financial LLM from scratch is rarely taken lightly. It typically arises when fine-tuning an existing foundation model is insufficient: when financial terminology is poorly represented in the base vocabulary, when proprietary analytical frameworks must be deeply integrated, when regulatory requirements demand architectural control, or when specific financial workflows benefit from structural innovations.

The purpose of this final section is to examine what that level of specialization actually entails. We move beyond prompting and fine-tuning to explore the full lifecycle of constructing a financial LLM: curating and preprocessing domain-specific datasets, designing specialized tokenization strategies, selecting training architectures and optimization methods, defining financial-specific loss functions, and building the infrastructure required to scale, secure, and monitor such systems in production environments.

### 2.6.1 Dataset preparation and tokenization

If a financial LLM is to be built from scratch, everything begins with the data. As we've seen earlier in this chapter, the scope, structure, and quality of the training corpus directly determine how well the model will reason about markets, regulation, valuation, and risk.

Constructing such a dataset requires deliberate curation across the full breadth of financial knowledge, while maintaining rigorous standards of accuracy and professional integrity, which goes beyond simply assembling a large volume of financial text.

### Constructing the financial corpus

The objective is to represent the different ways finance is analyzed, reported, and regulated. Quality assessment frameworks for financial content require specialized evaluation criteria that extend beyond generic web-scale filtering. Accuracy, completeness, bias, and adherence to professional standards must be assessed explicitly. In practice, this involves verifying numerical data and financial calculations, checking that analysis follows recognized frameworks and ethical guidelines, ensuring the information is current and relevant to prevailing market conditions, evaluating the credibility of the source, and assessing whether the analysis demonstrates sufficient depth and logical consistency.

Deduplication strategies for financial content must account for the legitimate repetition inherent in financial reporting while removing truly redundant information. Financial documents often contain similar language and standardized disclosures that serve important legal functions. For this reason, deduplication requires approaches that preserve meaningful repetition while still eliminating unnecessary duplication.

In this context, near-duplicate detection for financial content employs semantic similarity measures combined with structural analysis to identify documents that convey identical information through different language or formatting. The following example illustrates how you may combine these signals in a systematic deduplication process.

```
Algorithm 3 Financial document deduplication
**Input:** Document collection $D$, similarity threshold $\tau$
**Output:** Deduplicated collection $D'$

embeddings \(\leftarrow\) GenerateEmbeddings(D)
structural_features \(\leftarrow\) ExtractStructure(D)
temporal_metadata \(\leftarrow\) ExtractTemporalMetadata(D)

for each pair \((d_i, d_j) \in D \times D\) do
  sem_sim \(\leftarrow\) CosineSimilarity(embeddings[i], embeddings[j])
  struct_sim \(\leftarrow\) StructuralSimilarity(structural_features[i], structural_features[j])
  combined_sim \(\leftarrow\alpha \cdot\) sem_sim \(+\beta \cdot\) struct_sim
  if IsRevisedFiling($d_i, d_j$, temporal_metadata) then
    Keep the newer filing as current and link the older filing as historical evidence
  else if combined_sim \(>\tau\) AND Quality($d_i$) < Quality($d_j$) then
    Remove $d_i$ from collection
  end if
end for

return $D'$
```

Temporal metadata is essential in this step. If a company files a revision, amendment, or correction, the newer version should not be discarded merely because it is highly similar to the older filing. A robust pipeline keeps the current version for downstream analysis, preserves the superseded version for auditability, and records the relationship between the two.

While deduplication improves corpus quality, regulatory handling determines whether the corpus can be used at all. Privacy- and compliance-preprocessing ensures that training data meets applicable requirements while preserving analytical utility. Financial data often contains personally identifiable information, confidential business information, or material non-public information that must be carefully handled during corpus preparation.

### Designing specialized financial tokenization

As discussed in earlier sections, financial text differs from general-domain text along several recurring dimensions, including numerical density, specialized terminology, structured data formats, temporal references, and dense use of entity names. Accordingly, financial tokenization requires specialized approaches that preserve the semantic integrity of financial concepts while efficiently handling these diverse data types. Standard tokenization methods often fragment important financial terms or fail to capture numerical relationships that are central to financial analysis. For this reason, tokenization strategy design must begin with a careful analysis of financial vocabulary and document structure.

Subword tokenization for financial content therefore requires careful adaptation of algorithms such as Byte Pair Encoding (BPE) or SentencePiece to preserve financial concept integrity.
