
The practical meaning is straightforward. Autonomy should increase only while its marginal benefit exceeds the combined marginal implementation and risk costs. A task with reversible, low-value actions may justify a higher \(\alpha^*\) than a task involving customer suitability, capital allocation, or market-moving orders. Better validation, clearer permissions, stronger sandboxing, and reliable human escalation can reduce \(R'(\alpha)\) and permit more automation. Opaque behavior, correlated models, weak observability, or irreversible actions push the appropriate level of autonomy downward. The optimum is therefore task-specific and dynamic, not a universal number for the institution. The path forward requires robust governance and a sustained commitment to responsible innovation. Financial institutions must adapt rules, supervision, and infrastructure as capabilities change; deliberately monitor concentration in models and data; strengthen transparency for high-impact decisions; and observe markets as interacting ecosystems rather than collections of independent algorithms. Because agents can operate across jurisdictions, international standards for testing, incident reporting, and information sharing will also become increasingly important.

## 14.8 Evaluation contract for generative alpha systems

The most common error in evaluating generative alpha systems is to confuse a plausible narrative with a tradable signal. A generated thesis is not alpha unless it survives a decision-time protocol, a leakage audit, transaction-cost modeling, risk adjustment, and out-of-sample degradation analysis. The correct object is therefore not a text paragraph but a signed prediction record:

```
\(r_i = (t_i, \mathcal{I}_t, h_i, s_i, p_i, \ell_i, \text{audit}_i),\)
```

where \(t_i\) is the decision time, \(\mathcal{I}_t\) is the information set, \(h_i\) is the generated hypothesis, \(s_i\) is the tradable signal, \(p_i\) is the portfolio or action implied by the signal, \(\ell_i\) is the realized loss or utility, and \(\text{audit}_i\) records model, prompt, data, tool, and reviewer hashes.

A minimal evaluation uses three layers:

- **Predictive layer.** Does the generated signal predict a future target under a chronological split?
- **Economic layer.** Does the signal survive costs, capacity, turnover, risk limits, and drawdown constraints?
- **Governance layer.** Are the claims supported, the data licensed, the timestamps valid, and the actions authorized?

For a return target \(R_{i+1}\) and signal \(s_i\), the predictive layer may report information coefficient

```
\(\text{IC} = \text{Corr}(s_i, R_{i+1}),\)
```

while the economic layer reports cost-adjusted utility, for example

```
\(U = \mathbb{E}[p_i R_{i+1} - \text{TC}(p_i, p_{i-1})] - \frac{\gamma}{2} \text{Var}(p_i R_{i+1}).\)
```

The governance layer should be reported separately rather than hidden inside performance. A strategy with attractive backtest returns but repeated citation failures, timestamp leaks, or unauthorized tool use is not production-ready.

> **Final-phase update: governance as a risk envelope**
>
> The right question is not whether an agentic system produces a fluent trading narrative or a better headline backtest. The right question is whether the whole decision system improves after temporal integrity, costs, capacity, execution, stress, failure injection, governance, and replay are enforced. A governed agentic system may be economically valuable because it reduces volatility, tail loss, drawdown, operational error, or audit uncertainty, even when it does not dominate strong learned baselines on raw return or Sharpe ratio. This is why the chapter treats generative alpha as a hypothesis requiring evidence, while treating bounded authority and auditability as production requirements [Noguer i Alonso and Pacheco Aznar, 2026].

**Proposition 14.8.1** (Backtest admissibility under timestamped records). *If every prediction record* $r_i$ *is generated from artifacts available at* $t_i$, *the portfolio rule* $p_i = \phi(s_i, \mathcal{I}_{t_i})$ *is fixed before observing* $R_{i+1}$, *and transaction costs are computed from executable prices available after* $t_i$, *then the resulting backtest is free of direct lookahead through the signal-construction and cost-estimation channels.*

*Proof.* By assumption, $s_i$ is a function only of artifacts available at $t_i$. The portfolio rule is fixed before the future return is observed, so $p_i$ is measurable with respect to $\mathcal{I}_{t_i}$. Transaction costs use prices that would be available at execution after the decision time and are not used to revise the signal ex ante. Therefore neither the signal nor the cost estimate conditions on $R_{i+1}$ or later artifacts. As with all backtests, this does not eliminate selection bias, data-snooping, survivorship bias, or market-impact misspecification; those require additional controls and multiple-testing adjustments [Bailey et al., 2017].

## 14.9 Bounded autonomy as the right frontier

The frontier of financial agents should not be framed as a race from human decision-making to unconstrained machine autonomy. The technically and institutionally mature objective is *bounded autonomy*: the agent can plan, retrieve, calculate, and propose actions inside a formally defined action set, while external shields enforce limits and humans retain authority over material decisions.

Let $\mathcal{A}_{read}$ denote read-only actions, $\mathcal{A}_{write}$ denote state-changing actions, and $\mathcal{A}_{material}$ denote actions with direct client, market, regulatory, or balance-sheet impact. A conservative deployment path is

```
$\mathcal{A}_{read} \subset \mathcal{A}_{read} \cup \mathcal{A}_{draft} \subset \mathcal{A}_{read} \cup \mathcal{A}_{draft} \cup \mathcal{A}_{bounded-write},$
```

where $\mathcal{A}_{bounded-write}$ is permitted only under pre-defined limits and review gates. The important point is that the action set grows only after evidence of reliability, not because the model becomes more fluent.

**Proposition 14.9.1** (Escalation preserves accountability). *Suppose every material action* $a \in \mathcal{A}_{material}$ *requires either a logged human approval or a prior policy certificate that maps the state-action pair to an approved bounded-automation region. Then every executed material action has an accountable authority: either the approving human or the owner of the certified policy rule.*

Proof. By assumption, a material action has only two execution paths. In the first, the log records the human approval event and therefore the accountable reviewer. In the second, the action is covered by a policy certificate whose owner is recorded in the governance system. Since the action cannot execute outside these two paths, accountability is never assigned to the language model itself.

This framing is more ambitious than mere caution. It allows financial institutions to expand automation while preserving a clean separation between reasoning, execution authority, and responsibility. The agent may discover opportunities or assemble excellent evidence, but the institution must still decide which action classes it is willing to authorize.

## 14.10 Summary

This chapter examined the transition from predictive financial models to bounded autonomous agents. It explained why agency requires memory, planning, typed tools, governance gates, and auditability rather than model scale alone. It defined generative alpha as the factor-adjusted performance of AI-generated strategies or instruments while preserving conventional standards of evidence. It replaced unsupported universal convergence and collapse claims with more defensible optimization caveats, simulation-gap diagnostics, and a local spectral stability condition. It also distinguished deployed, near-term, and speculative technologies; presented a modern guarded implementation pattern; and developed an autonomy decision rule based on marginal benefits, costs, and risks.

The next and final chapter synthesizes the book's central lessons, identifies the capabilities and controls that should guide adoption, and closes with a practical agenda for building financial AI systems that are useful, accountable, and resilient.
