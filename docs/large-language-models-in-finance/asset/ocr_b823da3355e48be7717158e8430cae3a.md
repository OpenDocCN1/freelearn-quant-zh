# 11.1 The imperative for comprehensive governance

The central purpose of governance is not to prevent the use of LLMs. It is to make valuable uses repeatable while keeping failure within tolerable bounds. Financial institutions already operate under demanding obligations concerning safety and soundness, market integrity, consumer protection, privacy, operational resilience, and senior-management accountability. LLMs do not displace those obligations; they introduce new pathways through which the obligations can be breached, including unsupported generation, prompt-induced behavior, untraceable data use, tool misuse, common-mode responses, and excessive reliance on third-party models.

LLMs also differ from many conventional financial models. Their outputs are generated from learned statistical representations rather than a compact set of explicit equations, and their behavior can depend materially on prompts, retrieval context, tools, memory, decoding settings, and model version. A production “model” is therefore better understood as a socio-technical system that includes the base model, data and retrieval pipeline, prompts, tools, interfaces, users, controls, and organizational incentives. Validation of the base model alone is not validation of the deployed system.

The governance challenge is consequently broader than accuracy. A system can be accurate on average and still be unacceptable because its rare errors are severe, its explanations are misleading, its behavior changes after an update, its data lineage is incomplete, or its actions become correlated with other systems during stress. Conversely, an LLM need not be perfect to be useful when it is restricted to an appropriate role, surrounded by verification, and prevented from taking irreversible actions without additional controls.

Existing frameworks provide important foundations but must be combined with careful scope analysis. In the United States, SR 26-2 superseded SR 11-7 and SR 21-8. It is non-binding supervisory guidance, is expected to be most relevant to banking organizations with more than $30 billion in total assets, and narrows the model definition by excluding simple-arithmetic tools and deterministic rule-based software. Most importantly for this chapter, footnote 3 explicitly excludes generative-AI and agentic-AI models from its scope while preserving its application to traditional statistical and quantitative models and to non-generative, non-agentic AI models [Board of Governors of the Federal Reserve System et al., 2026]. The chapter therefore invokes SR 26-2 directly only for systems that fall within its formal perimeter and uses selected principles by analogy for LLM governance only when that analogy is stated. An institution’s own governance and risk-management practices, together with applicable sectoral law and supervisory requirements, remain the control basis for generative and agentic systems outside the guidance.

A mixed architecture can still create a validation dependency: when an out-of-scope LLM supplies data, features, narratives, or decisions to an in-scope quantitative model, the downstream review should examine that interface and record it in the system inventory. This is an architectural implication, not an expansion of SR 26-2’s formal scope. The NIST AI Risk Management Framework and its Generative AI Profile add lifecycle-oriented treatment of validity, reliability, security, transparency, privacy, and harmful-content risks [National Institute of Standards and Technology, 2023, 2024].
