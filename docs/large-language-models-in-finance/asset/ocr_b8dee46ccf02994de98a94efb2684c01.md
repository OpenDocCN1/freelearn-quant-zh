response \( y_l \), pushing the model to assign consistently higher rewards to the kinds of outputs that human reviewers favor. Formally, the reward model maps each prompt-response pair to a real-valued score:

```
\( r_\phi(x, y) = \text{RewardModel}_\phi(x, y) \in \mathbb{R} \)
```

and is trained by minimizing the Bradley-Terry loss over all collected preference pairs:

```
\( \mathcal{L}_{\text{RM}} = -\mathbb{E}_{(x, y_w, y_l)} [\log \sigma(r_\phi(x, y_w) - r_\phi(x, y_l))] \)
```

where \( y_w \) is the preferred (winner) response and \( y_l \) is the less preferred (loser) response.

Once this learned notion of “quality” is in place, it can be used to directly guide model behavior through reinforcement learning.

# 3.7.3 Policy optimization with Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO)

With a trained reward model in place, the third stage uses reinforcement learning to update the policy—the fine-tuned language model—so that it generates responses that receive higher rewards. The objective below captures the core trade-off: the first term drives the model toward high-reward responses, while the KL divergence penalty prevents it from drifting too far from the supervised fine-tuned reference model \( \pi_{\text{ref}} \). This penalty is essential in financial settings: without it, the model may find ways to “game” the reward signal by producing outputs that superficially match annotator preferences (say, by including more disclaimers and hedges) while losing the accuracy and coherence of the underlying response—a failure mode known as reward hacking.

Use PPO [Schulman et al., 2017] to fine-tune the model to maximize expected reward:

```
\( \max_\theta \mathbb{E}_{x \sim \mathcal{D}, y \sim \pi_\theta(y|x)} [r_\phi(x, y)] - \beta \cdot \text{KL}[\pi_\theta || \pi_{\text{ref}}] \)
```

The KL divergence term prevents the model from deviating too far from the reference policy \( \pi_{\text{ref}} \) (the SFT model), avoiding reward hacking and maintaining general capabilities.

PPO enforces stable policy updates through a clipped surrogate objective. The ratio \( r_t(\theta) \) measures how much the new policy deviates from the old one for a given action; clipping this ratio to the interval \( [1 - \epsilon, 1 + \epsilon] \) prevents any single update from making large, destabilizing changes to the policy. The advantage estimate \( \hat{A}_t \) captures whether the action taken (the token generated) turned out better or worse than the baseline, and scales the gradient accordingly:

The update step in PPO is defined as follows:

```
\( \mathcal{L}_{\text{PPO}}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_t \left[ \min \left( r_t(\theta) \hat{A}_t, \text{clip}(r_t(\theta), 1 - \epsilon, 1 + \epsilon) \hat{A}_t \right) \right] \)
```
