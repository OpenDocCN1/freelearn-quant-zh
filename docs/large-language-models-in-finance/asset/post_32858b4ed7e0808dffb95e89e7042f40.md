
## Model FLOPs utilization

A theoretical FLOPs count does not directly translate into wall-clock training time. Real hardware never converts its full advertised peak throughput into useful model updates. The relevant efficiency concept is **Model FLOPs Utilization (MFU)**, defined approximately as:

\(\text{MFU} = \frac{\text{achieved model FLOPs per second}}{\text{theoretical peak hardware FLOPs per second}}.\)

Given a compute requirement \(C_{\text{FLOPS}}\), aggregate peak hardware throughput \(P_{\text{peak}}\), and utilization MFU, the idealized training time is:

\(T_{\text{train}} \approx \frac{C_{\text{FLOPS}}}{\text{MFU} \cdot P_{\text{peak}}}.\)

MFU is reduced by communication across devices, memory bandwidth limits, activation recomputation, data loading, synchronization, pipeline bubbles, checkpointing, and fault recovery. This is why two systems with the same nominal FLOPs budget may have very different training times. BloombergGPT reported a compute budget of 1.3M GPU-hours on 40GB NVIDIA A100 GPUs and used 64 AWS p4d.24xlarge instances, each with eight A100 GPUs, linked by NVSwitch within nodes and EFA/GPUDirect across nodes [Wu et al., 2023b]. Those assumptions matter: changing GPU type, interconnect, parallelism strategy, batch size, or achieved utilization changes the wall-clock estimate even when the model size and token count remain fixed.

## Hardware requirements

Compute is only one constraint. Memory often becomes the limiting factor first. During training, model parameters, gradients, optimizer states, and intermediate activations must coexist in GPU memory:

- **Parameters**: N parameters × 4 bytes (FP32) or 2 bytes (FP16)
- **Gradients**: N × precision
- **Optimizer states**: 2N for Adam (momentum + variance)
- **Activations**: Proportional to batch size and sequence length

The implication is straightforward: as model size grows, single-device training rapidly becomes infeasible. Large models require distributed training across multiple GPUs and nodes:

- **Data parallelism**: Different GPUs process different batches
- **Model parallelism**: Model split across GPUs
- **Pipeline parallelism**: Different layers on different GPUs
- **Tensor parallelism**: Individual layers split across GPUs

Training duration reflects the cumulative weight of these constraints. The following orders of magnitude should be interpreted as planning estimates, not universal constants. They assume modern data-center GPUs, high-bandwidth interconnects, mixed precision, distributed data loading, and non-trivial but imperfect MFU:

- **Small continued-pretraining or fine-tuning runs (7B–13B)**: days to weeks on a small multi-GPU setup, depending on dataset size and context length.
- **Domain-scale pre-training runs (30B–70B)**: weeks to months on dedicated GPU clusters with tensor, pipeline, and/or ZeRO-style sharding.
- **BloombergGPT-scale reference point**: the published run used 512 A100 40GB GPUs, ZeRO stage 3, model parallelism, activation checkpointing, and a 1.3M GPU-hour compute budget, so the reported timing cannot be interpreted without those infrastructure assumptions [Wu et al., 2023b].

Ambition, therefore, has to be grounded in infrastructure and budget. Choices about parameter count, corpus size, and architectural complexity are not just technical design decisions, as they carry real financial and strategic consequences. The cost of pre-training influences not only how a model is built, but whether building it from scratch is a sensible path in the first place.

## 2.4 Understanding prompt engineering in finance

Up to this point, we have focused on how financial language models are built: architectures, tokenization strategies, training objectives, and infrastructure requirements. Yet even the most powerful model does not operate in isolation. Its practical value depends on how it is *instructed*. This section examines how prompt engineering translates financial expertise and regulatory constraints into structured instructions that LLMs can reliably execute. We will explore how prompts can be designed to produce compliant and professionally accurate outputs in financial contexts.

Prompt engineering, in this sense, is not cosmetic phrasing. It is the operational interface between human judgment and model capability. Financial analysis demands precision and contextual awareness. Effective prompts must therefore encode not only the task itself, but also the institutional environment in which the output will be used.

We begin with the foundations of prompt design, focusing on how to structure instructions so they are clear and repeatable. We then move into reasoning-enhancement techniques that strengthen analytical depth and reliability. Finally, we examine financial domain-specific strategies, where prompting must adapt to regulatory frameworks, risk discipline, quantitative validation, and evolving market realities.

### 2.4.1 Prompt design

Effective prompt engineering requires a systematic approach that considers the unique characteristics of financial data expected in financial services. Hence, let’s understand the design patterns that make prompts reliable in such regulated, high-stakes environments.

One boundary must be fixed before any of this is useful, because the rest of the book depends on it. A prompt can *express* a regulatory requirement; it cannot *enforce* one. An instruction not to give investment advice, not to exceed a position limit, or not to cite an unavailable document is a request to a stochastic system, and a stochastic system will occasionally decline it — under distribution shift, under adversarial input, or for no visible reason at all. Enforcement is the job of deterministic machinery outside the model: permission checks, schema validation, policy gates, and human review. Treat every constraint in a prompt as a hint that improves the odds, and place the control that actually binds somewhere the model cannot reach. With that understood: financial prompts must encode context, role, constraints, and output expectations in ways that reduce ambiguity and align model behavior with professional standards. We'll begin with structured prompt design frameworks that formalize how tasks are specified. We then examine advanced prompting techniques, including conditional logic and meta-prompting. Together, these elements transform prompts from informal instructions into repeatable specifications.

### Structured prompt design

Structured prompt design gives you a repeatable way to specify financial tasks. Rather than writing free-form instructions, the most effective prompts follow a clear structure: they set the context, define the task, state the constraints, and specify the required output format:

```
CONTEXT: [Market conditions, time period, regulatory framework]
TASK: [Specific financial analysis or computation required]
CONSTRAINTS: [Regulatory requirements, risk parameters, assumptions]
FORMAT: [Output structure, precision requirements, compliance notes]
EXAMPLES: [Representative input-output pairs for guidance]
```

This structure reduces ambiguity. It ensures the model understands not just what to do, but the conditions under which it should do it. For example, if you request a credit risk analysis, the prompt should clearly state the regulatory framework (such as Basel III or IFRS 9), define risk appetite parameters, and specify required output metrics and documentation standards. Without this information, the model may generate something plausible but misaligned with institutional requirements.

Establishing context matters especially in finance. The same analytical method can produce very different conclusions depending on market conditions or regulation. Effective prompts therefore include temporal markers, market indicators, regulatory frameworks, and any relevant institutional constraints:

```
Context: Analyzing Q3 2024 earnings for large-cap technology companies
under current Federal Reserve monetary policy (5.25% federal funds
rate) and updated SEC disclosure requirements for AI-related
investments. Market volatility (VIX) currently at 18.5, with technology
sector trading at 23.2x forward P/E ratio.
```
