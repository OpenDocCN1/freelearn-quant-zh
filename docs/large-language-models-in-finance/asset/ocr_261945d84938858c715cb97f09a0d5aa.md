where \(\rho_t(\theta)\) is the ratio between the new and old policy probabilities and \(\widehat{A}_t\) is an estimated advantage. This objective encourages conservative updates; it is not a general theorem that PPO converges almost surely to a locally optimal policy in live financial markets. Such markets are partially observed, non-stationary, strategically responsive, and affected by the agent’s own market impact. Concept drift, reward misspecification, transaction costs, and regime changes can invalidate assumptions used in stationary training environments [Gama et al., 2014, Dulac-Arnold et al., 2019]. In finance, convergence on a simulator should therefore be treated as an optimization result for that simulator, not as evidence of deployable optimality.

These architectural and methodological qualifications set the stage for a more careful definition of generative alpha.

# 14.3 Generative Alpha: A new paradigm for market outperformance

For decades, “alpha” has denoted performance unexplained by a chosen benchmark or factor model. LLMs and autonomous systems may change the *production process* for candidate strategies: they can search over rules, contracts, execution schedules, and market designs at a scale that would be difficult for a human team alone [Cao et al., 2021]. They do not change the statistical burden of proof. A generated strategy still requires temporal separation, transaction-cost modeling, multiple-testing controls, and genuine out-of-sample evaluation.

# 14.3.1 Mathematical framework for Generative Alpha

**Definition 14.3.1** (Generative Alpha). Let \(R_{t+1}^{(g)}\) denote the return of a strategy or instrument generated by an AI-assisted search process, \(R_{f,t+1}\) the risk-free return, and \(F_{t+1} \in \mathbb{R}^k\) a vector of benchmark factor returns. Assume that second moments exist and that the factor covariance \(\text{Var}(F_{t+1})\) is positive definite. Note that this is not the same assumption as nonsingularity of the uncentered second moment \(\mathbb{E}[F_{t+1}F_{t+1}^\top]\): the two coincide only when the factors are mean-zero, which benchmark factors are not. The formula below requires the covariance, so the covariance is what must be assumed invertible. Define the factor loading of the generated strategy by the population projection

\((\alpha_g, \beta_g) := \arg \min_{\alpha \in \mathbb{R}, \beta \in \mathbb{R}^k} \mathbb{E}\left[\left(R_{t+1}^e - \alpha - \beta^\top F_{t+1}\right)^2\right], \quad R_{t+1}^e := R_{t+1}^{(g)} - R_{f,t+1}. \tag{14.22}\)

The intercept must be estimated *jointly*, not recovered afterwards from the residuals of a projection that never had one. The normal equations of the joint problem give

\(\beta_g = \text{Var}(F_{t+1})^{-1} \text{Cov}(F_{t+1}, R_{t+1}^e), \qquad \alpha_g = \mathbb{E}[R_{t+1}^e] - \beta_g^\top \mathbb{E}[F_{t+1}], \tag{14.23}\)

provided the displayed inverse exists. Note the centering: the projection uses the factor *covariance*, not the uncentered second moment \(\mathbb{E}[FF^\top]\). Equivalently, \(\beta_g\) is the slope of the intercept regression of \(R^e\) on \(F\).
