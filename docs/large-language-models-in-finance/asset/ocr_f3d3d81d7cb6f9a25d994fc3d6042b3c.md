# # Preface

The financial industry is undergoing a rapid transformation. Large Language Models (LLMs), once experimental research artifacts, are now operational systems that reshape how markets are analyzed, how risks are managed, and how institutions interact with clients, regulators, and counterparties. The shift is structural: financial decisions are no longer supported only by econometric models and neatly structured datasets, but increasingly by systems capable of interpreting unstructured language at scale. From parsing earnings calls and central bank speeches to detecting anomalies in regulatory filings and client communications, the impact of LLMs on finance is already visible and accelerating.

My engagement with these technologies has been both professional and personal. A decade ago, financial machine learning was dominated by relatively small statistical models and hand-crafted rules. The introduction of the Transformer architecture in 2017 [Vaswani et al., 2017], followed by the deployment of models such as GPT-3 [Brown et al., 2020] and BERT [Devlin et al., 2019], marked the beginning of a new era. It became evident that the future of financial AI would not be built only on incremental refinements of legacy tools, but on new ways of representing and reasoning about language. The emergence of domain-specific models such as FinBERT [Araci, 2019], BloombergGPT [Wu et al., 2023b], and FinEAS [Gutiérrez-Fandiño et al., 2021] confirmed that this change was not theoretical but practical.

This book provides both a rigorous foundation and a practical guide for navigating that change. It explains how LLMs work, how they can be adapted to the particular constraints and requirements of finance, and how they must be governed to operate responsibly in regulated environments. The discussion moves from architectures, tokenization, and optimization objectives to fine-tuning strategies, retrieval-augmented generation, multi-agent systems, and protocol-level integration. At the same time, it confronts the risks, including bias, hallucinations, lack of interpretability, and systemic fragility, that must be addressed if these systems are to be trusted in high-stakes financial contexts.

Large Language Models in finance are best understood as part of a pipeline rather than as a point solution. Raw documents, time series, and market feeds are curated, normalized, and embedded; retrieval layers keep knowledge current; generation is constrained by financial identities, accounting rules, and risk limits; policies are aligned and evaluated; and decisions are executed within a framework of governance and oversight. This book is structured to guide you along that pipeline.
