Moving from correlation toward causation is another critical frontier. Current systems excel at detecting statistical regularities but struggle to distinguish between causal relationships and spurious patterns. Consider a model that learns that policy-rate cuts are followed by weak equity returns. A naive interpretation is that cutting rates causes the market to fall; in many episodes, however, deteriorating growth and financial stress cause both the rate cut and the equity decline. The predictive association can therefore be useful while the intervention claim is wrong. A similar problem arises when social-media attention predicts returns because both are driven by an earnings surprise: purchasing additional mentions would not reproduce the return effect.

Causal analysis asks a different question from forecasting: how would the outcome change under a specified intervention, holding the relevant data-generating structure fixed? Combining structural assumptions, natural experiments, instruments, event timing, and explicit causal graphs with multimodal and temporal models could improve policy analysis and stress testing of “what if” scenarios [Pearl, 2009, Peters et al., 2017]. Such claims require stronger identification assumptions than ordinary prediction, so progress is likely to be gradual, domain-specific, and accompanied by sensitivity analysis rather than universal.

Finally, collaborative modeling techniques such as federated learning offer a potential path to industry-wide models without centralizing sensitive data. In a federated setup, institutions train models locally and share only parameter updates, preserving data privacy while benefiting from collective learning. In practice, this approach faces non-trivial challenges—including heterogeneous data distributions, communication constraints, and complex governance questions about who controls the resulting models and how benefits are shared. For the foreseeable future, federated and collaborative approaches should be treated as promising research and pilot areas, not as immediate replacements for institution-specific models.

# 13.7 Open questions and recommended resources

# 13.7.1 Open research questions

The frontier described in this chapter remains defined as much by unresolved evaluation and governance problems as by architectural progress. Particularly important questions include:

+   1. **Verifiable financial reasoning:** How can a system expose the data, calculations, assumptions, and decision rules that support an answer without treating an unfaithful generated rationale as proof that the answer is correct?
+   
+   2. **Search under real-time budgets:** How should ToT, MCTS, and self-refinement allocate compute when market state, prices, and available actions change during the search itself?
+   
+   3. **Calibration under regime change:** Can reasoning confidence, multimodal agreement, and predictive uncertainty remain calibrated when liquidity, policy, or market microstructure shifts outside the training distribution?
+   
+   4. **Temporal and cross-modal alignment:** How should systems distinguish genuinely contemporaneous evidence from stale, revised, or asynchronously observed data, especially when alternative-data vendors use different timestamp conventions?
