The practical meaning is straightforward. Autonomy should increase only while its marginal benefit exceeds the combined marginal implementation and risk costs. A task with reversible, low-value actions may justify a higher \(\alpha^*\) than a task involving customer suitability, capital allocation, or market-moving orders. Better validation, clearer permissions, stronger sandboxing, and reliable human escalation can reduce \(R'(\alpha)\) and permit more automation. Opaque behavior, correlated models, weak observability, or irreversible actions push the appropriate level of autonomy downward. The optimum is therefore task-specific and dynamic, not a universal number for the institution. The path forward requires robust governance and a sustained commitment to responsible innovation. Financial institutions must adapt rules, supervision, and infrastructure as capabilities change; deliberately monitor concentration in models and data; strengthen transparency for high-impact decisions; and observe markets as interacting ecosystems rather than collections of independent algorithms. Because agents can operate across jurisdictions, international standards for testing, incident reporting, and information sharing will also become increasingly important.

# 14.8 Evaluation contract for generative alpha systems

The most common error in evaluating generative alpha systems is to confuse a plausible narrative with a tradable signal. A generated thesis is not alpha unless it survives a decision-time protocol, a leakage audit, transaction-cost modeling, risk adjustment, and out-of-sample degradation analysis. The correct object is therefore not a text paragraph but a signed prediction record:

```
\(r_i = (t_i, \mathcal{I}_t, h_i, s_i, p_i, \ell_i, \text{audit}_i),\)
```

where \(t_i\) is the decision time, \(\mathcal{I}_t\) is the information set, \(h_i\) is the generated hypothesis, \(s_i\) is the tradable signal, \(p_i\) is the portfolio or action implied by the signal, \(\ell_i\) is the realized loss or utility, and \(\text{audit}_i\) records model, prompt, data, tool, and reviewer hashes.

A minimal evaluation uses three layers:

+   1. **Predictive layer.** Does the generated signal predict a future target under a chronological split?
+   
+   2. **Economic layer.** Does the signal survive costs, capacity, turnover, risk limits, and drawdown constraints?
+   
+   3. **Governance layer.** Are the claims supported, the data licensed, the timestamps valid, and the actions authorized?

For a return target \(R_{i+1}\) and signal \(s_i\), the predictive layer may report information coefficient

```
\(\text{IC} = \text{Corr}(s_i, R_{i+1}),\)
```

while the economic layer reports cost-adjusted utility, for example

```
\(U = \mathbb{E}[p_i R_{i+1} - \text{TC}(p_i, p_{i-1})] - \frac{\gamma}{2} \text{Var}(p_i R_{i+1}).\)
```

The governance layer should be reported separately rather than hidden inside performance. A strategy with attractive backtest returns but repeated citation failures, timestamp leaks, or unauthorized tool use is not production-ready.
