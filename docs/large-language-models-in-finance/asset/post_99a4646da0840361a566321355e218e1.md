The prompt makes the target, horizon, cutoff, admissible inputs, output schema, and division between language and numerical computation explicit. A useful formalization is to write a reprogrammed forecast as

$\widehat{Y}_{T+1:T+H} = G_\phi (\text{LLM}_\theta (P, R_\psi(Y_{T-L+1:T}))),$

where $R_\psi$ maps the numerical history into LLM-compatible embeddings, $P$ is the textual prompt, $\theta$ is frozen or partially adapted, and $G_\phi$ decodes the forecast. For finance, every component of this expression must be timestamped. If $P$ contains a market narrative written after the forecast horizon, the benchmark is contaminated even if the numerical data are clean.

## 13.3.4 Financial forecasting targets

Time-series LLMs should not be evaluated only on next-period return prediction. In many financial settings, return forecasting is the hardest and least stable target. Other targets may be more useful and more predictable.

*Volatility forecasting* asks for \( \sigma_{T+1:T+H} \) or a distribution over realized volatility. This is often more stable than mean-return forecasting because volatility clusters strongly. TSFMs can learn nonlinear volatility regimes, post-event decay, and interactions between volume, range, and realized variance.

*Covariance forecasting* estimates \( \Sigma_{T+1:T+H} \) for portfolios. This is central for allocation, risk parity, factor hedging, and stress testing. Cross-variate architectures such as iTransformer are especially relevant because covariance is intrinsically multivariate.

*Tail-risk forecasting* predicts quantiles, expected shortfall, drawdown probabilities, or breach probabilities. Quantile-based TSFMs such as Moirai-style models are naturally aligned with this task because the output is distributional rather than a single mean [Liu et al., 2025].

*Event response forecasting* estimates the conditional path after earnings releases, central-bank announcements, macro surprises, rating changes, litigation news, or liquidity shocks. This is where hybrid text–time-series LLMs are most promising: the text identifies the event meaning, while the time-series module estimates the likely temporal response.

*Execution and liquidity forecasting* predicts spreads, market impact, fill probabilities, queue dynamics, and short-horizon volume. Here latency and robustness often matter more than expressive capacity. A TSFM may support slower calibration of intraday regimes, while simple models remain in the execution critical path.

## 13.3.5 Evaluation: Statistical accuracy versus economic usefulness

Let \(\ell_{i,t}\) be the forecast loss of model \(i\) at time \(t\). The Diebold–Mariano statistic compares two loss sequences through

\( d_t = \ell_{1,t} - \ell_{2,t}, \qquad DM = \frac{\bar{d}}{\sqrt{\widehat{\text{Var}}(\bar{d})}}. \)

For overlapping multi-step horizons, the variance estimate must account for serial correlation in \(d_t\) [Diebold and Mariano, 2002, Harvey et al., 1997]. This matters for the common 20-business-day horizon: adjacent forecast windows overlap heavily, so naive standard errors overstate significance.

A useful benchmark discipline is to report at least four layers: (i) error metrics against simple baselines such as random walk, historical mean, ARIMA, ETS, or seasonal naive; (ii) probabilistic calibration metrics; (iii) statistical significance tests with overlap adjustment; and (iv) economic metrics after costs. The companion benchmark’s finding that pretrained models win many ranks while many DM tests fail to reject equal accuracy is therefore not a contradiction. It is a realistic picture of financial predictability: models may be incrementally better and operationally useful while still falling short of strong statistical dominance.

## 13.3.6 Deployment in financial institutions

Production deployment requires matching model latency to decision horizon. A slow, expressive TSFM may be appropriate for daily risk reports, strategic allocation, volatility regime detection, or scenario generation. It is usually inappropriate as the only model in a high-frequency execution loop. A practical architecture uses a two-speed design: Fast transparent models handle quoting, execution, pre-trade checks, limit enforcement, and emergency fallback.
