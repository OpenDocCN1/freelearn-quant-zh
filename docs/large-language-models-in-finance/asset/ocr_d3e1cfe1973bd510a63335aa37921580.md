where the state space S aggregates the agent’s current observation, context, and memory, and the action space A includes tool invocations, messages to other agents, and final responses. The policy is instantiated via an LLM with parameters θ, possibly augmented by additional non-learned logic.

Crucially, the agent is defined not merely by the underlying LLM, but by the combination of *role*, *tools*, and *interaction patterns*. Two agents may use the same base model but differ in the tools they are allowed to call, the prompts they use, and the workflows they participate in; in such cases, they should be treated as distinct agents, as they implement different policies. In the research workstation, the Research Analyst agent and the Risk and Compliance agent could both be backed by the same foundation model, but the former is allowed to retrieve filings and research notes while the latter is allowed to query policy, restricted-list, and portfolio-limit tools. They are therefore different agents, not merely different prompt invocations.

In practice, this definition maps most directly to what is often called a **single-agent** setup. Single-agent architectures are often the simplest to implement and reason about. A single LLM agent receives user input, accesses tools and data sources, and produces a final response. This pattern is suitable for relatively constrained workflows, such as:

+   a regulatory Q&A assistant that retrieves and summarizes relevant sections of regulations and internal policies;

+   a portfolio overview assistant that aggregates positions, risk metrics, and performance for a given portfolio; or

+   a document classification assistant that assigns categories or tags to documents.

In these settings, the agent’s responsibilities can be encapsulated in a single prompt template and a small set of tools. Memory can be kept minimal, typically limited to the current conversation and retrieved documents, while orchestration logic can be handled by a thin controller layer. Most of the work here lies in getting prompts, tool interfaces, and evaluation right rather than coordinating multiple components. A simplified version of the research workstation could begin this way: a single research assistant retrieves documents, summarizes them, and produces a memo draft. The need for a multi-agent design appears when the workflow must add independent data analysis, risk review, and compliance controls without turning one prompt into an unmanageable bundle of responsibilities.

# 5.1.2 Multi-agent system

A **multi-agent system** (**MAS**) is a collection of such agents that communicate and coordinate, often under the guidance of an orchestrator or higher-level policy, to support a broader workflow (such as research, risk management, or client service) and collectively achieve a set of global objectives.

$$\mathcal{M} = \{\mathcal{A}_1, \mathcal{A}_2, \dots, \mathcal{A}_N\}$$ (5.2)
