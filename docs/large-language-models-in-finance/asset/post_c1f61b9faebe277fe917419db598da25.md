
where \( r_t(\theta) = \frac{\pi_\theta(a_t|s_t)}{\pi_{\text{old}}(a_t|s_t)} \) is the probability ratio and \(\hat{A}_t\) is the advantage estimate.

While PPO provides a principled way to perform this optimization, it is often complex to implement and tune in practice. This has led to simpler alternatives that achieve similar outcomes with less overhead.

### 3.7.4 Direct Preference Optimization (DPO)

Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) [Rafailov et al., 2023] is a widely used practical alternative to PPO-based RLHF. DPO avoids training a separate explicit reward model and running an online policy-optimization loop, recasting alignment as optimization over preference pairs. This simplifies the pipeline and can improve training stability, although it does not remove data-quality, specification, or reward-misspecification risks.

The key insight is that the optimal policy under the PPO objective has a closed-form relationship to the reward function:

\(\pi^*(y|x) \propto \pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x) \exp\left(\frac{1}{\beta} r(x, y)\right)\) (3.18)

Solving for the implicit reward and substituting into the Bradley-Terry loss yields the DPO objective, which operates directly on preference pairs without ever materializing a reward model:

\(\mathcal{L}_{\text{DPO}}(\theta) = -\mathbb{E}_{(x, y_w, y_l)}\left[\log \sigma \left(\beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y_w|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_w|x)} - \beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y_l|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_l|x)}\right)\right]\) (3.19)

where \(\beta\) is a temperature coefficient controlling how much the policy is allowed to deviate from the reference model, \(y_w\) is the preferred response, and \(y_l\) is the dispreferred response. Intuitively, the loss increases the likelihood of \(y_w\) relative to the reference policy while simultaneously decreasing the likelihood of \(y_l\), calibrated by the margin between the two log-ratios.

A more intuitive way to understand how DPO differs from PPO-based RLHF is to look at how the two approaches play out:

- **Simpler pipeline**: No reward model training, no separate RL loop—just one fine-tuning pass on preference data.
- **More stable**: Eliminates the policy gradient variance and reward hacking instability that make PPO difficult to tune in practice.
- **Lower compute**: Requires no online rollouts; training is as cheap as supervised fine-tuning on the preference dataset.
- **Limitation**: Relies on offline preference data; cannot incorporate live human feedback as easily as online RLHF with PPO.

For many financial teams, DPO is a strong starting point when a high-quality preference dataset is available. It is generally easier to implement and debug than a full PPO pipeline. PPO remains relevant when online interaction, explicit reward decomposition, or policy-level control justifies the additional complexity. The choice should be based on matched evaluation rather than on method popularity.

With these alignment methods in place, the remaining question is where they are most useful in financial systems.

### 3.7.5 RLHF for financial applications

RLHF is most valuable in financial settings where the quality of a response cannot be reduced to a single correct answer. The use cases below share a common property: they involve judgment calls about tone, framing, confidence calibration, and regulatory compliance that experienced practitioners recognize immediately but that are difficult to encode as labeled training examples.

RLHF is particularly useful in financial tasks where outputs must reflect judgment rather than just correctness. Typical use cases include generating investment commentary, drafting client communications that require the right tone and regulatory disclaimers, producing research reports, and writing risk assessment narratives.

What makes financial RLHF particularly demanding is the breadth of dimensions along which a response can be good or bad. An annotator reviewing model-generated investment commentary must simultaneously evaluate factual accuracy, the strength of the analytical argument, whether confidence levels are appropriate given available evidence, and whether the output satisfies compliance requirements around forward-looking statements. ## 3.8 Evaluation and validation

Rigorous evaluation is not a formality that happens after the interesting work is done—it is an integral part of the fine-tuning process that determines whether a model is safe to deploy. This is especially true in finance because errors in production are not just embarrassing; they can affect investment decisions, trigger compliance violations, or introduce systematic bias into credit and risk assessments. A model that achieves strong benchmark scores but fails on out-of-distribution inputs, hallucinates figures under time pressure, or degrades silently as market regimes shift presents serious operational risk.

This section organizes evaluation into three layers that complement one another. Offline metrics measure performance on held-out labeled data before deployment. Financial-specific validation tests properties that standard NLP benchmarks do not cover—numerical precision, temporal consistency, and factual grounding. Robustness and A/B testing extend evaluation into production, where the model faces the full complexity of real-world inputs and user behavior.

We start with the most familiar layer of evaluation: performance on held-out data.

### 3.8.1 Offline evaluation metrics

The right metric depends on the task type. Classification, generation, and numerical reasoning each call for a different primary measure, and using the wrong one can give a misleading picture of model quality. A model with high accuracy on a class-imbalanced sentiment dataset may simply be predicting the majority class most of the time; a model with high ROUGE on a summarization benchmark may be extracting surface phrases without understanding their meaning.

**Classification tasks** are typically evaluated using accuracy, precision, recall, and F1 score, with per-class metrics providing additional visibility in imbalanced datasets. Tools such as confusion matrices and ROC curves with AUC further help diagnose where the model is performing well and where it is systematically making errors.

**Generation tasks** require a different set of metrics that capture both surface-level and semantic quality. Perplexity measures how well the model fits the test data, while BLEU and ROUGE assess n-gram overlap with reference outputs. BERTScore complements these by capturing semantic similarity, and in practice, human evaluation remains essential to judge coherence, factual accuracy, and overall usefulness.

**Numerical tasks**, which are common in finance, demand metrics that reflect both precision and tolerance. Exact match accuracy is useful for strict correctness, while tolerance-based measures (such as ±1% or ±5%) distinguish between minor rounding differences and meaningful errors. Mean absolute error and root mean squared error provide additional insight into the magnitude and distribution of prediction errors. For numerical financial tasks, exact match is often too strict—a model that computes $1.401 billion when the ground truth is $1.400 billion has made a rounding error, not a reasoning error—while tolerances of ±1% and ±5% capture the distinction between acceptable approximations and meaningfully wrong
