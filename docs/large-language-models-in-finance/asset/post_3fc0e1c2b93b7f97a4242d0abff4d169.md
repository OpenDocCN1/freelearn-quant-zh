### 8.5 Open questions

Several research and implementation questions remain open:

- How should retrieval recall, long-context utilization, and document-graph accuracy be jointly evaluated for high-stakes financial questions?
- Which calibration methods remain reliable when customer behavior, product design, regulation, and economic conditions change over time?
- How can one policy architecture encode jurisdiction-specific suitability, disclosure, and recordkeeping obligations without allowing stale rules to persist in prompts or model weights?

## Reinforcement Learning in LLMs

This chapter introduces _reinforcement learning (RL)_ as a framework for aligning LLM behavior with feedback, preferences, and longer-term objectives. While pre-training and supervised fine tuning give large language models broad knowledge and the ability to follow examples, RL focuses on improving behavior through rewards and penalties.

This distinction is important in finance, where many tasks involve sequential decisions under uncertainty. Trading, portfolio allocation, risk management, and advisory workflows often require models or systems to consider not only a single output, but also the consequences of a sequence of decisions over time.

To build from this idea, the chapter first explains the basic RL framework, then moves from reward design and alignment algorithms to financial applications and safety considerations. This chapter covers the following topics:

- The basic reinforcement learning framework for language models
- Reward modeling and human feedback collection
- PPO, GRPO, and DPO for LLM alignment
- Financial applications in trading, risk management, and portfolio optimization
- Advanced RL techniques, including hierarchical and multi-agent RL
- Key challenges, safety concerns, and future research directions

### 9.1 A constrained alignment objective for financial LLMs

RLHF and related preference-optimization methods are often introduced as ways to make an LLM more helpful. In finance, helpfulness is only one term in the objective. A response must also be faithful to evidence, calibrated, numerically consistent, within authorization boundaries, and suitable for the relevant audience. This suggests a constrained alignment objective rather than an unconstrained reward-maximization problem.

Let $x$ be a prompt, $y$ a candidate response, and $\pi_\theta(y \mid x)$ the policy being aligned. Let $r_{\text{pref}}(x, y)$ be a human or model-assisted preference score, $r_{\text{faith}}(x, y)$ a faithfulness score, $r_{\text{num}}(x, y)$ a numerical-consistency score, and $c_j(x, y)$ policy-violation indicators or costs. A financial alignment objective can be written as

```
\(\begin{aligned} \max_{\theta} \quad & \mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{X}, y\sim\pi_\theta(\cdot|x)} [r_{\text{pref}}(x, y) + \alpha r_{\text{faith}}(x, y) + \beta r_{\text{num}}(x, y)] \\ & \quad - \eta \mathbb{E}_x [\text{KL}(\pi_\theta(\cdot|x)\|\pi_0(\cdot|x))] \end{aligned} \quad (9.1)\)
\(\text{subject to } \mathbb{E}_{x,y}[c_j(x, y)] \le \varepsilon_j, \quad j=1, \dots, J. \quad (9.2)\)
```

Here $\pi_0$ is the reference model, the KL term prevents uncontrolled drift, and the constraints encode hard governance requirements. For example, a constraint may bound the rate of unsupported material claims, unauthorized recommendations, missing disclosures, or numerical mismatches.

The Lagrangian form is

```
\(\mathcal{L}(\theta, \lambda) = \mathbb{E}[r_{\text{pref}} + \alpha r_{\text{faith}} + \beta r_{\text{num}}] - \eta \mathbb{E}[\text{KL}(\pi_\theta\|\pi_0)] - \sum_{j=1}^J \lambda_j (\mathbb{E}[c_j] - \varepsilon_j), \quad \lambda_j \ge 0. \quad (9.3)\)
```

This expression explains why a single scalar reward is dangerously in finance: if compliance, evidence, and numerical checks are merely weak reward terms, the optimizer may trade them away. Constraints or fail-closed post-processing are often preferable for high-impact use cases.

**Proposition 9.1.1** (Penalty interpretation of constrained alignment). _For fixed multipliers_ $\lambda_j \ge 0$, _maximizing the Lagrangian above is equivalent to maximizing an unconstrained reward in which each policy cost_ $c_j$ _is penalized by_ $\lambda_j$. If the original constrained problem satisfies standard convexity and regularity conditions in an appropriate policy class, then there exist optimal multipliers for which a saddle point of the Lagrangian solves the constrained problem.

_Proof._ For fixed $\lambda$, collect all terms depending on the sampled response into a single shaped reward

```
\(\tilde{r}(x, y) = r_{\text{pref}}(x, y) + \alpha r_{\text{faith}}(x, y) + \beta r_{\text{num}}(x, y) - \sum_j \lambda_j c_j(x, y).\)
```

The remaining constants $\sum_j \lambda_j \varepsilon_j$ do not affect the optimizer over $\theta$. This gives the penalty interpretation. The saddle-point statement is the standard Lagrange-duality result under convexity, feasibility, and constraint qualification assumptions; neural policy classes are generally non-convex, so in practice this result is a design guide rather than a guarantee of global optimality. PPO-style and preference-optimization methods should therefore be validated empirically and wrapped with deterministic controls rather than treated as proof of safety [Schulman et al., 2017, Christiano et al., 2017, Ouyang et al., 2022].

Reinforcement learning allows language models to be trained with feedback signals, so their outputs can be optimized for objectives that are difficult to express as standard supervised learning targets. To understand how RL is applied to language models, we first need to define the basic components of the RL setup: states, actions, rewards, and policies.
