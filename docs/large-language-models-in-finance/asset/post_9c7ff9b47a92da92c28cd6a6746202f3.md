
Step 9 - WACC Calculation: Risk-free rate 4.5%, equity risk premium 6%, beta 1.2, debt/equity 0.3, cost of debt 6%. WACC = 10.0%.
Step 10 - Terminal Value: 3% perpetual growth rate (conservative given GDP growth assumptions), terminal ROIC validation.
Step 11 - Sensitivity Analysis: Value range $45-65 per share based on growth rate (±1%) and WACC (±0.5%) variations.
Conclusion: Base case intrinsic value $55 per share with current trading price $48 suggesting 15% upside potential. Key risks: market share erosion, margin compression exceeding projections.

What makes this effective is not the math alone. It is the visibility of assumptions and transitions. Each step is defensible. Each projection is tied to context. Sensitivities are explicit. That transparency reduces the chance of confident but shallow analysis.

More advanced versions push this further. You can ask the model to solve the same problem using different methodologies before reconciling the results. Or you can break a large problem into smaller sub-problems, solve each independently, and then integrate the findings. Both approaches introduce internal cross-checking — something every experienced analyst does instinctively.

At this point, however, we move from strengthening reasoning mechanics to embedding prompting within the realities of financial institutions themselves.

## 2.4.3 Domain-specific strategies

Specialized prompting strategies for financial applications must address not only analytical complexity, but also regulatory requirements and professional standards. Here, prompting shifts from being a reasoning tool to becoming an institutional control mechanism.

### Regulatory-aware prompting

Financial analysis does not operate in isolation. It sits inside dense regulatory frameworks that shape how research is conducted, how risk is measured, and how conclusions are communicated. Regulatory-aware prompting ensures that model outputs remain compliant while preserving analytical integrity.

One approach is compliance-integrated prompting, where regulatory requirements are embedded directly into the instruction itself:

Conduct credit risk analysis in accordance with Basel III capital requirements, incorporating regulatory capital ratios (CET1, Tier 1, Total Capital) and stress testing scenarios as defined in CCAR guidelines. Ensure all probability of default estimates align with IRB methodology requirements and include appropriate documentation for regulatory examination.

Disclosure-aware prompting works similarly. Instead of assuming the model will include appropriate disclosures, the prompt explicitly requires them, such as incorporating FINRA Rule 2241 disclosure elements for equity research:

> Include all required FINRA disclosures for equity research recommendations, specifically: price target methodology, rating system explanation, conflict of interest disclosures, and material risk factors. Format disclosures according to current FINRA Rule 2241 requirements.

In cross-border contexts, jurisdictional prompting becomes necessary. The instruction may require alignment with SEC, FCA, and BaFin regulations simultaneously, ensuring compliance with MiFID II research unbundling requirements:

> For cross-border investment analysis, incorporate regulatory requirements from all relevant jurisdictions: SEC (US), FCA (UK), BaFin (Germany), ensuring compliance with MiFID II research unbundling requirements and appropriate local disclosure standards.

### Risk-integrated prompting

Beyond compliance, financial analysis must reflect disciplined risk thinking. Effective prompting makes risk assessment explicit rather than assumed.

Multi-dimensional risk prompting instructs the model to evaluate different categories of risk in a structured way. Instead of producing a single narrative, the model is guided to examine each dimension systematically:

> Analyze investment opportunity considering: (1) Market Risk - beta, correlation, volatility patterns; (2) Credit Risk - default probability, recovery rates, credit migration; (3) Liquidity Risk - bid-ask spreads, trading volume, market depth; (4) Operational Risk - key person dependency, process risks, technology risks; (5) Regulatory Risk - compliance costs, regulatory changes, examination risk.

Scenario-based risk prompting incorporates stress testing and alternative scenario analysis:

> Evaluate investment thesis under three scenarios: (1) Base Case - current economic conditions persist; (2) Stress Case - recession with 30% equity market decline, 200bps credit spread widening; (3) Tail Risk - sector-specific crisis with 50% individual security decline. Provide probability-weighted expected returns and maximum loss estimates.

Dynamic risk prompting adapts risk assessment based on market conditions and portfolio context:

> Adjust risk analysis based on current portfolio composition: if concentration in sector >15%, emphasize diversification benefits; if portfolio beta >1.2, focus on downside protection; if cash position <5%, consider liquidity requirements for position sizing.

### Quantitative integration prompting

Financial analysis often requires sophisticated integration of quantitative models and statistical analysis. Quantitative integration prompting ensures appropriate use of mathematical and statistical techniques while maintaining analytical coherence.

Methodology-selection prompting guides the model toward suitable valuation frameworks:

> Select appropriate valuation methodology based on company characteristics: DCF for stable cash flow companies, relative valuation for cyclical industries, sum-of-the-parts for conglomerates, option-based models for early-stage growth companies. Justify methodology selection and provide sensitivity analysis.

Statistical validation prompting ensures proper application of statistical techniques:

> For correlation analysis, verify statistical significance (p-value <0.05), check for non-linear relationships, test for structural breaks, and provide confidence intervals. Address potential spurious correlations and ensure economic intuition supports statistical relationships.

Cross-validation prompting encourages verification of quantitative results through alternative approaches:

> Cross-validate DCF results using: (1) Comparable company analysis with appropriate multiples; (2) Precedent transaction analysis with control premiums; (3) Asset-based valuation for downside protection; (4) Economic value added (EVA) analysis for performance validation. Reconcile valuation differences and provide weighted average with confidence intervals.

### Curriculum learning

Curriculum learning strategies present financial concepts in carefully designed sequences that mirror professional financial education and build complexity progressively. This approach often improves the convergence speed and final model performance for complex financial reasoning tasks.

Financial curriculum design typically follows hierarchical knowledge structures:

- 1. **Foundation phase**: Basic financial concepts, accounting principles, mathematical relationships
- 2. **Analytical phase**: Financial ratio analysis, comparative evaluation, trend analysis
- 3. **Integration phase**: Multi-factor analysis, cross-asset relationships, portfolio theory
- 4. **Advanced phase**: Complex derivatives, structured products, regulatory frameworks
- 5. **Application phase**: Real-world case studies, crisis scenarios, professional applications

Difficulty progression metrics guide curriculum advancement based on model performance and confidence measures. Financial curriculum implementations often use multiple difficulty dimensions including conceptual complexity, numerical sophistication, and analytical depth:

Difficulty(x) = α · Conceptual(x) + β · Numerical(x) + γ · Analytical(x) (2.29)

Adaptive curriculum strategies modify training progression based on model performance and learning patterns. If the model struggles with credit analysis concepts, the curriculum might provide additional exposure to credit-related materials before advancing to portfolio-level risk assessment.

### Continual learning and model updates

As we’ve already discussed, financial markets do not stand still. Regulations evolve, instruments emerge, and market regimes shift. Models must, therefore, adapt without losing previously acquired knowledge and capabilities. Continual learning approaches enable model updates without catastrophic forgetting of established financial knowledge.

**Elastic Weight Consolidation (EWC)** protects important parameters from modification during updates by adding penalty terms for changes to critical weights:

$\mathcal{L}_{EWC} = \mathcal{L}_{new} + \lambda \sum_i F_i(\theta_i - \theta_{A,i})^2$ (2.30)

where $F_i$ represents the Fisher Information Matrix diagonal elements indicating parameter importance, and $\theta_{A,i}$ are the parameters from previous tasks [Kirkpatrick et al., 2017].

In financial contexts, parameters associated with core relationships such as time value of money or risk-return trade-offs may warrant stronger protection. Moreover, memory replay systems maintain representative samples from previous training phases to prevent forgetting during model updates. Financial memory replay must carefully select examples that capture critical financial relationships while complying with data retention and privacy policies.

More structurally, Progressive Neural Networks expand model capacity with each learning phase while preserving links to previously acquired knowledge. This makes them particularly well-suited to financial domains, where regulatory shifts, market innovations, and new instruments continually reshape the analytical landscape.
