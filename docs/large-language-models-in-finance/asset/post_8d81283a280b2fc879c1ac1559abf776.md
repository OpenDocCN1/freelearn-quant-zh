
## 7.3.2 Fraud detection and case investigation

Fraud-detection systems often use:

- rules (e.g. large or unusual transactions) [Bolton and Hand, 2002],
- anomaly-detection models on transaction graphs [Akoglu et al., 2015],
- device, session, and behavioral signals, subject to privacy and proportionality controls.

These systems generate alerts that must be investigated by human analysts. LLMs can significantly improve *investigation efficiency* by:

- summarizing transaction histories and associated metadata,
- explaining, in natural language, why a pattern may be suspicious or benign [Hilal et al., 2022],
- suggesting additional data points to check.

### Implementation sketch: Fraud-alert summarization

Listing 7.5: Summarizing fraud alerts with an LLM

```
# Illustrative template. Not production code.

def summarize_alert(alert, llm):
    """
    alert: dict with keys such as:
    - "transactions": list of dicts with date, amount, currency, merchant, country, etc.
    - "customer_profile": dict with basic KYC info
    - "rules_triggered": list of rule identifiers
    llm: wrapper with llm.generate(prompt: str) -> str
    """
    tx_lines = []
    for tx in alert["transactions"]:
        tx_lines.append(
            f"{tx['date']} {tx['amount']} {tx['currency']} "
            f"at {tx['merchant']} ({tx['country']})"
        )
    tx_block = "\n".join(tx_lines)

    prompt = f"""
You are a fraud analyst at a bank.

Customer profile:
{alert['customer_profile']}

Rules triggered:
{alert['rules_triggered']}

Transactions:
{tx_block}

Task:
1. Summarize the transaction pattern in 3-4 bullet points.
2. Explain why this pattern might be suspicious or benign.
3. Suggest what additional information an analyst should check.
"""
    return llm.generate(prompt)

# Example usage (conceptual):
# summary = summarize_alert(alert_json, llm_client)
```

Such a function becomes part of an analyst workstation, where:

- each alert is paired with a structured summary and rationale,
- analysts can amend or correct the summary,
- feedback is logged and can be used to refine prompts or models [Lebichot et al., 2020].

## 7.3.3 KYC and AML workflows

Know your customer (KYC) and anti-money laundering (AML) processes rely on:

- identification documents,
- corporate registries and beneficial ownership data,
- address verification,
- transaction-monitoring systems, and
- adverse-media and sanctions screening [Weber et al., 2018].

LLMs support these processes by:

- parsing and normalizing documents into structured forms,
- synthesizing customer profiles (including complex corporate structures),
- summarizing adverse-media hits in a standardized way, and
- drafting suspicious activity reports (SARs) with clear, consistent language.

Importantly, LLMs operate under the following strict constraints:

- they do not decide onboarding or de-risking outcomes; they support analysts;
- they do not replace sanctions-screening engines or rule-based AML systems; they explain and prioritize alerts;
- all outputs are subject to human review and regulatory requirements [European Union, 2015].

## 7.3.4 Operational workflows and documentation

Beyond credit and AML, back-office operations produce and process a vast array of unstructured artifacts such as:

- confirmations, statements, and reconciliations,
- internal policy documents and procedures,
- exception reports and incident logs, and
- email threads between operations, front office, and counterparties.

LLMs can:

- read exception queues and cluster semantically similar issues,
- suggest likely root causes based on historical incidents, and
- generate draft responses or internal tickets with the relevant context attached.

The architecture is similar to other use cases: RAG over internal documentation [Guu et al., 2020], an LLM to propose drafts, and humans to review and act.

## 7.4 Four audited implementation cases

The preceding sections map the application landscape. This section makes the design standard concrete through four end-to-end cases. Each case is organized around the same seven questions: what decision is supported, which information is admissible, what the model may do, what deterministic systems must do, which human owns the outcome, how the system is evaluated, and what causes it to stop.

### 7.4.1 Case 1: Point-in-time earnings-call research

**Decision supported.** An equity analyst needs a same-day research note comparing management’s new guidance with the previous quarter, consensus expectations, and the latest filed financial statements. The system supports research prioritization; it does not place a trade or publish a recommendation without analyst approval.

**Admissible inputs.** Every input carries an availability timestamp, not merely a period date. The transcript, webcast slides, filing, prior guidance, consensus snapshot, price data, and house-research restrictions must all satisfy \(a_j \le t\), where \(t\) is the analyst’s decision time. A revised transcript received later must not silently enter a historical evaluation.

#### Pipeline.

1. Ingest the transcript and slides, preserve speaker turns, tables, page references, and source hashes.
2. Retrieve the previous call, current and prior filings, and the authorized consensus snapshot using issuer, fiscal period, and availability-time filters.
3. Extract reported values and guidance ranges into a typed schema.

4. Recompute growth rates, margin changes, guidance midpoints, and consensus deltas in deterministic code.

5. Generate a claim-level summary in which each material statement links to transcript passages, filing tables, or calculated objects.

6. Apply restricted-list, material-non-public-information, and distribution-policy checks before the note is shown or shared.

A compact output contract is:

$o = (\text{summary, guidance changes, reconciled metrics, citations, uncertainties, policy flags})$

The language model may explain that management sounded more cautious, but a separate classifier or reviewer should assign any sentiment score, and the score should not be treated as a causal trading signal without an out-of-time experiment.

**Evaluation.** The research system is tested on chronologically held-out calls. Measures include extraction precision/recall, numerical reconciliation rate, claim-level faithfulness, unsupported-claim rate, time to first usable draft, analyst correction rate, and decision impact. A useful productivity comparison records active analyst minutes, not only elapsed system latency.

**Stop conditions.** The workflow rejects or escalates when the transcript is incomplete, a consensus snapshot lacks an as-of time, a number fails reconciliation, a material claim lacks evidence, or distribution restrictions are unresolved.

## 7.4.2 Case 2: KYC/AML investigation assistance

**Decision supported.** An investigator must decide whether an alert can be closed, requires more information, or should be escalated for suspicious-activity review. The LLM prepares an evidence-linked case narrative; it does not make the filing decision.

**Admissible inputs.** The case may include customer and beneficial-owner records, transaction histories, alert rules, sanctions and politically exposed person results, adverse-media documents, prior cases, and analyst notes. Access is purpose-limited and jurisdiction-sensitive. Search results must retain source, retrieval time, language, identity-match confidence, and duplicate relationships.

#### Pipeline.

1. Resolve entities using deterministic identifiers and reviewed matching thresholds; do not let the LLM merge customers or beneficial owners on name similarity alone.

2. Build a transaction timeline and calculate amounts, velocity, geographic dispersion, peer deviations, and flow-of-funds summaries outside the model.

3. Retrieve only authorized adverse-media and policy evidence.

4. Ask the model to organize the case into observations, plausible explanations, contradictions, missing information, and recommended investigative steps.
