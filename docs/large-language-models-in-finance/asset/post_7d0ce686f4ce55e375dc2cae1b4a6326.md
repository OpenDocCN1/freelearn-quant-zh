
The top-n expansions at a given depth are conditionally independent and are therefore typically batched or distributed across GPUs in production. Without this parallelization, latency grows approximately with the product of search depth and the number of evaluated branches. The depth, branching factor, and number of retained nodes must consequently be treated as explicit latency-budget parameters rather than purely accuracy-oriented hyperparameters.

This multi-path exploration can be valuable during regime transitions, provided the branch scores and constraints are validated against decision-relevant outcomes. Traditional optimization might converge on a single "optimal" portfolio based on historical relationships. ToT exploration reveals how different assumptions about regime persistence lead to divergent allocations, enabling more robust decision-making under uncertainty. While ToT focuses on exploring multiple reasoning paths, recent research has also emphasized the quality of those paths through iterative self-evaluation and refinement.

### 13.1.3 Self-refinement and Monte Carlo Tree Search (MCTS)

Recent advances in self-refinement enable models to critique and improve their own reasoning [Madaan et al., 2023]. This capability transforms static analysis into dynamic processes that evolve toward better solutions. In financial applications, self-refinement manifests as iterative model development where initial valuations undergo successive refinements based on identified weaknesses.

The process begins with a baseline analysis using standard methodologies. The model then adopts a critical perspective, identifying assumptions requiring further scrutiny. Are growth projections consistent with industry dynamics? Do margin assumptions reflect competitive pressures? How sensitive are conclusions to key parameters? As an illustrative example, a discounted cash-flow analysis might begin with revenue growth of 8%, an operating margin of 19%, and a weighted average cost of capital of 8%. The self-critique compares these inputs with sector cyclicality, current funding spreads, and historical margin dispersion; it may conclude that the baseline is optimistic, revise growth to 5%, the margin to 17.5%, and the discount rate to 10.2%, and then rerun the valuation under downside and recovery scenarios. The practical output is not merely a new point estimate but a documented explanation of which assumptions changed and how the valuation range moved. A defensible stopping rule requires small changes across successive iterations, no unresolved hard-constraint violations, and stability of the decision under prespecified stresses; otherwise, the case is escalated to a human reviewer.

Monte Carlo Tree Search (MCTS) takes this further by enabling systematic exploration of complex strategy spaces. A common selection rule balances exploitation of branches with high estimated value and exploration of branches with greater uncertainty:

```
a^* = arg max_a \left[ \widehat{Q}(s, a) + c \sqrt{\frac{\log N(s)}{1 + N(s, a)}} - \lambda \text{Penalty}(s, a) \right],
```

where \(\widehat{Q}(s, a)\) is the estimated risk-adjusted value of action \(a\), \(N(s)\) and \(N(s, a)\) are visit counts, and the penalty captures constraint violations, excessive costs, or unacceptable tail exposure. In a derivatives application, rollouts simulate the payoff distribution under volatility, jump, and liquidity scenarios; backpropagation then updates both mean utility and downside-risk estimates. A path is promising when its upper-confidence score is high, not merely when its historical average payoff is high. This exploration–exploitation mechanism prevents the reasoning process from becoming a random walk and directs compute toward branches that are valuable, uncertain, and still feasible [Kocsis and Szepesvári, 2006, Browne et al., 2012].

Having explored methods for enhancing reasoning quality, we now turn to the role of inference-time computation in improving model performance.

### 13.1.4 Scaling test-time compute for financial intelligence

The strategic allocation of computational resources during inference emerges as a critical consideration for financial AI systems [Snell et al., 2024]. Rather than uniformly applying the same analytical depth to all problems, adaptive systems allocate resources based on decision importance, complexity, and time constraints. This principle challenges the conventional focus on model size, suggesting that smaller models with sophisticated inference procedures can outperform larger models using simple forward passes.

Financial institutions face a strategic choice between investing in larger foundation models and developing inference architectures that extract more value from smaller models. Neither option dominates universally. On some tasks, structured search, self-refinement, or additional test-time computation can allow a smaller model to outperform a larger model using a single forward pass; on other tasks, the extra latency and complexity provide little benefit. The trade-off should be established through task-level evaluation.

| Task type | Complexity | Compute scaling benefit |
|---|---|---|
| Daily risk reports | Low | Minimal |
| Credit decisions | Medium | Moderate |
| M&A valuations | High | Significant |
| Fraud detection | High | Significant |
| Portfolio optimization | Very high | Substantial |

The return on test-time compute is likely to vary across financial applications. Table 13.1 should therefore be read as an engineering hypothesis rather than a universal empirical ranking: routine report generation may need little deliberation, whereas complex valuation or structuring tasks may justify deeper search. Each tier requires a measured quality-latency-cost curve and a clear escalation policy.

As financial decision-making relies on diverse forms of information, the next frontier lies in enabling models to reason across multiple modalities.

## 13.2 Multimodality in financial analysis

Financial analysis is inherently multimodal. Analysts combine earnings transcripts, financial statements, price charts, and news to form investment views. They may also interpret presentation cues, analyze satellite imagery for economic signals, and monitor social-media sentiment. Systems restricted to a single modality capture only fragments of this information landscape. Multimodal Large Language Models move toward integrated cross-modal processing by combining diverse data types within a unified analytical framework.

The challenge of multimodal understanding extends beyond simple concatenation of different data streams. True multimodal intelligence requires understanding relationships across modalities: verifying that verbal claims in earnings calls align with reported numbers, detecting when chart patterns contradict fundamental analysis, or identifying when unusual options activity precedes news announcements. These cross-modal relationships often contain the most valuable signals, yet they remain invisible to unimodal systems.

### 13.2.1 Architectural foundations

The technical architecture of multimodal systems must bridge the fundamental differences between data types. Text arrives as discrete symbols with grammatical structure. Images present continuous pixel arrays with spatial relationships. Audio contains temporal waveforms with frequency components. Financial tables combine structure with semantics. Each modality requires specialized processing, yet all must ultimately integrate into a coherent understanding.

Modern multimodal architectures achieve this integration through a combination of specialized encoders and shared representation spaces. Vision Transformers process images by dividing them into patches, treating visual analysis as a sequence modeling problem compatible with text processing [Dosovitskiy et al., 2021]. Audio encoders like HuBERT extract linguistic and acoustic features from speech [Hsu et al., 2021]. Table understanding models parse structured data while preserving relationships between cells. These specialized encoders project their respective inputs into a shared embedding space where concepts with similar meanings cluster together regardless of origin modality.

Earlier multimodal systems often relied on late fusion: separate text, image, audio, and tabular models produced independent predictions that were averaged or combined only at the final layer. This design was brittle when timestamps were misaligned or a modality was missing, could not directly compare the meaning of a sentence with the meaning of a chart, and frequently allowed the statistically dominant modality to overwhelm weaker but economically important evidence. It also made contradictions difficult to localize because the representations remained isolated until the final decision.

The development of a shared semantic space helps overcome these limitations by aligning evidence before the final prediction. When properly trained, the embedding for a chart showing revenue growth should be similar to embeddings for text describing that growth or audio of executives discussing it. Cross-modal attention can then retrieve supporting evidence, test consistency, combine complementary information, and identify discrepancies
