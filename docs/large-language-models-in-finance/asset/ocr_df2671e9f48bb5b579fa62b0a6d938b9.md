# 14.1 Beyond prediction

The integration of large language models (LLMs) into finance has often been framed as a predictive challenge: forecasting stock movements, estimating credit default probabilities, or anticipating market sentiment [Sezer et al., 2020, Zhang et al., 2024b]. These applications are important, but they represent only one layer of a broader transition. The emerging frontier is a shift from systems that produce an estimate to systems that maintain state, pursue a goal, call tools, evaluate constraints, and take actions inside a governed workflow [Wang et al., 2024, Xi et al., 2025, Yao et al., 2023b, Schick et al., 2023].

Prediction and agency should not be conflated. A credit model can estimate the probability that a borrower breaches a covenant. An agentic credit workflow must additionally determine which documents are authoritative, retrieve the relevant covenant language, reconcile contradictory data, simulate possible interventions, check approval limits, request human authorization when required, execute an admissible action, and preserve an audit trail. Increasing the parameter count of the predictor does not, by itself, supply persistent memory, permissions, transaction semantics, or institutional accountability.

This broader evolution creates opportunities for faster analysis, more adaptive products, and lower operational friction. It also creates risks that are endogenous to deployment: model-generated actions alter prices, liquidity, counterparties’ incentives, and the information available to other agents. Consequently, the relevant object is not an isolated model but a socio-technical system embedded in a market [Daníelsson et al., 2022].

The chapter develops three connected themes:

+   1. **The rise of agentic finance.** The architectural and cognitive transition from instruction-following models to goal-seeking systems operating under explicit delegation boundaries.
+   2. **Generative Alpha.** Potential value derived from searching over strategies, contracts, execution policies, and market designs rather than merely forecasting a pre-existing target.
+   3. **Systemic risk among interacting agents.** The possibility that shared models, common data, feedback, and strategic interaction amplify shocks or reduce effective market diversity.

# 14.2 The architecture of autonomous financial agents

The transition from predictive LLMs to autonomous agents is not merely a matter of scale; it requires a different systems architecture. A useful financial agent combines a reasoning model with memory, planning, tools, deterministic controls, and a mechanism for learning from outcomes. It should be understood as a bounded decision system rather than as an unconstrained LLM [Wang et al., 2024, Shinn et al., 2023].
