# 6.10 Limitations and open problems

The architecture described in this chapter reduces the authority of the language model and makes failures more detectable, but it does not eliminate risk. Model error, malicious insiders, supply-chain compromise, flawed deterministic code, bad market data, weak approvals, and poor human judgment can still undermine a financial workflow. The control envelope should therefore be understood as a risk-reduction architecture, not as a proof that MCP-assisted systems are safe in all settings.

Several open problems remain:

+   Tool-selection reliability under distribution shift. Institutions need better ways to evaluate whether a model selects the right tool when market conditions, document types, prompts, or user behavior differ from the validation set.

+   Non-interference between servers sharing one host context. A host may connect to several MCP servers with different permissions, data classifications, and business purposes. Stronger methods are needed to show that one server cannot influence, observe, or corrupt another server's context or outputs.

+   Point-in-time provenance across vendors. Financial workflows often depend on documents, prices, positions, filings, and model outputs from different providers. Standardized provenance, revision linkage, and availability-time metadata remain difficult to enforce across vendors.

+   Automation bias in approval interfaces. Human review is meaningful only if reviewers can challenge the system. More work is needed to measure when users over-trust model-generated explanations, accept defaults too quickly, or fail to notice missing evidence.

+   Privacy-preserving audit evidence. Audit records must preserve accountability without creating unnecessary exposure of client data, positions, prompts, or confidential business logic. Better methods are needed for balancing reconstruction, privacy, and regulatory access.

+   Continuous certification of server and schema changes. MCP servers, schemas, prompts, policies, and dependencies may change between periodic reviews. Institutions need practical methods for continuous revalidation and certification of these changes.

Post-quantum cryptography, confidential computing, and federated architectures may become relevant for particular threat models, especially where long-term confidentiality, shared analytics, or third-party exposure are central concerns. However, they should not distract from immediate controls: least privilege, safe credential handling, schema validation, deterministic computation, point-in-time evidence, idempotency, and complete auditability. These controls are not frontier research problems; they are the baseline for making MCP-assisted financial workflows governable today.
