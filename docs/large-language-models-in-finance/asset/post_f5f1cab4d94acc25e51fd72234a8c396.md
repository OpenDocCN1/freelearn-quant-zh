Numerical tokenization strategies must balance preservation of numerical relationships with tokenization efficiency. Several approaches including the following have proven useful for financial applications:

- **Magnitude-cased tokenization**: Separate tokens for different numerical magnitudes (thousands, millions, billions)
- **Precision-aware tokenization**: Different treatment for whole numbers versus decimals
- **Financial format tokenization**: Specialized handling for percentages, currency amounts, ratios
- **Contextual numerical tokenization**: Different tokenization based on numerical context (prices vs. ratios vs. dates)

Scientific notation handling is particularly important in financial contexts involving very large values or precise percentage changes. The tokenization scheme must preserve the relationship between mantissa and exponent while enabling efficient model processing.

Token_unified = Concat(Token_text, Token_numerical, Token_structural) (2.32)

## 2.6.2 Training strategies for large-scale models

Training large-scale financial language models requires sophisticated strategies that address computational scalability, training stability, and domain-specific optimization objectives. In this section, we will examine the practical mechanics of training at scale: coordinating distributed updates, managing convergence behavior, and incorporating domain-aware constraints so that the resulting model learns financially coherent reasoning.

### Distributed training architectures

Training large-scale financial models inevitably exceeds the capacity of a single device. As a result, distributed approaches become necessary to coordinate learning across multiple GPUs, nodes, or even data centers while maintaining both efficiency and stability. The choice of distributed training strategy therefore has direct implications for training speed, resource utilization, and ultimately the quality of the resulting model.

The most straightforward approach is data parallelism, which distributes training batches across multiple devices and aggregates their gradients. This enables near-linear scaling of batch sizes and computational throughput. For financial models in particular, data parallelism is often highly effective due to the scale of available corpora and the large batch sizes typically required for stable convergence. In its simplest form, global gradients are computed as the average of device-level gradients:

Gradient_{global} = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^{N} Gradient_{device_i} \qquad (2.33)

where N represents the number of participating devices and gradients are averaged across devices to maintain training consistency.

However, as model sizes grow, data parallelism alone may no longer suffice. When parameter counts exceed the memory capacity of a single device, model parallelism becomes necessary. Pipeline parallelism partitions layers across devices, while tensor parallelism distributes individual operations. In practice, financial model implementations frequently employ hybrid strategies that combine data and model parallelism, adapting to both architectural constraints and available hardware resources.

Even with distributed execution, hardware limits often constrain feasible batch sizes. Gradient accumulation provides a practical solution by simulating larger effective batch sizes across multiple forward and backward passes before performing a parameter update. This approach is particularly valuable in financial contexts, where larger batches can contribute to improved training stability and smoother convergence. A simplified formulation is shown below:

```
Algorithm 4 Gradient accumulation for financial model training
Input: Model M, dataset D, accumulation steps K
Output: Trained model M'

Initialize accumulated gradients G_acc = 0
for each training step do
    for k = 1 to K do
        batch <- SampleBatch(D)
        loss <- ComputeLoss(M, batch)
        gradients <- BackwardPass(loss)
        G_acc <- G_acc + gradients / K
    end for
    Update model parameters using G_acc
    Reset G_acc = 0
end for
```

Finally, distributed training introduces communication overhead, particularly during gradient synchronization. Communication optimization techniques, including gradient compression, hierarchical aggregation, and asynchronous updates, can meaningfully reduce this overhead.

### Optimization and training strategies

Beyond distributed coordination, financial model training depends on careful control of the optimization process. Optimization strategies must therefore balance convergence speed with training stability and final model quality. Learning rate scheduling is central to this balance. In financial settings, conservative schedules are typically preferred, with warm-up phases that gradually increase the learning rate to prevent early instability. A common formulation is:

LR(t) = \begin{cases} \text{LR}_{base} \cdot \frac{t}{t_{\text{warmup}}} & \text{if } t \le t_{\text{warmup}} \\ \text{LR}_{base} \cdot \left( \frac{t_{\text{warmup}}}{t} \right)^\alpha & \text{if } t > t_{\text{warmup}} \end{cases} (2.34)

where t_warmup represents the warm-up period and \alpha controls the decay rate.

Mixed-precision training reduces memory requirements and accelerates training while maintaining numerical stability, which is crucial for financial applications. Financial implementations often use conservative mixed precision approaches that maintain higher precision for critical financial computations [Micikevicius et al., 2018].
