These numbers measure a leakage channel under controlled conditions. They are not a measurement of any market, and by the ladder of Section 12.6.1 they remain Status D. What has changed is that they can now be checked.

Table 13.5 shows the fixed-seed anatomy, and three of the five rows say something the design did not anticipate.

The full-sample supervised oracle (A1) raises mean rank IC from 0.0665 to 0.1721: it more than doubles the signal, with a Newey–West _t_ of 15.78 and a block-bootstrap interval of [0.0930, 0.1186] nowhere near zero. That is the easy result. The instructive one is A2.

**A2 does not collapse.** Matching the training count to the clean protocol — so that the only remaining difference is _when_ the labels came from — still leaves a gap of +0.0714 with _t_ = 8.11. The advantage therefore cannot be narrated as “the oracle simply saw more data.” It saw future data, and future data is worth more than an equal quantity of past data. That is the whole argument of this section reduced to one row of a table, and it is the row to ask about first when someone reports that their model improved after “retraining on the full history.”

**Full-sample normalization (C) has exactly zero effect — and that is not good news.** The ΔIC is 0.0000 to four places, with _t_ = 0.00. The reason is arithmetic: a global affine rescaling of a score cannot change the _cross-sectional ranks_ of that score on any given day, and rank IC depends only on ranks. The leakage channel is entirely real — the normalizer has seen the whole sample — but this particular metric is structurally blind to it. Do not read the zero as an all-clear. Read it as a warning that a metric can be incapable of detecting a violation that is unambiguously present, which is exactly why the audit in this section is stated over _artifacts_ rather than over test statistics. A clean number is not a clean pipeline.

Label admission before realization (B, +0.0011) and timestamp fiction (D, +0.0007) are small in this calibration. Both are genuine channels; both are weak here because the simulated post-cutoff articles carry the same distribution as the pre-cutoff ones, so the extra coverage that D manufactures is mostly noise. In a real corpus, where news arriving after the close is systematically different from news arriving before it, D is a far larger channel. That sensitivity is a property of the calibration, not of the method, and it is why this table measures _mechanism_ rather than magnitude.

A multi-seed run reproduces the ordering. For each seed, the clean and violating protocols are evaluated on the same simulated events, timestamps, latent sentiment, and return shocks, with a semantic break imposed at the midpoint. Over 60 seeds the mean IC gap is +0.0911 for A1 (interval [+0.0776, +0.1028]) and +0.0640 for A2 (interval [-0.0520, +0.0806]). Both exclude zero by wide margins, and the A2 gap survives the drift break. The result therefore isolates the information-set advantage of future labels from the simple advantage of a larger training count.

A stronger claim — that look-ahead redistributes the advantage *across* the break, giving some back before it and recovering more after — is plausible on this evidence and is *not* asserted here, because the shipped script does not compute the windowed pre/post decomposition that would establish it. It is stated as an open question in Section 13.5.6, not as a finding.

Two qualifications govern all of these numbers. The signal-to-noise ratio is deliberately generous, so absolute Sharpe levels claim nothing about live markets; and a full-sample supervised oracle is a severe stress test, not an exact analogue of ordinary self-supervised pretraining, whose contamination is application specific and can even be negative.

### 13.5.5 Componentwise provenance: dictionaries, transformers, agents

For a static dictionary, the audit record must include the source corpus cutoff, construction rule, and release date. The Loughran–McDonald lists, for example, were constructed from 1994–2008 10-K filings and published in 2011 [Loughran and McDonald, 2011]; applying the published list to a 2000 backtest combines post-2000 source data with an artifact that did not exist, while applying it to 2010 raises mainly release-availability concerns.

No single “2011 vintage” label captures that distinction. For a transformer, provenance is componentwise: FinBERT-style models carry separate general-pretraining, domain-pretraining, and fine-tuning cutoffs [Araci, 2019]; a dated base model paired with a future sentiment head is contaminated; a current tokenizer can import vocabulary absent from the base model’s era; and a historical checkpoint served through a mutable API alias is not replayable.

For an LLM or agentic system, the artifact expands to the complete execution graph: a prompt selected after observing the backtest is evaluation leakage; a retrieval index built today returns ex-post documents no matter what date the prompt claims, and a tool call can quietly fetch current company profiles or ticker maps. Prompting a current model to “pretend it is 2008” is not a provenance control, and neither is declaring a model generic; the operative test is counterfactual — could changing future-only records alter the historical score?

Contamination has been demonstrated in multiple settings, and it must be treated as a live artifact-level risk unless it has been ruled out for the exact model, data, retrieval, prompt, and deployment vintage in use. Section 13.4 catalogues the channels, and the LLM-sentiment literature supplies the worked cases [Lopez-Lira and Tang, 2023]. That is a weaker claim than “no longer in dispute,” and it is the one the evidence supports: absence of contamination is a property of a specific artifact, not of a literature.

Two complementary responses have matured. _Prevention_ builds admissibility in by design, through chronologically bounded model families and point-in-time checkpoint sequences. _Detection_ applies statistical tests for look-ahead behaviour when provenance is incomplete. Neither substitutes for the artifact record itself.

Four remedies follow, in descending order of strength: use a chronologically bounded artifact restricted to dates after its operational release; build the scorer from scratch on each walk-forward prefix; maintain a versioned sequence of historical dictionaries, checkpoints, prompts, and retrieval snapshots; or label the result _temporally unverifiable_ rather than point-in-time. That last label is the honest default, and it is used far less often than it should be.

### 13.5.6 What this experiment does not settle

Three questions are left open, and they are listed rather than papered over because the companion script does not answer them. First, whether look-ahead redistributes the advantage across a regime break — conceding IC before it and recovering more after — rather than merely raising the average: establishing this requires a windowed pre/post decomposition that is not implemented.

Second, whether the rank-IC gaps reported above survive translation into portfolio returns after costs; the script computes rank IC and does not compute portfolio returns or Sharpe, so no such claim is made. Third, whether the effect sizes transfer to a real corpus with a real exchange calendar; the world here is synthetic and its session index is abstract.

Each of these is a reason to distrust a number, not a reason to distrust the mechanism. The channels in Table 13.4 are structural and hold whatever the effect size turns out to be. The magnitudes are Status D.
