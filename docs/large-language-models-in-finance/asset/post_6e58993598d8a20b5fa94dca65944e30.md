## Figure 14.1: Conceptual architecture of a governed autonomous financial agent

decision process that observes a history, forms a belief state, proposes an action, and may alter an institutional state if and only if an independent control layer permits the action. This distinction is the central theoretical upgrade required when moving from predictive finance to agentic finance. A predictor is evaluated by the accuracy of an output; a delegated agent must be evaluated by the distribution of actions it induces, the mechanisms through which those actions are authorized, and the counterfactual consequences of executing them [Noguer i Alonso et al., 2026, Pearl, 2009].

Let \( H_t \) denote the replayable history available at time \( t \). It contains observations, prompts, retrieved documents, position snapshots, tool traces, approvals, rejected actions, model versions, and relevant market states. Let \( \mathcal{A}_t \) be the action space and let \( K_t(\cdot \mid H_t) \) be the conditional law of the agent's proposed action. At the behavioral level, the deployed agent is therefore a stochastic kernel

```
\( K_t : H_t \times \mathcal{F}_{\mathcal{A}_t} \longrightarrow [0, 1], \quad \widetilde{A}_t \sim K_t(\cdot \mid H_t), \)
```

where \( \widetilde{A}_t \) denotes a proposed action rather than an executed action. This kernel view is deliberately substrate-neutral: a human analyst, a rules engine, a reinforcement-learning policy, an LLM with tools, or a committee can all be represented by their conditional action law once the history and action spaces are fixed. Substrate neutrality does not imply substrate indifference. A language model may be valuable for semantic parsing and hypothesis generation, while a deterministic engine may be superior for a certifiable limit check. The kernel defines the behavioral object; the governance architecture decides which component may hold authority.

## Principle: Behavioral primacy for financial agents

A claim about an autonomous financial agent should ultimately be expressible as a statement about its history-conditioned action process. Persona, chain-of-thought text, verbal intention, and self-reported confidence are admissible evidence only when they improve prediction or control of the action law under a stated protocol.

This principle prevents a common category error. A model that says it will respect a mandate has not yet respected the mandate. A model that produces a prudent explanation has not yet produced an admissible order. A model that refuses a risky request in a benchmark has not thereby proved that a future tool call cannot violate an entitlement or capital limit. The audit object is the action process together with the evidence and controls surrounding it, not the rhetorical quality of the model's answer.

A governed deployment separates belief formation, proposal, admissibility projection, execution, memory, and monitoring. One compact representation is the controlled-evidence tuple

```
\( \mathbb{A}_t = (\rho_t, \pi_t, \Pi_t, E_t, M_t, J), \)
```

with operational chain

```
\( H_t \xrightarrow{\rho_t} b_t \xrightarrow{\pi_t} \widetilde{A}_t \xrightarrow{\Pi_t} A_t, \)
\( A_t \xrightarrow{E_t} S_{t+1} \xrightarrow{M_t} H_{t+1}, \quad J \text{ monitors risk, conduct, and auditability.} \)
```

Here \(\rho_t\) maps histories and observations to a controlled belief state \(b_t\); \(\pi_t\) is the proposal policy or semantic kernel; \(\Pi_t\) is the external admissibility projection; \(E_t\) is the permissioned execution adapter; \(M_t\) is the memory and audit update; and \(J\) is the monitored institutional objective. The notation is intentionally strict. The proposal \(\widetilde{A}_t\) is what the model emits. The executed action \(A_t\) is what the institution commits to after validation. The architecture is sound only if these are not confused.

The admissibility projection is the mathematical form of the governance gate. Let \(\text{Act}(H_t)\) be the set of institutionally admissible actions at history \(H_t\). This set may encode trading limits, suitability constraints, instrument eligibility, sanctions screens, model-use restrictions, human-approval requirements, and hard capital or liquidity budgets. A shield is a map

\(\Pi_t : H_t \times \mathcal{A}_t \longrightarrow \mathcal{A}_t \cup \{\perp\} \quad (14.6)\)

where \(\perp\) denotes rejection or escalation. The shield is valid when

\(\Pi_t(H_t, a) \in \text{Act}(H_t) \cup \{\perp\} \quad \text{for every history-action pair } (H_t, a). \quad (14.7)\)

If the executor runs only non-rejected actions returned by \(\Pi_t\), then every executed action is admissible by construction. This is not a claim that the system is optimal, fair, profitable, or immune to all failures. It is a pointwise safety statement: hard constraints enforced outside the model do not depend on the model's internal compliance. This is the financial analogue of shielding in safe reinforcement learning, but with predicates written over orders, approvals, entitlements, instrument states, and records rather than over a toy control environment [Alsheikh et al., 2018].

**Proposition 14.2.1** (External shield safety). *Suppose the executor commits only actions of the form* \(A_t = \Pi_t(H_t, \widetilde{A}_t)\) *with* \(A_t \neq \perp\), *and suppose Equation (14.7) holds. Then every executed action satisfies* \(A_t \in \text{Act}(H_t)\), *independently of the distribution of the proposal* \(\widetilde{A}_t\).

_Proof._ For the realized history \(H_t\) and proposed action \(\widetilde{A}_t\), validity of the shield implies that \(\Pi_t(H_t, \widetilde{A}_t)\) is either \(\perp\) or an element of \(\text{Act}(H_t)\). The executor runs no action in the first case and an admissible action in the second. The conclusion is pointwise and therefore independent of the model, prompt, sampling temperature, or tool wrapper that generated \(\widetilde{A}_t\). \(\square\)

The strongest implication is architectural. High-stakes authority should not be located in the stochastic text generator. It should be located in typed commitments, deterministic checks, proof-carrying calculations when possible, permissioned adapters, and replayable logs. In a trading assistant, the LLM may parse a natural-language instruction; the order-management system should validate instrument status, account permissions, restricted-list status, price collars, participation caps, and pre-trade risk. In a credit assistant, the LLM may summarize evidence; adverse-action reasons, protected-class exclusions, and approval thresholds should be governed by deterministic policy and independent validation.

Statistical gates extend the same idea to uncertain predicates. Some constraints are not binary facts but estimated risks. Consider an action \(a\) with loss \(L_a \in [0, M]\) and a tail-risk budget $B$ expressed in terms of conditional value-at-risk, \(\rho(L) = \text{CVaR}_\alpha(L)\). If \(\widehat{\rho}_n(L_a)\) is an empirical CVaR estimate from \(n\) scenarios, a naive shield that admits \(a\) whenever \(\widehat{\rho}_n(L_a) \le B\) is vulnerable to optimistic underestimation. Under bounded losses and a declared empirical-CDF concentration model, a conservative DKW-style margin for the CVaR estimate can be written as

\(\epsilon(n, \eta) = \frac{M}{1 - \alpha} \sqrt{\frac{\log(1/\eta)}{2n}},\) (14.8)

_This is an illustrative conservative margin, not a proved finite-sample certificate._ A genuine certificate would have to fix the one- or two-sided DKW form, the i.i.d. or dependent scenario model, the empirical-CVaR estimator convention, the derivation from CDF sup-norm error to CVaR error, whether the bound holds simultaneously across candidate actions, and the confidence convention. None of those is supplied here, and until they are, the correct reading of the display below is a design heuristic that errs toward caution — useful as a shield, inadmissible as a guarantee.

**Where the margin goes matters, and it is easy to get backwards.** Suppose the gate admits whenever \(\widehat{\rho}_n(L_a) \le B\) and one then concludes, on the event that the concentration bound holds,

\(\rho(L_a) \le \widehat{\rho}_n(L_a) + \epsilon(n, \eta) \le B + \epsilon(n, \eta).\) (14.9)

This is a true statement, and it is _not_ budget compliance. It certifies a _relaxed_ budget \(B + \epsilon\): the admitted action may exceed the stated budget \(B\) by up to the margin, which is precisely the loosening the gate was built to prevent. A shield that reports “within budget” on the strength of (14.9) is reporting compliance with a budget nobody approved.

To certify the budget that was actually declared, the margin must be _reserved on the admission side_, not spent on the conclusion side. Admit \(a\) only if

\(\widehat{\rho}_n(L_a) + \epsilon(n, \eta) \le B,\) (14.10)

whereupon, on the same event, \(\rho(L_a) \le \widehat{\rho}_n(L_a) + \epsilon(n, \eta) \le B\) and the population budget holds. The two rules differ by exactly one \(\epsilon\), they look almost identical on the page, and only one of them enforces what it says.

The distinction deserves a name in any control document that uses it:

- **Budget compliance.** Admission requires \(\widehat{\rho}_n + \epsilon \le B\). Conclusion: \(\rho \le B\). Fewer actions pass; the ones that do respect the declared limit.

- **Bounded exceedance.** Admission requires \(\widehat{\rho}_n \le B\). Conclusion: \(\rho \le B + \epsilon\). More actions pass, and the effective limit is \(B + \epsilon\), which must then be the number written in the risk mandate.

Either is defensible. Choosing one and reporting the other is not, and the failure is silent: the gate returns `ADMIT`, the log records “within budget,” and the limit that was actually enforced appears nowhere.
