
- 5. Require every factual statement to reference a transaction, customer record, screening result, or source document.
- 6. Route the case through an investigator and, where required, a second-line or money-laundering-reporting approval.

The system should represent uncertainty explicitly. A media article about a similarly named person is an unresolved identity hypothesis, not proof. A large transfer is an observation, not by itself suspicious intent.

### Evaluation.
In addition to summary quality, measure entity-linking errors, omitted material transactions, unsupported allegations, escalation precision and recall, investigator correction rate, time saved, protected-attribute leakage, and consistency across languages. A benchmark should preserve realistic class imbalance and costs: missing a material case and over-escalating thousands of benign alerts are different failures.

### Stop conditions.
The system escalates when identity resolution is ambiguous, a sanctions source is unavailable, protected data would cross a jurisdictional boundary, the model generates an unsupported allegation, or the case requires a legal conclusion.

## 7.4.3 Case 3: Covenant monitoring and credit review

### Decision supported.
A credit team needs to determine whether a borrower remains compliant with a leverage, coverage, liquidity, or reporting covenant and whether a waiver or deeper review may be needed.

### Admissible inputs.
The authoritative set includes the executed agreement, amendments, definitions, schedules, borrower financial statements, compliance certificate, permitted accounting adjustments, and the institution's approved calculation methodology. Drafts and superseded agreements remain available for lineage but cannot be treated as operative.

### Pipeline.

- 1. Segment the agreement by clause hierarchy and preserve cross-references and defined terms.
- 2. Extract the covenant formula, test dates, thresholds, baskets, cure provisions, and exceptions into a structured representation.
- 3. Link every extracted field to exact pages and clauses and require legal or credit validation before first use.
- 4. Map financial-statement values to the agreed definitions, including add-backs, exclusions, currency conversion, and averaging conventions.
- 5. Calculate the covenant in a deterministic engine and compare the result with the threshold and any headroom requirement.
- 6. Generate a review memo that separates contract text, calculated facts, assumptions, open legal interpretations, and proposed action.

For a leverage covenant, the machine-readable object might contain

```
Leverage_t = \frac{\text{Debt}_t - \text{PermittedCash}_t}{\text{AdjustedEBITDA}_{t-12:t}}
```

plus the exact definitions and adjustment sources. The LLM helps interpret and map language; it must not invent an add-back or silently choose among competing definitions.

### Evaluation.
Test clause extraction, definition linking, amendment precedence, numerical agreement with expert calculations, breach classification, and reviewer correction. Report performance separately for standard and highly negotiated documents. A high average extraction score is insufficient if the model misses rare but material exceptions.

### Stop conditions.
Escalate when amendments conflict, a defined term cannot be resolved, source figures are unaudited or unavailable, permitted adjustments require judgment, or the calculated headroom is within a documented uncertainty band.

## 7.4.4 Case 4: Governed portfolio-research workstation

### Decision supported.
A portfolio manager asks for an evidence-linked comparison of several securities, scenario effects on the existing portfolio, and a draft investment memo. The system may support a proposal but does not bypass the order-management, mandate, pre-trade, or approval controls.

### Pipeline architecture.
A supervisor coordinates specialist components for filings, news, quantitative analysis, portfolio risk, and compliance. Each specialist receives the minimum context required for its task and returns a typed object. The supervisor cannot execute trades. A separate policy service checks restricted lists, client mandates, concentration, liquidity, turnover, and approval requirements.

### Evaluation:
Use point-in-time research tasks and portfolio snapshots. Measure retrieval recall, evidence faithfulness, numerical reconciliation, scenario consistency, restricted-list and mandate test accuracy, recommendation stability, human correction, latency, and cost. Any economic backtest must include transaction costs, realistic availability times, prompt and model version freezing, and all agent memory.

### Stop conditions:
Reject or escalate if data are stale, a security is restricted, a proposed position breaches a limit, liquidity assumptions are missing, claims lack evidence, or the model proposes an action outside the user's mandate.

## 7.4.5 A common release scorecard

The four cases differ in business objective but share a common release standard. Table 7.4.5 prevents a team from declaring success based on one semantic metric.

| Dimension | Representative measures | Release question |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Task quality | Precision/recall, reviewer score, omission rate, correction rate | Does the system produce useful work on a deployment-like sample? |
| Evidence | Retrieval recall, claim support, source authority, availability-time validity | Can every material statement be traced to admissible evidence? |
| Numbers | Reconciliation rate, unit/currency checks, identity violations | Are all material quantities computed or verified deterministically? |
| Policy | Unauthorized-action rate, refusal accuracy, escalation precision/recall | Does the system fail closed and route judgment correctly? |

## 7.5 Cross-cutting blueprint: from data to decisions

The previous sections described individual applications, but financial institutions rarely build each use case as a completely separate system. In practice, they usually deploy shared platforms that support multiple workflows with common data, models, retrieval systems, tools, and governance services [Arnet et al., 2017, Feuerriegel et al., 2024]. The goal is not to create one monolithic application, but to build reusable layers that can support different business functions while preserving access control, auditability, and model-risk oversight.

A realistic data-to-decision blueprint combines the following layers:

- **Data layer:** Market data, fundamental data, documents, transactions, logs, and reference data, all with strong governance, quality controls, and lineage [Baesens et al., 2016].
- **Model layer:** Pretrained LLMs, fine-tuned financial LLMs, smaller task-specific models, time-series models, and graph models [Wu et al., 2023b, Liu et al., 2024c].
- **RAG layer:** Retrieval indexes over filings, research, policies, KYC documents, legal contracts, and operational runbooks [Lewis et al., 2020, Izacard et al., 2022].
- **Agent layer:** Research agents, risk agents, execution-support agents, KYC/AML assistants, and operations assistants [Wang et al., 2024, Li et al., 2023].
- **Integration layer:** Tool-context and system-integration protocols connecting agents to internal APIs, databases, order-management systems (OMS), execution-management systems (EMS), risk engines, compliance systems, and document repositories. MCP-style interfaces are one possible implementation, but the blueprint is not tied to MCP specifically.
- **Governance layer:** Logging, monitoring, explainability, access control, approvals, model-risk controls, audit trails, and regulatory reporting [Barocas et al., 2019, Gebru et al., 2021].

These layers can be reused across business areas. A single institution may share:

- one or more *model hubs*, meaning curated repositories of approved financial models;
- common RAG infrastructure with separate search spaces or namespaces for different business areas;
- a unified agent runtime with role-based access to tools and data; and
- cross-cutting governance services, including model risk management, compliance, and audit [Board of Governors of the Federal Reserve System et al., 2026].

The main advantage of this blueprint is consistency. Different teams can build specialized applications, but they still rely on common controls for data access, model approval, tool permissions, monitoring, and audit. This makes it easier to scale financial LLM systems without losing visibility over how data moves into decisions.

## 7.6 Summary

This chapter showed how pretrained and fine-tuned language models, retrieval-augmented generation, agentic workflows, tool-context integration, and risk-governance layers come together in real financial pipelines. In the front office, we saw how these components support hybrid time-series-plus-text forecasting, sentiment and narrative signals, event-driven and macro strategies, and multi-agent research workstations. In the middle office, the same stack underpins credit analysis and covenant extraction, narrative-consistent scenarios, and the triage and explanation of market-surveillance alerts. In the back office, LLMs assist in credit underwriting, fraud investigation, KYC/AML workflows, and the production and maintenance of operational documentation, all inside controlled processes rather than as stand-alone black boxes.

The common theme is that LLMs create real value only when embedded in disciplined, well-governed systems: robust data engineering and traditional quantitative models, clearly defined roles for agents and tools, sharp boundaries between automated actions and human authority, and comprehensive logging, monitoring, and model-risk management. Across all examples, the key constraint is that LLMs must remain governed, validated, and supervised.
