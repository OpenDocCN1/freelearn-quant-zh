Data engineering is often more important than the model. Every input must be stored point-in-time. Revised macro series, restated financial statements, corrected alternative data, adjusted prices, survivorship-biased universes, and delayed vendor feeds can all create artificial predictability. The deployment pipeline should therefore include timestamped feature stores, data-version hashes, corporate-action handling, universe snapshots, and audit logs that identify exactly what the model knew at each forecast origin.

Point-in-time correctness is not free. In an institutional setting, the natural observation is usually bitemporal: each record has an economic time, which says what period the observation refers to, and an availability or system time, which says when that exact version could have been used.

For high-volume alternative data—satellite estimates, web traffic, credit-card panels, app data, news embeddings, or vendor sentiment scores—this implies append-only storage of vendor drops and corrections rather than destructive overwriting. The operational cost is higher storage, slower as-of joins, more indexing, more cache invalidation, and more complex monitoring of late or revised records.

The latency cost is especially relevant when the decision horizon is intraday: a fully bitemporal research store may be too slow for live execution unless the institution pre-materializes certified snapshot views. The benefit is auditability. A researcher can reconstruct not just the value of a feature, but the exact version of the feature that was available when the forecast was made.

Finally, TSFMs should be treated as forecasting engines, not decision engines. A forecast becomes a trade only after portfolio construction, risk constraints, execution costs, and governance checks. For a forecast \(\widehat{r}_{t+1}\), a simple position might be

\(w_t = \Pi_{\mathcal{C}} \left( \frac{\widehat{r}_{t+1}}{\widehat{\sigma}_{t+1}^2} \right),\)

where \(\Pi_{\mathcal{C}}\) projects the raw signal onto the feasible set of leverage, concentration, liquidity, turnover, and regulatory constraints. This separation is essential: even a good forecast can become a bad portfolio if it ignores costs, capacity, and risk limits.

## 13.4 Pitfalls of LLMs in financial forecasting and backtesting

The most important failure modes of LLM-based financial forecasting are not exotic. They are familiar backtesting errors amplified by modern model complexity. Classical backtests already suffer from look-ahead bias, survivorship bias, data snooping, overfitting, and unrealistic transaction costs. LLM and agentic systems add new channels: pretrained knowledge may overlap the test period, prompts may contain future context, retrieval tools may access documents that were not available at the forecast origin, memory may carry information from earlier evaluation episodes, and code-generation assistants may silently tune to the test set. The result is a backtest that looks rigorous but violates the information structure of the market.

The correct language for these problems is filtration. Let \((\mathcal{F}_t)_{t \ge 0}\) denote the information available to the forecaster at time \(t\). A valid forecast at origin \(t\) must be measurable with respect to \(\mathcal{F}_t\):

\(\widehat{Y}_{t+1:t+H} \in \mathcal{F}_t.\)

Look-ahead bias occurs whenever the model, feature pipeline, prompt, tool, memory, benchmark, or evaluation decision depends on information in \(\mathcal{F}_{t+h} \setminus \mathcal{F}_t\) for some \(h > 0\). In an LLM system, this condition must apply to every component, not merely to the raw price array.

| Layer | Description |
|---|---|
| Data layer | Revised filings, adjusted prices, future constituents, survivorship-biased universes. |
| Prompt layer | Future narratives, post-hoc labels, benchmark summaries, hindsight explanations. |
| Tool layer | Live web search, current databases, non-point-in-time vector stores. |
| Memory layer | Cross-episode state, previous test labels, analyst feedback from the evaluation window. |
| Model layer | Pretraining contamination, benchmark memorization, public test-set exposure. |
| Research layer | Repeated prompt tuning, model selection on test performance, and unreported failed trials. |

## 13.4.1 Classical look-ahead bias

Classical look-ahead bias appears when a feature at time _t_ uses information that was not available until later. Examples include using restated fundamentals as if they were known on the original filing date, applying today's index constituents to historical periods, using split-adjusted or dividend-adjusted prices without respecting announcement timing, or aligning macro variables by period rather than release date. In a time-series LLM pipeline, the same problem appears when the context window is constructed from clean arrays whose timestamps do not correspond to real availability.

A safe feature must satisfy

```
X_t = g(D_{\le t}^{\text{available}}),
```

not merely $X_t = g(D_{\le t}^{\text{dated}})$. The distinction between dated and available is crucial. A quarterly report may describe a period ending on March 31, but it may be filed weeks later. A macroeconomic series may refer to January but be released in February and revised in March. A news summary may describe an event using later interpretations. If the model sees the period label instead of the availability timestamp, it can learn from the future.

## 13.4.2 Temporal leakage in train–validation–test splits

Random splits are usually invalid for financial forecasting. If observations are autocorrelated or share overlapping forecast horizons, randomly assigning examples to train and test allows nearby information to cross the boundary. With overlapping windows, the problem is even stronger. Suppose an input uses _L_ observations and predicts _H_ future days. Two examples with origins $t$ and $t + 1$ share almost the same input and almost the same target horizon. If one is in training and the other is in testing, the test is contaminated.

A leakage-aware split uses rolling-origin evaluation. At each origin $T_j$, the model is trained or selected using data available up to $T_j$, validated on a subsequent but earlier block, and tested on the next block. Formally,

$\mathcal{D}_j^{\text{train}} \subseteq \mathcal{F}_{T_j}, \qquad \mathcal{D}_j^{\text{test}} \subseteq (T_j, T_j + H_j],$

with embargo gaps when labels overlap. In finance, the embargo is not cosmetic. If a label uses forward returns from $t + 1$ to $t + H$, then examples whose windows overlap the test horizon should be removed from training and validation.

## 13.4.3 A worked example, from one of my own papers

The cleanest illustration of the previous section that I know of is a paper I co-authored, and it is more useful as an example than any I could construct.

FinEAS [Gutiérrez-Fandiño et al., 2021] fine-tunes a Sentence-BERT encoder to predict a continuous RavenPack sentiment score from a news headline. The model works: with the backbone frozen it reaches a mean squared error of 0.0556, against 0.2124 for BERT and 0.2108 for a bidirectional LSTM. That is a fourfold improvement over both baselines and it is the number of the paper leads with.

The training, validation, and test sets were split at random, in the proportion 99.5/0.25/0.25, from a corpus of time-stamped financial news.

A random split over time-ordered news is a violation of the filtration condition of Section 13.4: a headline from March may sit in the training set while a headline from the preceding January sits in test, and the model is scored on its ability to interpolate within a period it has already seen. The paper also reported—to its credit—a genuine out-of-time holdout: the two weeks immediately following the training window. The two numbers sit in the same table, and the gap between them is the price of the shortcut.

| MSE (frozen backbone) | Random split | True out-of-time | Ratio |
|---|---|---|---|
| FinEAS (6-month sample) | 0.0556 | 0.1061 | **1.91**× |
| FinEAS (12-month sample) | 0.0654 | 0.1058 | 1.62× |
| FinEAS (24-month sample) | 0.0671 | 0.1065 | 1.59× |
| BERT baseline | 0.2124 | 0.2190 | 1.03× |
| BiLSTM baseline | 0.2108 | 0.2194 | 1.04× |

Table 13.3: [Evidence level 3] Benchmark experiment. Reported error of FinEAS and its baselines under a random split and under a true out-of-time holdout, computed from Table 1 of Gutiérrez-Fandiño et al. [2021]. The ratio column is the authors’ own numbers, divided.

The headline result nearly halves in quality when it is asked to predict forward rather than interpolate. That is worth knowing on its own. But the row that should stop the reader is not FinEAS — it is the two baselines, which barely move at all.
