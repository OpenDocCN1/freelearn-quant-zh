## 13.2.2 Training strategies and objectives

Training multimodal models requires careful consideration of objectives that encourage meaningful cross-modal understanding. Contrastive learning, popularized by CLIP, provides a powerful framework for alignment [Radford et al., 2021]. The model learns to maximize similarity between matched pairs (an image and its correct caption) while minimizing similarity to unmatched pairs. This simple objective, scaled across millions of examples, produces remarkable zero-shot capabilities where models can understand novel combinations of concepts never seen during training.

Financial applications benefit from domain-specific training strategies. Rather than generic image-caption pairs, models train on financial charts paired with analyst descriptions, earnings call audio matched with subsequent price movements, and satellite imagery correlated with reported economic statistics. This domain-specific training captures the unique relationships in financial data: the connection between executive tone and future guidance, the relationship between technical patterns and market sentiment, or the correlation between alternative data signals and fundamental metrics. Algorithm 17 shows a simplified contrastive objective in which text, chart, and fundamental representations from the same firm and period are pulled together while mismatched observations are pushed apart.

```
Algorithm 17 Financial contrastive learning (FinCLIP)
1: Input: Batch of (financial_text, chart, fundamentals) triplets
2: Objective: Learn aligned representations across modalities
3: for each triplet in batch do
4:   e_text = encode_text(earnings_commentary)
5:   e_chart = encode_chart(stock_price_chart)
6:   e_fund = encode_table(financial_ratios)
7:   Compute similarity matrix S_ij between all pairs
8:   Positive pairs: Same company, same period
9:   Negative pairs: Different companies or periods
10: Loss = - log \frac{exp(s_{pos}/\tau)}{\sum exp(s_{all}/\tau)}
11: end for
12: Result: Aligned multimodal embedding space
```

Multi-stage training has emerged as a practical approach to building financial multimodal models. The first stage aligns feature spaces using large datasets of loosely paired data, such as financial documents and their summaries. This stage typically freezes sophisticated pretrained encoders and trains only lightweight projection layers, making it computationally efficient. The second stage fine-tunes the system on high-quality financial tasks with careful human annotation. This might include examples of multimodal financial analysis where experts identify key insights requiring synthesis across modalities.

## 13.2.3 Applications in financial document intelligence

Financial documents exemplify the need for multimodal understanding. An annual report combines narrative discussions, structured financial statements, charts visualizing trends, and footnotes containing critical details. Understanding such documents requires not just reading text but interpreting layouts, understanding table structures, and connecting visual elements with textual descriptions.

Modern document intelligence systems like LayoutLMv3 [Huang et al., 2022] and Donut [Kim et al., 2022] approach documents as inherently visual objects where layout conveys meaning. The position of text on a page, the structure of tables, the relationship between sections—all contain information beyond the raw text. These models process documents as images while incorporating textual understanding, capturing both what is written and how it is presented.

In practice, this enables sophisticated analysis previously requiring human expertise. The system can identify key financial metrics regardless of varying report formats, extract complex table relationships that span multiple pages, detect unusual items buried in footnotes, and verify consistency between narrative claims and reported numbers. This capability proves particularly valuable for large-scale analysis across thousands of documents with varying formats, languages, and accounting standards.

## 13.2.4 Real-time market intelligence

Multimodal models excel at synthesizing real-time information from diverse sources. During earnings announcements, the model simultaneously processes the press release text, executive commentary from the conference call, presentation slides, and concurrent market reactions. This comprehensive view enables nuanced analysis that would be impossible from any single source.

The integration of audio analysis adds a particularly valuable dimension. Beyond transcribed words, the model analyzes speaking pace, tone variations, and hesitation patterns that might indicate uncertainty or evasion. Research shows that vocal features contain predictive information about future performance, yet traditional text-based analysis discards these signals. Multimodal models capture these subtle cues, providing a more complete picture of management sentiment and confidence.

Alternative data integration represents another frontier for multimodal analysis. Satellite imagery of retail parking lots, ship tracking data for trade flows, weather patterns affecting commodities, and social media trends all provide economic signals. Multimodal models can process these diverse inputs alongside traditional financial data, identifying patterns and relationships that human analysts might miss. A model might detect that declining foot traffic in satellite imagery precedes disappointing retail earnings, or that unusual options activity coincides with changes in executive tone during presentations.

These gains come with substantial data-engineering and governance challenges. Alternative sources are often asynchronous, revised after initial publication, geographically uneven, and subject to vendor-specific collection changes. Selection bias, survivorship bias, sparse coverage, ambiguous entity matching, licensing restrictions, privacy concerns, and high acquisition costs can all dominate the apparent modeling advantage. The most serious technical danger is look-ahead leakage—for example, assigning a subsequently corrected satellite or shipment estimate to its original observation date. Production pipelines therefore require point-in-time storage, source-level provenance, timestamp audits, coverage diagnostics, modality-ablation tests, and legal review before the signal is admitted into a forecasting or decision system.

Another major application of LLMs in finance is forecasting temporal patterns and market dynamics.

## 13.3 Time-series LLMs in finance

Time-series foundation models (TSFMs) extend the logic of large language models from symbolic sequences to temporal numerical sequences. In finance, this move is attractive but dangerous. It is attractive because markets generate long, heterogeneous, multi-scale streams: prices, returns, volumes, order-flow imbalance, volatility estimates, macro releases, earnings events, credit spreads, analyst revisions, and textual narratives. It is dangerous because the signal-to-noise ratio is low, regimes change, transaction costs destroy small statistical advantages, and even a small amount of temporal leakage can transform an otherwise ordinary model into an apparently exceptional one. The right way to introduce time-series LLMs in finance is therefore not as a replacement for econometrics, but as a new family of sequence models that must be evaluated under the same point-in-time discipline as any trading or risk model.

The conceptual bridge is simple. A language model receives tokens $x_1, ..., x_T$ and learns conditional structure of the form

$$p_\theta(x_{t+1} | x_{1:t})$$.

A time-series LLM receives a numerical history $y_{1:T}$, possibly with covariates $c_{1:T}$, and learns a forecast distribution

$$p_\theta(y_{T+1:T+H} | y_{1:T}, c_{1:T}, \mathcal{I}_T)$$,

where $H$ is the forecast horizon and $\mathcal{I}_T$ denotes the information set available at time $T$. The mathematical object is not merely a point forecast. In finance, the forecast distribution matters because risk, sizing, hedging, and execution depend on tails, calibration, and uncertainty. A model that improves mean absolute error but worsens tail calibration may be worse for risk management than a simpler baseline.

Recent work has begun to evaluate whether time-series foundation models transfer reliably to financial forecasting tasks, with particular attention to cross-asset generalization, forecast horizons, and the gap between statistical forecasting skill and economically useful trading performance [Rahimikia et al., 2025]. The materials in the companion time-series forecasting study provide a useful empirical frame. In that benchmark, pretrained time-series foundation models were compared against neural baselines on equity return forecasting with an equalized context length of $L = 512$ observations and a 20-business-day forecasting horizon.
