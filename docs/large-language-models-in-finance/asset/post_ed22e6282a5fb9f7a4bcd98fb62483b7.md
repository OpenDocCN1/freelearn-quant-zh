
## Adaptive market making and execution

A third channel is the joint design of quotes, inventory targets, and execution schedules. The relevant value is not simply a predicted price movement; it can arise from reducing adverse selection, managing inventory more efficiently, or routing orders across venues under a cost and risk budget. Any claimed advantage must be evaluated after latency, queue position, partial fills, fees, market impact, and the possibility that competitors adapt to the policy.

## New contracts and market mechanisms

Agents may also help search over auction rules, collateral arrangements, index definitions, insurance triggers, or tokenized claims. This is the most institutionally demanding source of generative value because the generated object changes the market itself. Economic usefulness therefore depends on incentive compatibility, legal enforceability, manipulability, settlement design, and whether liquidity can be sustained after launch.

## 14.3.3 Technical challenges of Generative Alpha

**Remark 14.3.1** (The simulation-to-reality gap). Let \( V^\pi(s) \) denote the value of policy \( \pi \) from state \( s \). A useful measure of simulation mismatch is

$$\Delta_{\text{sim2real}} = |\mathbb{E}_{s \sim p_\text{real}}[V^\pi(s)] - \mathbb{E}_{s \sim p_\text{sim}}[V^\pi(s)]|. \tag{14.26}$$

A large value means that performance depends on assumptions present in the simulator but absent in deployment—for example, unrealistically favorable fills, stationary spreads, unlimited liquidity, or competitors that do not react. Domain randomization exposes the policy to varied simulator parameters, while robust optimization, historical stress periods, walk-forward evaluation, and shadow deployment test whether the result survives plausible misspecification [Tobin et al., 2017, Dulac-Arnold et al., 2019]. None of these techniques eliminates the gap; they make it observable and harder to ignore.

The same search process that produces novel candidates can produce correlated errors. If many institutions use similar foundation models, data vendors, prompts, and objective functions, their independently developed systems may still converge on similar trades. Generative opportunity must therefore be analyzed jointly with systemic interaction.

## 14.4 Systemic risk in a world of interacting agents

The rise of autonomous financial agents creates risks that cannot be assessed by evaluating each model in isolation. The actions of one agent change the state observed by others, and common model architectures can create correlated behavior even without explicit coordination. A useful starting point is a stylized interacting system with \( N \) agents.

### 14.4.1 Mathematical model of agent interactions

Let \( x_i(t) \) denote the state of agent \( i \) and \( \pi_i \) its policy. A continuous-time representation is

$$\frac{dx_i}{dt} = f_i(x_i, \pi_i) + \sum_{j \neq i} g_{ij}(x_i, x_j, \pi_i, \pi_j) + \eta_i(t). \tag{14.27}$$

where $f_i$ captures individual dynamics, $g_{ij}$ captures interaction, and $\eta_i(t)$ represents external shocks and model error. The equation is deliberately general: interaction may occur through prices, order books, common funding constraints, shared data, or direct communication.

Let $x^*$ be an equilibrium and let $z = x - x^*$. Linearization gives

$$\dot{z}(t) = Jz(t), \quad J = \frac{\partial F(x)}{\partial x}\bigg|_{x=x^*}. \tag{14.28}$$

where the off-diagonal blocks of $J$ encode cross-agent coupling.

### **Proposition 14.4.1** (Local Spectral Stability)

For the deterministic linearized system $\dot{z} = Jz$, the equilibrium is locally exponentially stable when

$$\alpha(J) := \max_k \text{Re } \lambda_k(J) < 0, \tag{14.29}$$

and it is unstable when $\alpha(J) > 0$.

*Proof.* The solution is $z(t) = e^{Jt}z(0)$. If all eigenvalues have strictly negative real parts, the matrix exponential decays exponentially; if at least one eigenvalue has positive real part, perturbations along the corresponding invariant direction grow.

The proposition does not imply that more agents or more connectivity are always destabilizing. Connectivity can diversify small shocks and propagate large shocks, producing the familiar “robust-yet-fragile” pattern studied in financial networks [Haldane and May, 2011, Acemoglu et al., 2015]. In an agentic market, greater strategic homogeneity or stronger feedback can move the spectral abscissa toward zero, slowing recovery from shocks, or above zero, creating local amplification. This spectral condition replaces an unsupported universal probability law with a testable stability diagnostic.

### Emergent coordination and competition risk

Agents optimized independently can learn strategies that soften competition or align behavior through the environment. No single control can manage interacting-agent risk. A defensible framework combines observation, intervention, diversity, and coordination.

## 14.5 The future of financial LLMs: Emerging trends

Earlier chapters introduced multimodal and advanced reasoning systems in general terms. This section avoids repeating that material and instead focuses on their specifically financial implications: temporal alignment, provenance, constrained decisions, and integration with an agentic control loop. The maturity distinction remains important. Multimodal extraction from documents, charts, and calls is already emerging; closed-loop multimodal agents and neuro-symbolic controls remain early; a financial singularity is a speculative thought experiment.

### 14.5.1 Multimodality as time-aligned financial evidence

A financial agent rarely receives one clean input. It may need to align a filing published at 08:00, an earnings-call transcript at 09:00, a chart image, an order-book snapshot, and a policy document last revised months earlier. The central challenge is therefore not merely feature fusion but synchronized, provenance-aware fusion.
