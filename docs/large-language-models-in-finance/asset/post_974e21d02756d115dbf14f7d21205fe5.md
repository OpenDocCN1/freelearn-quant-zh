
## 11.3.2 Governance implications and evidence limits

The controlled studies above are useful for identifying possible risks, but they should not be treated as direct evidence of live-market behavior. Their main value is to show where governance controls, validation tests, and evidence boundaries are needed. The following implications translate the research findings into practical areas that financial institutions should examine before deploying or relying on LLM-agent systems.

- **Regime and distribution-shift risk:** Controlled shock treatments show that behavior can change when fundamentals or information change, but there is no universal number of periods within which an LLM will adapt. Institutions should estimate adaptation and recovery for each use case through versioned change-point, stress, and out-of-distribution tests. A system should not operate outside its validated domain merely because it continues to produce fluent outputs.

- **Common-mode and coordination risk:** Shared prompts, objectives, data, providers, and market signals can make independently deployed systems respond similarly. The appropriate control is not an unsupported universal correlation threshold. It is a baseline-and-action framework: measure strategy and order-flow correlation under normal and stressed conditions; identify common dependencies; define institution-specific limits; and link breaches to concrete actions such as limit reductions, strategy diversification, additional human review, or temporary suspension.

- **Semantic and input-channel risk:** There is no defensible basis for applying a universal percentage to the way LLM agents interpret an ambiguous central-bank phrase. The supported conclusion is broader: generative systems can be sensitive to wording, retrieved context, prompt injection, and conflicting instructions. Market-facing systems therefore require semantic perturbation tests, source authentication, provenance checks, and an abstention path when evidence is ambiguous [National Institute of Standards and Technology, 2024, Vassilev et al., 2025].

- **External-validity boundary:** A simulation demonstrates that a mechanism can arise under specified conditions; it does not establish that the mechanism has occurred in live markets or that its effect size will transport unchanged. Experimental reports should therefore disclose model versions, prompts, tools, information sets, decoding parameters, replication counts, invalid outputs, market rules, and sensitivity analyses. Governance committees should classify evidence as documented operational evidence, controlled experimental evidence, hypothetical stress testing, or expert judgment before using it to set policy.

## 11.3.3 Translating research into practice

The gap between controlled research and production can be narrowed through staged deployment, bounded adaptation, and collaborative testing. These mechanisms convert emerging evidence into controls without claiming more certainty than the evidence supports. The following practices provide a practical bridge from research findings to governed deployment:

- **Graduated deployment protocols:** New systems should progress from offline evaluation to historical replay, sandbox testing, paper trading or shadow mode, tightly limited production, and only then broader deployment. Promotion between stages should depend on pre-specified evidence rather than calendar time. Each stage should test not only task performance but also calibration, prompt sensitivity, security, operational resilience, limit compliance, and behavior under stress [Board of Governors of the Federal Reserve System et al., 2026, National Institute of Standards and Technology, 2023].

- **Controlled change and continuous learning:** Any mechanism that changes prompts, retrieval data, tools, memory, parameters, or decision policy can alter system behavior. Changes should therefore be versioned, tested, approved, and reversible. Online or continual learning should be bounded by explicit risk limits, with revalidation triggers for material drift, new capabilities, or changes in intended use. A system should never be permitted to rewrite its own risk appetite, permissions, or control logic without an independently authorized process.

- **Cross-institution research collaboration:** Institutions compete on implementation but share an interest in stable markets and credible governance. Pre-competitive collaboration can support common incident taxonomies, stress scenarios, semantic challenge sets, third-party dependency mapping, and privacy-preserving measurement of aggregate behavior. The FSB's recent work emphasizes information sharing, organization-wide governance, lifecycle controls, and monitoring of market correlations and third-party concentration [Financial Stability Board, 2024, 2026].

## 11.4 Implementation architecture: From theory to practice

The three pillars define what must be governed; the implementation architecture defines where controls operate. The architecture has four layers, illustrated in Figure 11.1. Evidence and assurance flow upward from infrastructure and behavior to risk and accountability, while policy, limits, permissions, and escalation rules flow downward. The layers are interdependent rather than sequential maturity levels.

A practical architecture should also separate generative reasoning from irreversible execution. In many use cases, the LLM is best placed in research, interpretation, monitoring, or recommendation functions, while deterministic services validate calculations and enforce permissions, position limits, order types, and customer-impact rules. An LLM should enter a hard real-time execution path only after the complete path has met a measured, use-case-specific latency and reliability budget.

## Final-phase update: Authority as a revocable budget

A minimal governance refinement is to treat authority as a ladder rather than a binary switch. A system may be an analytical assistant, a decision-support tool, a supervised proposer, a bounded allocator, a bounded executor, or a self-adapting institution; promotion between these states should depend on evidence, and demotion should be automatic when monitored metrics leave their approved bands. For capital-bearing workflows, a compact authority budget can be written as

```
\frac{B_t^{\text{auth}}}{B_{\max}^{\text{auth}}} = K_t \quad \prod_{j \in \{\text{cal,reg,data,ops,gov}\}} \quad \rho_{j,t}, \quad K_t \in \{0, 1\}, \quad 0 \le \rho_{j,t} \le 1.
```

The factors represent calibration, regime familiarity, data integrity, operational health, and governance status. The hard gate \( K_t \) is a kill switch: when it is zero, authority collapses to abstention or human approval. The formula is not a universal regulatory requirement; it is an implementation pattern that makes bounded autonomy measurable and revocable [Noguer i Alonso and Pacheco Azmar, 2026].

| Layer | Description |
|-------|-------------|
| 4 Accountability, audit, and governance | named owner, escalation authority, kill switch |
| 3 Risk decisioning, limits, and escalation | what the system may do without a human |
| 2 Behavioral monitoring and control | drift, faithfulness, override rate |
| 1 Foundational infrastructure | data lineage, versioning, permissions, immutable log |

A layer can only be as good as the one below it: behavioural monitoring over data you cannot version is theatre, and an escalation rule that fires into a log nobody owns is a rule that does not exist.

Evidence flows up and policy flows down, and the ordering is not decorative — it is a dependency. Institutions consistently build layer 3 first, because limits and escalation rules are the part that reassures a committee, and then discover at examination that they cannot evidence a single one of them because layer 1 was never built. Start at the bottom. It is the least impressive slide and the only one that holds.

### 11.4.1 Layer 1: Foundational infrastructure

The foundational layer provides the technical conditions under which higher-level governance can be credible. It covers computing and model serving, data and retrieval architecture, identity and access management, secure development, observability, versioning, business continuity, and recovery.

The following infrastructure components form the baseline for reliable and governable financial AI systems:

- **Compute, serving, and resilience:** Capacity, latency, availability, and recovery targets should be derived from the use case rather than declared as universal numbers. A customer-service assistant, an overnight surveillance process, and a market-making component have different service-level requirements. Critical paths require redundancy, health checks, deterministic fallback behavior, and the ability to disable model-dependent functions without disabling essential financial controls.

- **Data architecture and governance:** Every consequential output should be traceable to its model version, system and user instructions, retrieved sources, tool calls, relevant data, post-processing, and approval state. Data-quality checks should cover freshness, completeness, leakage, conflicting records, licensing, and representativeness. Retrieval-augmented systems need source-level controls because a well-behaved model can still generate an incorrect decision from stale, malicious, or irrelevant retrieved content. Protecting sensitive data can require differential privacy, federated learning, secure aggregation, or secure multi-party computation. These techniques are powerful but not free. Differential privacy introduces a privacy–utility trade-off; federated learning adds communication rounds and non-identically distributed data challenges; and cryptographic aggregation or multi-party computation adds communication, memory, and compute overhead [Dwork and Roth, 2014, Kairouz et al., 2021, Bonawitz et al., 2017, Evans et al., 2018]. They are often well suited to asynchronous training, cross-institution analytics, and batch model development. Their use in an ultra-low-latency trading path should be justified by measured end-to-end performance rather than by architectural preference.

- **Model development and deployment pipeline:** The deployment unit should include code, model, prompt templates, retrieval configuration, tools, permissions, datasets, evaluation suites, and dependency versions. Quality gates should test functional correctness, security, data leakage, behavioral stability, performance, and rollback. Production promotion should be blocked when lineage is incomplete or when a material test fails, regardless of headline accuracy.
