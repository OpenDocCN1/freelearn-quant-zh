## 13.6 Integration and future directions

The convergence of advanced reasoning, multimodal understanding, and specialized time series architectures creates the _possibility_ of more comprehensive financial intelligence systems, rather than guaranteeing it. Whether this potential is realized depends on several concrete conditions: the components must solve genuinely complementary parts of the task; their data must be point-in-time, provenance-tracked, and temporally aligned; the integrated workflow must demonstrate incremental value over strong single-model baselines; latency and compute costs must fit the decision horizon; confidence estimates and failure modes must be observable; and human reviewers must retain clear escalation and override paths. Integration is therefore primarily an orchestration problem, not an argument for one monolithic model.

In principle, a well-designed platform could reason about complex strategic decisions while consuming diverse information sources and maintaining awareness of evolving market dynamics. For example, an M&A workflow could combine financial statements, news and regulatory filings, executive communications, and market pricing signals; a reasoning layer would formulate synergy and financing scenarios, while a time series layer would update market-implied risk and a governance layer would preserve sources, assumptions, and human approvals. The system is useful only if these components remain synchronized and the final recommendation is traceable to evidence.

Realizing this vision in practice, however, requires solving several hard problems. Computational demands remain substantial, particularly when combining test-time reasoning (e.g., Tree of Thoughts), large multimodal encoders, and high-capacity time series models in a single workflow. While architecture and inference optimizations continue to reduce cost and latency, most institutions will need to adopt a layered approach: reserving the most expensive capabilities for a small set of high-impact use cases, and relying on lighter models elsewhere.

Data quality and coverage also vary dramatically across markets, asset classes, and institutions. Deep time series models and multimodal systems are only as reliable as the data they see. Emerging markets, private assets, and niche products often exhibit sparse, noisy, or shifting data regimes, limiting the benefits of sophisticated architectures. For many applications, careful feature engineering and robust baselines remain competitive with, or superior to, the most advanced models.

Regulatory and supervisory expectations are no longer a blank slate, but they remain layered, use-case dependent, and jurisdiction specific. The European Union’s AI Act applies a risk-based structure whose obligations depend on the system’s classification and role in the value chain [European Parliament and Council of the European Union, 2024].

In the United States, SR 26-2 superseded SR 11-7 and SR 21-8, but its relevance to this chapter is deliberately limited. The guidance is non-binding, is expected to be most relevant to banking organizations with more than $30 billion in total assets, narrows the model definition by excluding simple-arithmetic tools and deterministic rule-based software, and in footnote 3 expressly excludes generative-AI and agentic-AI models. It continues to apply to traditional statistical and quantitative models and to non-generative, non-agentic AI models that meet the revised definition [Board of Governors of the Federal Reserve System et al., 2026]. Advanced reasoning LLMs, multimodal generators, and agentic systems should therefore be governed through the institution’s own proportionate controls and the laws and supervisory frameworks that actually apply, not described as SR 26-2-compliant. Where an out-of-scope LLM feeds an in-scope model, the interface remains a material validation dependency that should be inventoried and tested.

The NIST AI Risk Management Framework and its Generative AI Profile offer voluntary lifecycle practices organized around governing, mapping, measuring, and managing risk [National Institute of Standards and Technology, 2023, 2024]. For capital markets, IOSCO’s 2026 supervisory toolkit adds practical questions concerning use-case design, model and data risk, testing, monitoring, and third-party dependencies [International Organization of Securities Commissions, 2026].

Financial institutions should therefore map each application to the laws and supervisory guidance that apply to its jurisdiction, function, materiality, and affected clients. Advanced reasoning, multimodality, and time-series LLMs should remain governed extensions to the modeling stack, with documented intended use, formal scope analysis, access controls, performance thresholds, incident procedures, human oversight, dependency testing, and a tested fallback process.

Moving from correlation toward causation is another critical frontier. Current systems excel at detecting statistical regularities but struggle to distinguish between causal relationships and spurious patterns. Consider a model that learns that policy-rate cuts are followed by weak equity returns. A naive interpretation is that cutting rates causes the market to fall; in many episodes, however, deteriorating growth and financial stress cause both the rate cut and the equity decline. The predictive association can therefore be useful while the intervention claim is wrong. A similar problem arises when social-media attention predicts returns because both are driven by an earnings surprise: purchasing additional mentions would not reproduce the return effect.

Causal analysis asks a different question from forecasting: how would the outcome change under a specified intervention, holding the relevant data-generating structure fixed? Combining structural assumptions, natural experiments, instruments, event timing, and explicit causal graphs with multimodal and temporal models could improve policy analysis and stress testing of “what if” scenarios [Pearl, 2009, Peters et al., 2017]. Such claims require stronger identification assumptions than ordinary prediction, so progress is likely to be gradual, domain-specific, and accompanied by sensitivity analysis rather than universal.

Finally, collaborative modeling techniques such as federated learning offer a potential path to industry-wide models without centralizing sensitive data. In a federated setup, institutions train models locally and share only parameter updates, preserving data privacy while benefiting from collective learning. In practice, this approach faces non-trivial challenges—including heterogeneous data distributions, communication constraints, and complex governance questions about who controls the resulting models and how benefits are shared. For the foreseeable future, federated and collaborative approaches should be treated as promising research and pilot areas, not as immediate replacements for institution-specific models.

## 13.7 Open questions and recommended resources

### 13.7.1 Open research questions

The frontier described in this chapter remains defined as much by unresolved evaluation and governance problems as by architectural progress. Particularly important questions include:

- 1. **Verifiable financial reasoning:** How can a system expose the data, calculations, assumptions, and decision rules that support an answer without treating an unfaithful generated rationale as proof that the answer is correct?
- 2. **Search under real-time budgets:** How should ToT, MCTS, and self-refinement allocate compute when market state, prices, and available actions change during the search itself?
- 3. **Calibration under regime change:** Can reasoning confidence, multimodal agreement, and predictive uncertainty remain calibrated when liquidity, policy, or market microstructure shifts outside the training distribution?
- 4. **Temporal and cross-modal alignment:** How should systems distinguish genuinely contemporaneous evidence from stale, revised, or asynchronously observed data, especially when alternative-data vendors use different timestamp conventions?
