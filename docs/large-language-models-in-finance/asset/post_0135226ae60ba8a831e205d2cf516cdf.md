RNNs process tokens sequentially, creating a bottleneck for long financial documents. Transformers attend to all positions in parallel — but the claim needs a boundary drawn around it, because it is routinely overstated. Parallel applies to training and to prompt processing: a 200-page filing can be ingested in one forward pass. Generation is a different matter. Autoregressive decoding remains strictly sequential, one token at a time, each conditioned on all those before it. This is why a long prompt and a long answer cost differently: prefill is parallel and decode is not. It is not, however, a licence to treat prompts as free. Prefill is O(n^2) in attention and at very long contexts it can dominate both latency and cost outright; what parallelism buys is that the work can be done at once, not that there is less of it. Within that boundary, parallel attention enables efficient processing of full earnings transcripts or regulatory filings.

The self-attention mechanism is computed as:

```
Attention(Q, K, V) = softmax( (QK^T / sqrt(d_k)) V ) (1.2)
```

where Q, K, and V denote the queries, keys, and values, respectively, and dk is the dimension of the keys. This mechanism computes a weighted sum over all positions in the sequence, allowing the model to capture dependencies irrespective of distance. Conceptually, this formula allows the model to determine which words in a financial document (like “debt” and “covenant”) are most relevant to one another, regardless of how far apart they appear in the text.

The advantages of Transformers for financial text processing include:

- 1. Parallel processing: Training and prompt prefill can process positions in parallel; autoregressive decoding remains sequential

- 2. Long-range dependencies: Attention creates direct interaction paths between distant positions, subject to masking and context-window limits

- 3. **Scalability**: Performance can improve with appropriately scaled data and compute, although gains depend on task fit, data quality, and diminishing returns

- 4. **Transfer learning**: Pre-trained models can be fine-tuned for specific financial tasks

Together, these characteristics make Transformers the foundation of modern LLMs used in financial applications. Now that we've outlined the move from statistical models to modern neural architectures, we can look at how these architectures evolve into today's pre-trained models, including GPT, BERT, and T5.

## 1.5 Pre-trained language models (e.g., GPT, BERT, T5)

Pre-trained models have become a cornerstone of modern NLP. Models such as GPT, BERT, and T5 illustrate the power of unsupervised or self-supervised pre-training followed by fine-tuning for specific tasks.

This section will introduce the main model families that underpin most financial AI systems today, highlighting the differences in how they learn and generalize. Understanding these pre-training paradigms is essential for financial practitioners because many downstream capabilities, such as summarizing filings or interpreting earnings calls, ultimately depend on the strengths and limitations of these foundational model families.

### 1.5.1 GPT series

The GPT family progressed from GPT-2 (1.5B parameters) to GPT-3 (175B) and GPT-4, each iteration improving text coherence and generation quality. GPT models employ autoregressive training, predicting next tokens to learn language patterns. To understand how later architectures diverged, it helps to first ground ourselves in GPT's core generative philosophy. Key characteristics of GPT models are:

- **Unidirectional context**: Only sees previous tokens, not future ones
- **Autoregressive generation**: Generates text token by token
- **Few-shot learning**: Can adapt to new tasks with just a few examples
- **Scale advantages**: Larger models show emergent capabilities

GPT employs a causal language modeling objective where the model predicts the next token given the previous tokens. Its training objective is given by:

```
$\mathcal{L}_{\text{GPT}} = - \sum_{t=1}^T \log P(w_t|w_1, \dots, w_{t-1})$ (1.3)
```

This unidirectional approach supports generative tasks such as text completion and creative writing [Brown et al., 2020], [OpenAI, 2022]. Successive generations continue to build on this principle.

### 1.5.2 BERT and its derivatives

BERT introduced masked language modeling, training on dual directions to capture bidirectional context. Successors such as RoBERTa and DeBERTa refined training regimes and data scale, enhancing performance on comprehension tasks.

BERT's innovations include:

- **Bidirectional training**: Sees both left and right context simultaneously
- **Masked language modeling**: Learns by predicting randomly masked tokens
- **Segment embeddings**: Can process pairs of sentences/documents
- **Pre-training objectives**: MLM and Next Sentence Prediction (later removed in RoBERTa)

BERT uses a masked language modeling (MLM) objective in which a percentage of tokens is masked, and the model predicts these tokens based on their context:

```
$\mathcal{L}_{\text{BERT}} = - \sum_{i \in \mathcal{M}} \log P(w_i|w_1, \dots, w_{i-1}, [\text{MASK}], w_{i+1}, \dots, w_T)$ (1.4)
```

where $\mathcal{M}$ represents the set of masked positions [Devlin et al., 2019]. While foundational, newer architectures often incorporate or evolve beyond pure MLM.

### 1.5.3 T5 and the text-to-text paradigm

T5 reframes diverse NLP tasks—translation, summarization, question answering—as text-to-text transformations, simplifying fine-tuning and promoting transfer across tasks.

T5's unified approach offers several advantages:

- **Task unification**: All tasks use the same model architecture and training procedure
- **Simplified deployment**: One model handles multiple tasks
- **Transfer learning**: Knowledge transfers between related tasks
- **Flexible prompting**: Task behavior controlled through input prefixes

T5 treats every NLP problem as a text-to-text problem, enabling a unified approach across diverse tasks. Its objective is formalized as:

```
$$\mathcal{L}_{T5}(\theta) = -\sum_{t=1}^T \log P_\theta(y_t \mid y_{<t}, x_1, \dots, x_{T'}),$$ (1.5)
```

where \(y_{<t} = (y_1, \dots, y_{t-1})\), \(x_1, \dots, x_{T'}\) is the input text, and \(y_1, \dots, y_T\) is the output text [Raffel et al., 2020].

By extending the strengths of GPT-style generation and incorporating BERT-like understanding, T5 demonstrates how pre-training on massive corpora produces highly adaptable, general-purpose models.

The distinctions among GPT, BERT, and T5 arise not only from their training objectives, but also from the building blocks they share. We, therefore, shift our focus from model families to the shared architectural backbone that underpins nearly all modern LLMs.

> **Case study — Earnings call assistant:** Choosing the right pre-trained model matters. A GPT-style model is best for *generating* the summary output; a BERT-style model works better for *classifying* management sentiment (positive/negative/neutral) because it reads the full transcript bidirectionally before deciding. A T5-style model can handle both tasks with a single architecture before each as text-to-text transformation: “summarize: [_transcript_]” or “classify sentiment: [_excerpt_]”.

## 1.6 Architecture and components

In this section we briefly outline the key components of modern Transformer-based architectures—the backbone of contemporary LLMs. The goal is not to provide a complete technical treatment (that is the focus of Chapter 2), but to build enough intuition that later discussions of fine-tuning, retrieval-augmented generation, and multi-agent systems are easier to follow.

Understanding the architectural components of LLMs is crucial for practitioners working with financial data because these components directly influence how models process, interpret, and generate text. The multi-head attention mechanism, for instance, determines how well a model can connect distant pieces of information in a long financial document, while the feedforward networks control the model’s ability to transform and combine features. By grasping these fundamentals, you’ll be better equipped to select appropriate models, optimize their performance for financial tasks, and troubleshoot issues that may arise during implementation.

LLMs like Transformers are composed of several interlinked components, each essential for capturing complex linguistic structures. Let’s examine them.
