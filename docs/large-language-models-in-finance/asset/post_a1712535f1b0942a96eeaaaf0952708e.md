
## 5.4.3 Human-in-the-loop design

Agentic systems in finance must be _human-in-the-loop_ by design. Rather than attempting to fully automate decisions, systems should support humans by:

- providing summaries, recommendations, and explanations;
- highlighting areas of uncertainty or risk;
- enabling users to inspect intermediate steps (e.g., retrieved documents, tool calls);
- capturing user feedback and corrections.

This design philosophy increases trust, reduces the risk of catastrophic errors, and aligns with regulatory expectations. For the research workstation, human-in-the-loop design can be implemented as three gates: analyst review of evidence quality, portfolio-manager review of the investment thesis, and risk or compliance review of flagged constraints. The agentic system accelerates the preparation of the memo, but the accountable human decision remains visible.

## 5.4.4 Observable and testable workflows

Agentic systems should be designed so that their behavior can be observed, tested, and reproduced. This requires more than logging the final answer. Teams should capture the request, model and prompt versions, retrieved document identifiers, tool parameters, intermediate outputs, validation errors, human overrides, and final approval decisions.

For the research workstation, observability turns a draft memo into a traceable workflow. A reviewer should be able to reconstruct how the orchestrator decomposed the request, which documents the Research Analyst agent used, which data snapshot the Data Analyst agent consulted, which policy checks the Risk and Compliance agent applied, and why a human review gate was or was not triggered.

## 5.4.5 Versioning and change management

Prompts, tool schemas, retrieval indexes, policy rules, and model versions should be versioned together. A change to any one of these components can alter system behavior. For example, updating the retrieval index may change which evidence appears in a memo, while modifying a policy tool may change whether a risk flag is triggered. Financial institutions should therefore treat agent workflows as governed software systems rather than informal prompt collections.

A useful release process is to maintain a small benchmark set of representative requests, including normal cases, adversarial inputs, missing-data scenarios, and known compliance edge cases. Before deploying a new version of the workstation, the team can rerun these benchmarks and compare outputs, tool traces, risk flags, and escalation behavior.

## 5.4.6 Layered guardrails and policy-as-code

Guardrails are often discussed as if they were a single filter placed before or after the LLM. In financial workflows, guardrails are more effective when they are layered. The system should combine prompt-level constraints, tool-level permissions, schema validation, policy checks, human review, and post-deployment monitoring. Each layer covers a different class of failure, and no single layer should be treated as sufficient.

For the research workstation, policy-as-code means that restricted-list checks, concentration limits, disclosure rules, and escalation thresholds are encoded in tools or rules that the LLM cannot silently rewrite. The LLM may explain a policy result or help a reviewer understand it, but the underlying threshold should come from a governed policy store. This distinction matters because it prevents a fluent model from becoming the source of truth for rules that should be controlled by the institution.

## 5.4.7 Evaluation and monitoring as first-class design objects

Agentic systems should be designed together with their evaluation harness. Waiting until the end of a project to define evaluation usually leads to vague success criteria such as “the answer looks good.” A stronger approach is to define task-level and workflow-level measures from the beginning.

For the workstation, task-level measures include citation precision, citation coverage, metric accuracy, extraction accuracy, and policy-flag recall. Workflow-level measures include time saved per memo, review-cycle reduction, escalation precision, latency, failure rate, and percentage of outputs requiring manual correction. These measures should be segmented by request type, asset class, document source, and user group. A system may perform well for large-cap equities with abundant public filings but poorly for private credit, small-cap issuers, or emerging-market securities with sparse data.

Monitoring should also capture qualitative feedback. Reviewers should be able to mark a claim as unsupported, a citation as irrelevant, a metric as stale, or an escalation as unnecessary. Those markings become data for future evaluation and prompt refinement. In this sense, human review is not only a safety mechanism; it is also a source of continuous measurement.

## 5.4.8 Security, privacy, and permissioning

Financial agent systems often operate across data boundaries. A research agent may have access to public filings and internal notes, while a portfolio agent may access holdings, risk exposures, and trade history. A compliance agent may access restricted lists or sensitive policy records. The architecture should enforce these boundaries directly rather than relying on the model to remember them.

Useful practices include role-based access control for tools, row-level and document-level permissions, data minimization in prompts, redaction of sensitive fields, and separate logging for sensitive tool calls. In the workstation, the Research Analyst agent may not need full portfolio holdings; it may only need the target ticker and analysis horizon. The Risk and Compliance agent may need portfolio exposure, but not the entire history of an analyst's private notes. Passing only the required context reduces the blast radius of model errors and prompt-injection attacks.

## 5.4.9 Cost, latency, and user experience

Agentic systems can become expensive and slow because they call models and tools many times. A workflow that feels impressive in a demonstration may be frustrating in daily use if it takes too long to produce a draft or if it consumes excessive compute. Cost and latency should therefore be architectural constraints, not afterthoughts.

Common techniques include caching repeated retrieval results, using smaller models for classification and routing, reserving larger models for synthesis or ambiguous cases, running independent tool calls in parallel, and streaming intermediate progress to the user. For the research workstation, the orchestrator can fetch research documents and market data in parallel, use deterministic code for metrics, and call the largest model only when drafting or reconciling the memo. The user interface should show progress such as “retrieving evidence,” “computing metrics,” and “running policy checks,” rather than presenting the system as a silent black box.

## 5.4.10 Documentation and model-risk artifacts

In regulated environments, the final system should be documented as a governed workflow. Useful artifacts include an architecture diagram, a list of agents and their permitted tools, prompt versions, schema definitions, evaluation reports, known limitations, escalation policies, incident-response procedures, and examples of approved and rejected outputs. These artifacts help developers, users, risk managers, and auditors understand what the system does and where human responsibility remains.

For the research workstation, documentation should make clear that the system drafts and annotates research material; it does not approve trades, guarantee investment outcomes, or replace regulated review processes. Stating these boundaries explicitly is not merely legal caution. It also improves product design by preventing scope creep and making it easier to decide which new features belong inside the agentic workflow.

## 5.4.11 Supervisor-and-specialist pattern

A common pattern in financial agent systems is to separate supervision from specialist work. Specialist agents perform bounded tasks such as retrieving evidence, computing metrics, or checking policy rules. A supervisor or orchestrator agent manages sequencing, conflict resolution, and escalation. This separation improves maintainability because the institution can change one specialist tool or prompt without redesigning the entire workflow.

The pattern descends from the role-play and conversational multi-agent frameworks [Li et al., 2023, Wu et al., 2023a], and Section 5.3 draws it with the one edge that those frameworks leave implicit and that finance cannot.
