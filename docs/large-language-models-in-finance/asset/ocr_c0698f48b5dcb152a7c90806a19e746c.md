Read the table again with that in mind. The random split did not inflate everyone equally. It inflated *the best model*, and it inflated it by a factor of two, while leaving the weak models almost exactly where they were. The reason is not mysterious: BERT and the LSTM were not good enough to exploit the temporal adjacency the split handed them, so they had nothing to lose when it was taken away. FinEAS was. The advantage a leak confers is not a constant added to every competitor's score — it accrues to whichever model has the capacity to notice it, which is to say, it accrues disproportionately to the model you are trying to sell.

This is the general form of the danger and it is why leakage is not a hygiene issue to be delegated to a data engineer. A leaky protocol does not merely make your numbers too good. It makes your *model comparison* wrong, in the specific direction of favouring the most powerful architecture in the bake-off — and powerful architectures are exactly what an LLM system is made of. Had FinEAS been compared against its baselines only out-of-time, it would still have won, and the paper's architectural claim would still have stood. It would simply have won by less, and honestly. The correct protocol was available, it was cheap, and we did not use it.

I am not asking the reader to discount that paper. I am asking the reader to notice how easy this was to do, in a paper written by people who knew better, and to build the protocol of Section 13.4 into the pipeline rather than trusting that they will remember.

# 13.4.4 Label leakage and target engineering

Label leakage occurs when the target definition or labeling process embeds future information in the input. For example, a binary label \( y_t = \mathbf{1}[r_{t+1:t+20} > 0] \) is valid only if no feature summarizes the next 20 days. But an LLM prompt might accidentally include a news summary written after the horizon, a final analyst interpretation, or a chart whose endpoint reveals the target. In supervised fine-tuning, label leakage can also occur through instruction text: “classify whether this event led to a positive reaction” is not equivalent to “forecast whether this event will lead to a positive reaction.”

The safest practice is to separate event description from outcome description. A training example may contain the event as known at time \( t \), the market state as known at time \( t \), and the label computed after the horizon. It may not contain any language produced after the horizon that explains the outcome. This rule is particularly important when using LLM-generated synthetic labels or rationales. A synthetic rationale generated with knowledge of the label can teach the model hindsight reasoning rather than forecasting.

# 13.4.5 Feature leakage through revised filings, restatements, and alternative data

Financial databases are full of revisions. Accounting statements are restated, fundamentals are standardized by vendors after publication, macro series are revised, index memberships are reconstructed, and alternative-data vendors backfill improved estimates. A model trained on the final version of a database may appear to predict the future because the database itself has been rewritten by the future.
