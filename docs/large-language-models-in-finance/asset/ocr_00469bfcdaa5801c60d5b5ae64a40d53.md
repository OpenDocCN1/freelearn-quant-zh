# 5.4.6 Layered guardrails and policy-as-code

Guardrails are often discussed as if they were a single filter placed before or after the LLM. In financial workflows, guardrails are more effective when they are layered. The system should combine prompt-level constraints, tool-level permissions, schema validation, policy checks, human review, and post-deployment monitoring. Each layer covers a different class of failure, and no single layer should be treated as sufficient.

For the research workstation, policy-as-code means that restricted-list checks, concentration limits, disclosure rules, and escalation thresholds are encoded in tools or rules that the LLM cannot silently rewrite. The LLM may explain a policy result or help a reviewer understand it, but the underlying threshold should come from a governed policy store. This distinction matters because it prevents a fluent model from becoming the source of truth for rules that should be controlled by the institution.

# 5.4.7 Evaluation and monitoring as first-class design objects

Agentic systems should be designed together with their evaluation harness. Waiting until the end of a project to define evaluation usually leads to vague success criteria such as “the answer looks good.” A stronger approach is to define task-level and workflow-level measures from the beginning.

For the workstation, task-level measures include citation precision, citation coverage, metric accuracy, extraction accuracy, and policy-flag recall. Workflow-level measures include time saved per memo, review-cycle reduction, escalation precision, latency, failure rate, and percentage of outputs requiring manual correction. These measures should be segmented by request type, asset class, document source, and user group. A system may perform well for large-cap equities with abundant public filings but poorly for private credit, small-cap issuers, or emerging-market securities with sparse data.

Monitoring should also capture qualitative feedback. Reviewers should be able to mark a claim as unsupported, a citation as irrelevant, a metric as stale, or an escalation as unnecessary. Those markings become data for future evaluation and prompt refinement. In this sense, human review is not only a safety mechanism; it is also a source of continuous measurement.

# 5.4.8 Security, privacy, and permissioning

Financial agent systems often operate across data boundaries. A research agent may have access to public filings and internal notes, while a portfolio agent may access holdings, risk exposures, and trade history. A compliance agent may access restricted lists or sensitive policy records. The architecture should enforce these boundaries directly rather than relying on the model to remember them.

Useful practices include role-based access control for tools, row-level and document-level permissions, data minimization in prompts, redaction of sensitive fields, and separate logging for sensitive tool calls.
